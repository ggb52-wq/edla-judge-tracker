II. LEGAL STANDARD
Diversity jurisdiction depends upon the simultaneous existence  of complete
diversity and an amount in controversy exceeding $75,000.  28 U.S.C. § 1332(a).  A
defendant seeking to remove a case to federal court must file a notice of removal
“within 30 days after receipt by the defendant, through service or otherwise, of a
copy of the initial pleading . . . or within 30 days after the service of summons upon
the defendant….”  28 U.S.C. § 1446(b) (1).  If a case is not initially removable, a
notice of removal may be filed within 30 days after receipt of “an amended pleading,
motion, order or other paper from which it may first be ascertained that the case is
one which is or has become removable.”  Id. § 1446(b)(3).  A case asserting diversity
jurisdiction cannot be removed  more than one year after commencement of the
action, unless plaintiff acted in bad faith to prevent removal.   Id. § 1446(c)(1); see
Hoyt v. Lane Construction Corp., 927 F.3d 287, 293 (5th Cir. 2019).  The removing
party has the burden of showing federal jurisdiction exists.  Mumfrey v. CVS
Pharmacy, Inc., 719 F.3d 392, 397 (5th Cir. 2013) (citing Manguno v. Prudential

III. ANALYSIS
Here, the requirements of complete diversity and the amount in controversy
are conceded.  The only dispute is when the delay for removal was triggered.  That
dispute centers around the conte nt of the “initial pleading”, or , alternatively, what
constitutes an “other paper”.
Thorla first argues the policy limits demand sent to Travelers on September
18, 2024 triggered the delay.  The inference is the case was removable when it was
initially filed in Louisiana state court.  That argument is without merit.
Louisiana law prevented Thorla from pleading a specific amount of monetary
damages in his petition.  See Louisiana Code of Civil Procedure art. 893(A)(1) (“No
specific monetary amount of damages shall be included in the allegations or prayer
for relief of any original, amended, or incidental demand.”).  Nervertheless, the Fifth
Circuit has held that a petition must contain “ a specific allegation that damages
exceed the federal jurisdictional amount. ”  Mumfrey, 719 F.3d at 399 (quoting
Chapman v. Powermatic, Inc. , 969 F.2d  160, 162 (5 th Cir. 1992)).  The court
reasoned that “[s]uch a statement would provide notice to defendants that the
removal clock had been triggered, but would not run afoul of state laws that prohibit

pleading unliquidated damage amounts.”).  Id.  No such allegation is in Thorla’s state
court petition.  Thus, under 28 U.S.C. § 1446(b) (1), the case was not initially
removable.
Thorla next argues the policy limits demand letter constitute s “other paper”
under 28 U.S.C. § 1446(b)(3) , thus triggering removal.  That argument is likewise
meritless.  The letter, which include d medical information regarding Thorla’s
injuries and treatments, was provided to defendants before suit was filed.  Pre-suit
papers do not constitute “other paper” under the statute.  See Robertson v. Safepoint
Insurance Co., No. 24-cv-1172, 2025 WL 610642, at *3 (E.D. La. Feb. 26, 2025)
(“[A] pre-suit demand letter cannot count as ‘other paper ’ because [defendant]
received it before receiving [plaintiff’s] original petition.) (citing Chapman, 969 F.2d
at 164).
Thorla then argues the June 17, 2025 discovery requests and response
constitute “other paper” under § 1446(b)(3).  The court rejects th at argument.  To
start the delay for filing notice of removal, the information supporting removal in
the other paper “must be unequivocally clear and certain….”  Bosky v. Kroger Texas,
LP , 288 F.3d 208, 211 (5th Cir. 2002) (emphasis added).   “Courts following Bosky
have found that other papers describing injuries and other damages that seem likely
to exceed the amount in controversy requirement, but which do not show
unequivocally that the requirement is met, are insufficient to trigger the removal

clock.”  Hampton v. Family Dollar, Inc., No. 25-cv-2355, 2026 WL 380619, at *4
(E.D. La. Feb. 11, 2026) (citing Cole ex rel. Ellis v. Knowledge Learning Corp., 416
F. App’x 437, 440 (5th Cir. 2011) ) (other citation and internal quotation marks
omitted).  “A discovery response may constitute an ‘other paper’ under the federal
removal statute, notifying defendant of an action ’s removability and triggering the
30–day removability period  … however, the discovery response must be
‘unequivocally clear and certain ’, so that defendant may ascertain the action ’s
removability.”  Cole, 416 F. App ’x at 40 (citing Bosky, 288 F.3d at 211) (other
citations omitted).
The June 17, 2025 discovery responses did not provide  unequivocal clarity
and certain ty that plaintiff’s damages exceed $75,000.  The responses to
Interrogatories 2 through 4 address Thorla’s injuries, his medical providers and his
treatment status, but do not provide the certainty necessary to establish damages
exceeding $75,000.  In response to Interrogatory 11, Thorla states he “reasonably
anticipates the totality of his damages may exceed $75,000.00.  Plaintiff has received
over $23,900.00 in medical charges to date.”  The phrases “reasonably anticipates”
and “may exceed” do not convey certainty, and fail the Bosky test.  $23,900 in
medical expenses does not make it unequivocally clear that damages will exceed
$75,000.  The 30-day removal delay was not triggered on June 17, 2025.

On January 16, 2026, plaintiff counsel informed defense counsel Thorla could
not stipulate that damages are below $75,000, and that he had seen a neurosurgeon
and was a candidate for surgery.  A refusal to stipulate to damages below $75,000,
coupled with other relevant factors, can satisfy the “unequivocally clear and certain”
standard to meet  the amount in controversy requirement.  See Carter v. Strategic
Restaurant Acquisition Co. LLC , No. 16-cv-2673, 2016 WL 2825848, at *5 n. 47
(E.D. La. May 13, 2026) (“A refusal to stipulate to the amount of damages is a factor
in determining the amount in controversy. ”) (citation omitted).  While discovery
responses suggested the amount in controversy could exceed $75,000 , not until
defendants received the January 16, 2026 email from plaintiff counsel did it become
certain the amount in controversy exceeded $75,000.  Removal on January 30, 2026
was timely under 28 U.S.C. § 1446 (b)(3).  The motion is denied.
IV.  CONCLUSION
 Plaintiff’s Motion to Remand (R. Doc. 8) is DENIED.
