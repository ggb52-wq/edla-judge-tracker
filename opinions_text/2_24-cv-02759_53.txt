II. Analysis

Pursuant to 42 U.S.C. § 1988(b), a court may, in its discretion award attorney
fees to the prevailing party in a § 1983 action. Roark & Hardee LP v. City of Austin ,
522 F.3d 533, 556 (5th Cir. 2008). Simply establishing plaintiff’s claims were
unsuccessful, however, is insufficient to support an award of attorney fees to a
prevailing defendant. Rather, under § 1988, the prevailing defendants must show the
plaintiff’s claims were “frivolous, unreasonable or groundless.” DeLeon v. City of
Haltom City, 113 F. App’x 577, 578 (5th Cir. 2004) (quoting Christiansburg Garment
Co. v. E qual Employment Opportunity Commission , 434 U.S. 412, 422 (1978)). The
high standard applied to defendants under § 1988 is designed to ensure that no
chilling effect is imposed upon plaintiffs seeking to vindicate their civil rights who

In considering whether to award attorney fees to a prevailing party, courts
should avoid “ post hoc reasoning by concluding that, because a plaintiff did not
ultimately prevail, his action must have been unreasonable or without foundation.”
Christiansburg Garment Co., 434 U.S. at 421–22. “This kind of hindsight logic could
discourage all but the most airtight claims, for seldom can a prospective plaintiff be
sure of ultimate success.” Id. at 422. The factors important to frivolity determinations
are: “(1) whether the plaintiff established a prima facie case; (2) whether the
defendant offered to settle; and (3) whether the court dismissed the case or held a full
trial.” Doe v. Silsbee Indep. Sch. Dist. , 440 F. App ’x 421, 425 (5th Cir. 2011) .
Ultimately, the decision to award attorney fees rests within the trial court’s sound
discretion. 42 U.S.C. § 1988; Vitale v. Georgia Gulf Corp., 82 F. App ’x 873, 876 (5th
Cir. 2003)  (“[P]revailing defendants may  receive fees under the Christiansburg
standard.”).
Significantly, the Supreme Court has warned that attorney fees should “rarely”
be awarded against pro se prisoners in a civil rights action. Hughes v. Rowe, 449 U.S.
5, 15 (1980). The Court explained, “An unrepresented litigant should not be punished
for his failure to recognize subtle factual or legal deficiencies in his claims.” Id.

Although the Court dismissed some of Shorts’s claims after a Spears hearing,
the Court cannot say that Shorts pursued those claims in bad faith. Nor can the Court
find that Shorts’s principal claims of excessive force, which survived frivolity
screening, were wholly frivolous.
The fact that the Court ultimately granted summary judgment in favor of
defendants does not require a finding that the claim was baseless. Davis v. Par. of St.
Tammany, No. CIV.A. 10-364, 2011 WL 121902, at *3 (E.D. La. Jan. 12, 2011) (stating
that “the fact that [plaintiff’s] claims were dismissed on summary judgment instead
of proceeding to trial on the merits does not necessitate” a finding that the claims
were frivolous and denying motion for fees and costs). “[C]areful consideration given
to the case by the district court is some indication that the suit was not frivolous.”
Jones v. Texas Tech Univ. , 656 F.2d 1137, 1146 (5th Cir. 1981) . Here, the Court
carefully considered the merits of the arguments in  the light of the evidence in
deciding to grant summary judgment in favor of the defense, further supporting the
conclusion that this case was not frivolous.
Given Shorts’s pro se  prisoner status and the need to protect the ability of
plaintiffs to initiate litigation to pursue potentially valid constitutional claims
without the unnecessary threat of incurring onerous legal fees should those claims
fail, the Court exercises its discretion to not award attorney fees in this matter.
CONCLUSION
IT IS ORDERED  that defendants’ Motion for Attorney Fees, R. Doc. 51, is
DENIED.
