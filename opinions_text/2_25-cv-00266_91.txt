ORDER AND REASONS
Before the Court is Defendants’ Motion for Reconsideration 1 of this Court’s
September 16, 2026, order and reasons 2 granting Plaintiff’s first motion for partial
summary judgment.3 Plaintiff Malaco, Inc. opposes this motion.4 Defendants filed a
reply in support.5 For the following reasons, the Court denies the motion.
I. Background
This case involves a dispute over the legal rights to two popular New Orleans
nightclub songs recorded in the early 1990s: “Where They At” and “Bitches (Reply).”6
On September 16, 2026, this Court issued an order and reasons granting Malaco’s first
motion for partial summary judgment on declaratory judgment counts one through
four.7 The Court found, primarily, that Defendants hold no copyright ownership in the
publishing or sound recording rights for either song, as their claims to such rights are
time-barred.8

On September 21, 2026, Defendants filed the instant motion for reconsideration,
arguing that the Court committed manifest errors of law and fact regarding the accrual
of the statute of limitations for their copyright ownership claims arising from their
claimed authorship of the two songs .9 Defendants presented new legal theories and
new evidentia ry support , claiming a long -running course of dealing between
Defendants and Malaco and Malaco’s predecessor-in-interest and filing exhibits not
previously submitted to the Court .10 Malaco argues first that the Court should not
consider rejected arguments or new arguments that could have been raised previously
by Defendants.11 Second, Malaco argues that Defendants’ arguments fail on the merits
because any conduct that occurred more than three years after 1992 cannot revive the
claims that had already accrued and become time barred. 12 Lastly, Malaco argues a
reconsideration of counts one through four would significantly prejudice Malaco given
the imminent trial date.13
II. Law and Analysis
This Court’s order adjudicated “fewer than all the claims”  and therefore is
interlocutory.14 A district court “possesses the inherent procedural power to
reconsider, rescind, or modify an interlocutory order for cause seen by it to be
sufficient.”15 The court “must exercise this broad discretion sparingly ,” however, “to

In Calpetco, the Fifth Circuit affirmed a district court’s denial of a motion for
reconsideration when the plaintiff offered thirty-seven new documents and offered no
explanation as to its failure to submit the documents earlier. 18 The Court reasoned
that the district court was not required to reconsider a summary judgment “simply
because Calpetco belatedly came forward with evidence not submitted prior to the
ruling.”19 If the answer was to reconsider the new evidence, “the cycle of
reconsideration would be never-ending.”20
The same reasoning applies here. Defendants now rely on more than forty new
exhibits that they failed to tender previously,  with no explanation for their earlier
omission, and Malaco claims were never produced in discovery. As the Court held in
Calpetco, just because Defendants now come forward with  new evidence of course of
dealing, the Court is not required to reconsider its ruling on partial summary
