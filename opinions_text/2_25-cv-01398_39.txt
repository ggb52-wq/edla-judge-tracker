 The immediate events that gave rise to this litigation began in roughly 2018.
However, the underlying issues involved date back to the time of slavery. Beginning
in the 18th century, Louisiana was home to sugarcane plantations along the
Mississippi River between New Orleans and Baton Rouge. The sugarcane plantation
owners accumulated great w ealth by using the labor of enslaved people, many of
whom died because of this oppressive and high -risk work. In fact, “[s]ugarcane
cultivation was brutal, even by the standards of American slavery.” John Bardes,

LEGAL STANDARD
A. Rule 12(b)(1)
The Court must consider Defendant’s challenge to subject matter jurisdiction
under Rule 12(b)(1) before deciding the other Rule 12 motions to dismiss because
jurisdiction is a threshold determination. See In re Great Lakes Dredge & Dock Co.,
LLC, 624 F.3d 201, 209 (5th Cir. 2010). When deciding a motion to dismiss for lack of
subject matter jurisdiction under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(1), “the
district court is ‘free to weigh the evidence and resolve factual disputes in order to
satisfy itself that it has the power to hear the case.’” Krim v. pcOrder.com, Inc. , 402
F.3d 489, 494 (5th Cir. 2005). The party asserting jurisdiction must carry the burden
of proof to defeat a Rule 12(b)(1) motion to dismiss. Randall D. Wolcott, M.D., P.A. v.
Sebelius, 635 F.3d 757, 762 (5th Cir. 2011) (citation omitted). The standard of review
for a Rule 12(b)(1) motion to dismiss is the same as that for a motion to dismiss
pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6).
B. Rule 12(b)(6)
To survive a Rule 12(b)(6) motion to dismiss, the plaintiff must plead sufficient
facts to “state a claim to relief that is plausible on its face.” Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S.
Case 2:25-cv-01398-CJB-EJD     Document 39     Filed 09/29/26     Page 7 of 25
662, 678 (2009) (quoting Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly, 550 U.S. 544, 570 (2007)). A claim
is facially plausible when the plaintiff pleads facts that allow the court to “draw the
reasonable inference that the defendant is liable for the misconduct alleged.” Id. The
factual allegations in the complaint “must be enough to raise a right to relief above
the speculative level.” Twombly, 550 U.S. at 555. “[D]etailed factual allegations” are
not required, but the pleading must present “more than an unadorned, the -
defendant-unlawfully-harmed-me accusation.” Iqbal, 556 U.S. at 678. The court must
accept all well-pleaded facts as true and must draw all reasonable inferences in favor
of the plaintiff. Lormand v. US Unwired, Inc. , 565 F.3d 228, 232 (5th Cir. 2009).
However, “conclusory allegations or legal conclusions masquerading as factual
conclusions will not suffice to prevent a motion to dismiss.” Beavers v. Metro. Life Ins.
Co., 566 F.3d 436, 439 (5th Cir. 2009) (citation omitted).
DISCUSSION
I. Motion to Dismiss for Lack of Subject Matter Jurisdiction Under 12(b)(1)
Here, Defendant claims that the Court lacks subject matter jurisdiction on two
justiciability grounds. First, Defendant contends that Plaintiffs lack standing
because they have not sufficiently alleged an injury in fact that is concrete,
particularized, and redressable. Next, according to  Defendant, Plaintiffs’ claims are
unripe, “resting on hypothetical future events rather than any existing controversy.”
(Rec. Doc. 9-2, at 2). The Court will address Article III standing first and then proceed
to analyze ripeness.
Case 2:25-cv-01398-CJB-EJD     Document 39     Filed 09/29/26     Page 8 of 25
The standing inquiry identifies “disputes which are appropriately resolved
through the judicial process,” Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife , 504 U.S. 555, 560 (1992)
(citation omitted), and when “a plaintiff lacks Article III standing, then a federal court
lacks jurisdiction to hear the complaint.” Delta Com. Fisheries Ass’n v. Gulf of Mexico
Fishery Mgmt. Council, 364 F.3d 269, 272 (5th Cir. 2004) (citation omitted). To have
standing, a plaintiff must allege (and ultimately prove) (1) an injury in fact, which is
“a harm suffered by the plaintiff that is concrete and actual or imminent, not
conjectural or hypothetical,” (2) causation, which is “a fairly traceable conn ection
between the plaintiff’s injury and the complained -of conduct of the defendant,” and
(3) redressability, which is “a likelihood that the requested relief will redress the
alleged injury.” Servicios Azucareros de Venezuela, C.A. v. John Deere Thibodeaux,
Inc., 702 F.3d 794, 799–800 (5th Cir. 2012) (internal quotation marks omitted).
“A plaintiff must demonstrate standing with the manner and degree of
evidence required at the successive stages of the litigation.” TransUnion LLC v.
Ramirez, 594 U.S. 413, 431 (2021) (quoting Lujan , 504 U.S. at 561) (internal
quotation marks omitted). Thus, “at the pleading stage, general factual allegations of
injury resulting from the defendant’s conduct may suffice, for on a motion to dismiss
[courts] presume that general allegations embrace thos e specific facts that are
necessary to support the claim.” Tex. Cable & Telecomms. Ass’n v. Hudson , 265 F.
App’x 210, 216 (5th Cir. 2008) (quoting Lujan, 504 U.S. at 561) (internal quotation
marks omitted); see also Metro. Washington Airports Auth. v. Citizens for Abatement
of Aircraft Noise, Inc., 501 U.S. 252, 264–65 (1991) (noting that on a motion to dismiss
Case 2:25-cv-01398-CJB-EJD     Document 39     Filed 09/29/26     Page 9 of 25
for want of standing, courts must accept as true all material allegations contained in
the complaint).
Associations, such as Plaintiffs in this case, can assert two theories of standing:
organizational and representational (which is frequently referred to as
“associational”). Organizational standing, on the one  hand, allows an association to
“base standing on an injury to that organization itself —such as incurring a non-
litigation expense or curtailing its activities as a result of the challenged action.” Hat
v. Landry, No. 20-CV-00983, 2021 WL 1823089, at *8 (W.D. La. May 5, 2021) (citing
OCA-Greater Houston v. Texas, 867 F.3d 604, 612 (5th Cir. 2017)). For organizational
standing, an association must satisfy the same standing requirements that apply to
an individual: (1) injury, (2) causation, and (3) redressability. Id.
Representational standing, on the other hand, allows an association to bring
suit on behalf of its members when: “(a) its members would otherwise have standing
to sue in their own right; (b) the interests it seeks to protect are germane to the
organization’s purpose; and (c) neither the claim asserted nor the relief requested
requires the participation of individual members in the lawsuit.” Ass’n of Am.
Physicians & Surgeons, Inc. v. Tex. Med. Bd., 627 F.3d 547, 550 (5th Cir. 2010)
(quoting Hunt v. Wash. State Apple Advert. Comm’n, 432 U.S. 333, 343 (1977)).
In this case, two associations, Inclusive Louisiana and The Descendants
Project, seek declaratory and injunctive relief on claims that Defendant FG
(1) has violated the Thirteenth Amendment by denying and/or hindering access to the
Buena Vista Plantation gravesite, thereby perpetuating the badges and incidents of
Case 2:25-cv-01398-CJB-EJD     Document 39     Filed 09/29/26     Page 10 of 25
slavery, and (2) has run afoul of Louisiana law, custom, and jurisprudence constante
related to dedicated cemeteries. Plaintiffs allege that they have both organizational
and representational standing to bring these claims. The Court will begin by
discussing organizational standing.
 To establish organizational standing, Plaintiffs must first demonstrate an
injury in fact. Relying on the Fifth Circuit’s decision in Texas State LULAC v. Elfant,
Defendant argues that Plaintiffs lack organizational standing because they have not
had to “divert[] significant resources to counteract the defendant’s conduct” such that
the Organizations’ “ability to pursue [their] mission” has been impaired. (Rec. Doc. 9-
2, at 8 (quoting Tex. State LULAC v. Elfant , 52 F.4th 248, 253 (5th Cir. 2022))).
Conversely, Plaintiffs contend that diversion of resources is not the only way for an
organization to demonstrate injury in fact for the purpose of organizational standing.
They cite Texas Tribune v. Caldwell County, Texas to support their claim that “[w]hen
an organization’s mission relies on physical access, denial of access confers standing.”
(Rec. Doc. 20, at 8 (citing Tex. Tribune v. Caldwell Cnty., Tex., 121 F.4th 520, 526–27
(5th Cir. 2024))).
 In the case Defendant cites, Texas State LULAC v. Elfant , two voter
registration organizations sued county election officials to enjoin the enforcement of
Texas Senate Bill 1111, which made changes to the Texas Election Code’s residency
provisions. Tex. State LULAC , 52 F.4th at 251 –52. In deciding cross -motions for
summary judgment, the district court found that the plaintiff -organizations had
established organizational standing on two grounds: (1) S.B. 1111 chilled the
Case 2:25-cv-01398-CJB-EJD     Document 39     Filed 09/29/26     Page 11 of 25
organizations’ speech, and (2) the organizations had diverted resources “to counteract
the law’s effects on their voter registration activities.” Id. at 252. In other words, the
district court concluded, in part, that S.B. 1111 impaired the organizations’ missions
based on their diversion of resources, but in addition, the court recognized that a
constitutional rights violation also represented a cogni zable injury on which
organizational standing could be based. The United States Court of Appeals for the
Fifth Circuit reversed the district court’s decision without reaching the merits of the
case because the Fifth Circuit held that the organizations lacked Article III standing.
However, the court did not base this holding on a lack of injury in fact, but instead,
the Court found that the plaintiff -organizations had failed to demonstrate
traceability and redressability. Id. at 254–55.
In the instant case, Plaintiffs do not base their argument for organizational
standing on a diversion of resources theory. Instead, Plaintiffs claim that Defendant’s
conduct has “frustrated or otherwise impeded” their ability to carry out their
organizational missions, and that this impairment of the Organizations’ missions
constitutes an injury in fact. (Rec. Doc. 20, at 7). To support this claim, Plaintiffs rely
on Texas Tribune v. Caldwell County, Texas, a case that involved two non-profit news
organizations and an advocacy organization that sought to enjoin the enforcement of
a county policy which “categorically exclude[ed] the press and the public from
observing criminal pretrial proceedings commonly referred to as magistrations.” Tex.
Trib., 121 F.4th at 524. The three plaintiff-organizations’ missions overlapped in that
the news organizations reported on local criminal justice issues, including bail and
Case 2:25-cv-01398-CJB-EJD     Document 39     Filed 09/29/26     Page 12 of 25
pretrial detention, and the advocacy group, Mano Amiga, “relie[d] on access to
magistrations” so that they could bail people out of jail, “advocate for arrestees and
victims of crime,” and provide support and education for the local community. Id. at
527.
All three organizations alleged that the policy prohibiting them from attending
magistrations substantially impeded their missions and violated their First
Amendment right of access. Id. at 526–27. The primary issue the court considered
when evaluating standing was whether the organizations had established a
cognizable injury in fact. The Fifth Circuit affirmed the district court’s ruling that the
organizations had organizational standing to challenge the policy not b ased on a
diversion of resources theory of injury, but instead because the denial of access itself
constituted an injury in fact by impairing the organizations’ missions. Id. at 527.
In this case , Plaintiffs argue that they cannot fulfill their organizational
missions without access to the Buena Vista Cemetery where formerly enslaved
persons are buried. Inclusive Louisiana is a faith -based “grassroots community
advocate organization” whose mission  is “to spread enlightenment and hope to all
people to create a fairer and more inclusive society.” About Us, Inclusive Louisiana,
https://inclusivelouisiana.org/about/ (last visited Mar. 30, 2026). Further, Inclusive
Louisiana’s vision includes educating the community about “social, racial, and
environmental justice.” Id. The property Defendant owns is located in a majority -
black district of St. James Parish, and many of these black residents are likely
descendants of enslaved people. Having access to the unmarked cemeteries of
Case 2:25-cv-01398-CJB-EJD     Document 39     Filed 09/29/26     Page 13 of 25
enslaved people who died on plantations, and being able to recognize, honor, and
respect these final resting places, would inevitably help advance Inclusive
Louisiana’s mission of spreading hope, creating a fairer and more inclusive society,
and educating the community about social, racial, and environmental justice.
Similarly, The Descendants Project “aim[s] to champion the voice of the Black
descendant community while demanding action that supports their total well-being.”
About, The Descendants Project, https://www.thedescendantsproject.org/about (last
visited Mar. 30, 2026). The ability of descendants of enslaved people to acknowledge
their history and to save their ancestors from erasure would inevitably support their
total well-being, and The Descendants Project cannot truly champion the voice of the
Black descendant community without having access to the sacred sites where their
ancestors are buried. In opposition to Defendant’s argument concerning injury,
Plaintiffs allege that they have suffered an injury in fact because FG  has impaired
their missions, namely by restricting their access to the Buena Vista Cemetery and
thereby preventing them from visiting the graves of their ancestors, maintaining the
cemetery, placing gravestones, and otherwise honoring the formerly enslaved
individuals who are buried there.
Here, the injury in fact that Plaintiffs allege meets all requirements for
standing purposes. First, the injury in fact must consist of “an invasion of a legally
protected interest.” Lujan, 504 U.S. at 560 (citations omitted). Louisiana courts have
long affirmed the ancient principle, also recognized at common law and dating back
at least to Justinian, that “[r]egardless of the laws and rules relating to the ownership
Case 2:25-cv-01398-CJB-EJD     Document 39     Filed 09/29/26     Page 14 of 25
and control of real property, when a plot of ground is set apart for cemetery purposes,
and burials are made in the land, the ground changes its character in the minds and
feelings of the community.” Humphreys v. Bennett Oil Corp. , 197 So. 222, 229 (La.
1940). Moreover, Louisiana courts have also required “mandatory access for members
of the public to isolated cemeteries.”2 Ryan M. Seidemann, The Sanctity of the Dead:
Cemetery Protection Laws Are Not Takings of Private Property, 46 S.U. L. Rev. 53, 75
(2018) (citing In re St. James Methodist Church of Hahnville, 95-410 (La. App. 5 Cir.
12/27/95), 666 So. 2d 1206, 1209; Vidrine v. Vidrine, 225 So. 2d 691, 697– 98 (La. Ct.
App. 1969); Roberts v. Stevens, 389 So. 2d 782, 785 (La. Ct. App. 1980); and cases from
other states). Therefore, Plaintiffs have sufficiently alleged that Defendant’s denial
and hindrance of access to the Buena Vista Cemetery have caused an invasion of one
of Plaintiffs’ legally protected interests.
 Second, the injuries that Plaintiffs allege are concrete and particularized. For
example, on some occasions when members of Inclusive Louisiana have visited the

Defendant has called sheriff’s deputies to the property who have threatened Inclusive

Plaintiffs have also provided examples of access restrictions and what they call
Defendant’s “interfering conduct” during the gathering on February 23, 2025, namely
Defendant’s denial of vehicle access to the cemetery and the employment of a private

Next, Defendant asserts that Plaintiffs cannot establish associational, or
representational, standing either. As explained above, for associational standing,
Plaintiffs would have to prove that (1) at least one of Plaintiffs’ members has standing
in their own right, (2) “the interests [each organization] seeks to protect are germane
to the organization’s purpose”; and (3) that the lawsuit would not require the
individual member’s participation. Ass’n of Am. Physicians & Surgeons, Inc., 627 F.3d

at 550. Defendant argues primarily that Plaintiffs have not alleged that any of their
individual members have suffered an injury in fact, and that  even if an individual
member did have standing, that member would have to participate in the proceeding
to prove a direct family lineage to one or more of the people buried at the Buena Vista
site. The Court disagrees.
First, as explained above in the Court’s discussion of organizational standing,
not only have Plaintiffs qua  organizations suffered an injury in fact, but also
Plaintiffs’ individual members who have visited the Buena Vista site and experienced
onerous restrictions meant to impede their access have suffered an injury in fact. Also
as discussed above, the interes ts that the organizations seek to protect are directly
related, and therefore germane, to their organizational purposes.
 As its last justiciability argument for dismissal, Defendant urges that the relief
Plaintiffs seek is not ripe for judicial review. Article III of the Constitution restricts
a federal court’s jurisdiction to actual “cases” or “controversies.” U.S. Const. art. III,
§ 2, cl. 1. Whether this requirement is met when a plaintiff requests relief pursuant
to the federal Declaratory Judgment Act depends upon “whether the facts alleged,
under all the circumstances, show that there is a substantial controversy, between
parties having adverse legal interests, of sufficient immediacy and reality to warrant
the issuance of a declaratory judgment.” Maryland Cas. Co. v. Pacific Coal & Oil Co.,
312 U.S. 270, 273 (1941) (citing Aetna Life Ins. Co. v. Haworth, 300 U.S. 227, 239–42
(1937)).
Case 2:25-cv-01398-CJB-EJD     Document 39     Filed 09/29/26     Page 17 of 25
The controversy at the heart of a declaratory judgment must also be ripe, and
the Supreme Court stated the general rule for determining ripeness in Aetna Life
Insurance Co. v. Haworth when it explained that the controversy “must be a real and
substantial controversy admitting of specific relief through a decree of a conclusive
character, as distinguished from an opinion advising what the law would be upon a
hypothetical state of facts.” Aetna, 300 U.S. at 241 (citations omitted). In general, a
claim is “ripe if any remaining questions are purely legal ones; conversely, a case is
not ripe if further factual development is required.” Chevron U.S.A., Inc. v. Traillour
Oil Co., 987 F.2d 1138, 1153 (5th Cir. 1993). To determine ripeness, a court must
consider and balance “the fitness of the issues for judicial decision and the hardship
to the parties of withholding court consideration.” New Orleans Pub. Serv., Inc. v.
Council of New Orleans, 833 F.2d 583, 586 (5th Cir. 1987) (citations omitted).
Here, Defendant first argues that Plaintiffs have not identified an existing
injury that is concrete, but that instead, their “Complaint describes the possibility of
future or continuing harm.” (Rec. Doc. 9-2, at 10). Furthermore, Defendant states that
Plaintiffs have acknowledged that in the past, “commemorative events occurred

allege exactly the opposite.  Plaintiffs do not deny that Defendant allowed their
members to attend a Black History Month ceremony on Defendant’s property in 2025,
but they claim that obtaining consent to do so involved onerous restrictions and a
filed-but-later-voluntarily-dismissed lawsuit against them. Further, Plaintiffs assert
that their members were harassed and surveilled by the security firm hired by

Defendant during the event. At oral argument, defense counsel pointed out that
Defendant has granted every request for access to the Buena Vista Cemetery site,
and the Court acknowledges that as an accurate statement. However, this consent
did not come “without interference.”
Next, the parties in this matter have adverse legal interests concerning the
Buena Vista Cemetery, and at one point, Defendant must have agreed because it filed
an action against Inclusive Louisiana, among others,  to determine the parties’ legal
rights. Moreover, the only questions remaining in the matter are legal ones, i.e.,
whether Plaintiffs have a legal right to access and maintain the Buena Vista
Cemetery. Finally, this dispute could be resolved by a conclusive judgment issued by
the Court. In oth er words, the Court finds that the issues in this matter are fit for
judicial decision. Furthermore, Plaintiffs would continue to suffer a hardship if

visit a full-scale legal negotiation.
Finally, the Court does not understand how allowing Plaintiffs reasonable

Louisiana Supreme Court explained in Humphreys, “Instinctively [people] resent any
rude invasion of, or approach to, ground set apart as a resting place for the dead.
Deference to this sentiment and respect for the feelings of those who have deposited
the remains of their relatives in burial fields usually restrain others from violating
the sanctity of such places.” Humphreys , 197 So. at 229. It seems that such

considerations would counsel in favor of granting reasonable access to Plaintiffs’
members. In the absence of Defendant’s consent, however, the Court concludes that
Plaintiffs’ claims are fit for judicial determination and that they would suffer
continued hardship without judicial resolution.
II. Motion to Dismiss for Failure to State a Claim
Next, Defendant urges the Court to dismiss Plaintiffs’ claims pursuant to Rule
12(b)(6) for failure to state a claim on which relief can be granted. First, Defendant
contends that the Court should dismiss Plaintiffs’ Thirteenth Amendment claim
because the Thirteenth Amendment does not create a private cause of action, and
therefore, “a plaintiff must proceed under an implementing statute.” (Rec. Doc. 9 -2,
at 13 (citing La. Acorn Fair Hous. v. Quarter House, 952 F. Supp. 352, 357 (E.D. La.
1997))). Plaintiffs maintain, however, that “Formosa’s ownership and control of the
sacred remains and graves of people who died in slavery” constitute a literal and
direct effect of slavery “that was never rectified after the Thirteenth Amendment
entered into forc e.” (Rec. Doc. 20, at 15). Accordingly, Plaintiffs explain that they
bring their Thirteenth Amendment claim both directly under the Thirteenth
Amendment and under an implementing statute, 42 U.S.C. § 1985(3).
 Defendant accurately states the law concerning the Thirteenth Amendment.
Indisputably, Congress has the authority under the Thirteenth Amendment’s
Enforcement Clause “rationally to determine what are the badges and the incidents
of slavery” and “to transla te that determination into effective legislation.” Jones v.
Alfred H. Mayer Co., 392 U.S. 409, 440 (1968). However, the Court knows of no case
Case 2:25-cv-01398-CJB-EJD     Document 39     Filed 09/29/26     Page 20 of 25
in which the right to bring a claim directly under the Thirteenth Amendment has
been recognized.
At the hearing on Defendant’s motion to dismiss, Plaintiffs and Defendant
presented compelling arguments on both sides of this issue. First, defense counsel
raised doubts about a plaintiff’s ability to bring a claim directly under the Thirteenth
Amendment. Specifically, defense counsel asked why the drafters of the Amendment
would have included Section 2, the Enforcement Clause that grants Congress the
“power to enforce this article by appropriate legislation,” if they intended for Section
1 to create a direct cause of action. U.S. Const. amend. XIII. Further, Defendant cited
the Fifth Circuit’s decision in Channer v. Hall  to support its argument that the
Thirteenth Amendment does not provide a stand-alone cause of action, but the court’s
holding in that matter is more nuanced than Defendant acknowledges. Channer v.
Hall, 112 F.3d 214 (1997).
In Channer, a federal inmate  and later Immigration and Naturalization
Service (“INS”) detainee brought a Thirteenth Amendment claim for involuntary
servitude against the warden and other officers of the federal correctional institution
where he was housed. Id.  at 215. He alleged that the correctional officers subjected
him to involuntary servitude after his criminal sentence ended but while he was still
being detained by the INS. Id. The Fifth Circuit held that Channer had not been
involuntarily forced into servitude, id. at 219, but in a footnote, the court stated that
while a suit under Section 2 of the Thirteenth Amendment must be brought pursuant
to a statute enacted by Congress, “suits attacking compulsory labor arise directly
Case 2:25-cv-01398-CJB-EJD     Document 39     Filed 09/29/26     Page 21 of 25
under prohibition of § 1, which is ‘undoubtedly self -executing without any ancillary
legislation’ and ‘[b]y its own unaided force and effect . . . abolished slavery, and
established universal freedom,’” id. at 217 n.5 (quoting The Civil Rights Cases,  109
U.S. 3, 20 (1883)).  This distinction between Section 1 and Section 2 is crucial. The
Fifth Circuit definitively stated that to challenge the badges and incidents of slavery,
a plaintiff must use an implementing statute; however, the court also made it clear
that an individua l may bring a cause of action related to compulsory labor and
involuntary servitude directly under Section 1 of the Thirteenth Amendment.
The instant matter undoubtedly addresses a direct and lingering effect of the
slavery system in the United States, and Plaintiffs argued compellingly that
Defendant retains ownership and control of the people buried at the Buena Vista
Cemetery even to this  day. Ultimately, the Court agrees with Plaintiffs that “the
cemeteries and the continuing inability of Plaintiffs to freely access, care for, and
congregate at Buena Vista, are clear and direct ‘burdens and incapacities [that] were
[and are] the inseparable incidents of the institution’ of slavery.” (Rec. Doc. 20, at 17
(quoting The C.R. Cases, 109 U.S. at 22)). At the same time, the Court agrees with
Defendant concerning Fifth Circuit precedent  that any suit challenging the badges
and incidents of slavery, and not forced labor, must be brought pursuant to an
implementing statute. Accordingly, Plaintiffs may not bring their claim directly
under the Thirteenth Amendment but must use an implementing statute instead.
 In their opposition to Defendant’s motion to dismiss, Plaintiffs point to an
implementing statute, Section 1985(3), and claim that “the Complaint amply states
Case 2:25-cv-01398-CJB-EJD     Document 39     Filed 09/29/26     Page 22 of 25
factual allegations demonstrating all of the elements of a § 1985(3) claim . . . .” Id. at
19. As Defendant asserts, however, Plaintiffs make only a cursory reference to § 1985
in the “Jurisdiction and Venue” section of the complaint. More importantly, the Court
finds that Plaintiffs have not sufficiently alleged facts to establish the elemen ts of a
§ 1985(3) claim. Section 1985 provides a cause of action for civil rights conspiracies
that deprive a person or class of persons of the equal protection of the laws or of equal
privileges and immunities under the laws. See 42 U.S.C. § 1985(3).
In Hilliard v. Ferguson , the Fifth Circuit explained the elements a plaintiff
must allege to state a claim under § 1985(3): “(1) a conspiracy involving two or more
persons; (2) for the purpose of depriving, directly or indirectly, a person or class of
persons of the equal protection of the laws; and (3) an act in furtherance of the
conspiracy; (4) which causes injury to a person or property, or a deprivation of any
right or privilege of a citizen of the United States.” 30 F.3d 649, 652 –53 (5th Cir.
1994). Plaintiffs’ counsel acknowledged at oral argument that the complaint never
uses the word “conspiracy,” and while that is not technically required, the Court
concludes that Plaintiffs did not allege sufficient facts to demonstrate the elements
of a § 1985 c laim. Accordingly, the Court finds that Defendant’s motion to dismiss
Plaintiffs’ Thirteenth Amendment claim should be granted.
 Plaintiffs’ other claim arises under Louisiana law. Plaintiffs allege that this
Court has jurisdiction based on federal question and also pursuant to 28 U.S.C.
§ 1332 “because there is diversity of citizenship among the parties and the amount in
controversy exceeds $75,000.00.” (Rec. Doc. 1, at 6). However, Plaintiffs seek
Case 2:25-cv-01398-CJB-EJD     Document 39     Filed 09/29/26     Page 23 of 25
declaratory and injunctive relief that the Court concludes would not meet the amount
in controversy required for diversity jurisdiction. Only if Plaintiffs are seeking a
transfer of ownership of the property, which the Court cannot grant under the
Thirteenth Amendment, would the amount in controversy exceed $75,000.00.
 Therefore, the Court finds that federal question, and not diversity of
citizenship, constitutes the valid basis for jurisdiction in this matter. Furthermore,
the Court has found that Defendant’s motion to dismiss Plaintiffs’ Thirteenth
Amendment claim should be granted. Without this federal question, the Court
declines to exercise supplemental jurisdiction over Plaintiffs’ state law claim.
 Based on the Court’s decisions concerning Defendant’s motion to dismiss under
Rule 12(b)(6), it is unnecessary for the Court to reach Defendant’s motion to dismiss
pursuant to Rule 12(b)(7).
