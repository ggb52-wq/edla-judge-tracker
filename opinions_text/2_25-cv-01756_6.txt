As soon as practicable after docketing, the court must review a prisoner’s § 1983 complaint
for a cognizable claim, or dismiss the complaint if it is frivolous and/or fails to state a claim.2  The
court must dismiss an in forma pauperis § 1983 complaint if, upon review, it  is frivolous and/or
fails to state a claim.3  A claim is frivolous if it “lacks an arguable basis in law or fact.”4  A claim
lacks an arguable basis in law if it is “based on an indisputably meritless legal theory, such as if

frivolous claim alleges only facts that are “‘clearly baseless,’ . . . are ‘fanciful,’ ‘fantastic,’ and
‘delusional’ . . .  [or] rise to the level of the irrational or wholly incredible . . . .”6  A court may not
dismiss a claim simply because the facts are “unlikely.”7

III. AP PLICABLE LAW AND ANALYSIS  - §1983 CLAIM
A. Required Elements of a § 1983 Claim
Section 1983 creates a damages remedy for the violation of federal constitutional or
statutory rights under color of state law:
Every person who, under color of any statute, ordinance, regulation, custom, or
usage, of any State . . . subjects, or causes to be subjected, any . . . person within
the jurisdiction thereof to the deprivation of any rights, privileges, or immunities
secured by the Constitution and laws, shall be liable to the party injured in an action
at law . . . .16

“The purpose of § 1983 is to deter state actors from using their badge of authority to deprive
individuals of federally guaranteed rights and to provide relief to victims if such deterrence fails.”17
A plaintiff must satisfy three elements to establish § 1983 liability:
(1) deprivation of a right secured by the U.S. Constitution or federal law;
(2) that occurred under color of state law; and
(3) was caused by a state actor.18

Because § 1983 merely provides a remedy for designated rights, rather than creating any
substantive rights, “an underlying constitutional or statutory violation is a predicate to liability.”19
This requires the plaintiff to identify both the constitutional violation and the responsible person
acting under color of state law. 20  “The traditional definition of acting under color of state law
requires that the defendant in a § 1983 action have exercised power ‘possessed by virtue of state

1. Section 1983 Claims Based on Conditions of Confinement
While the Constitution does not require that custodial inmates be housed in comfortable
prisons, the Eighth Amendment's22 prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment does require
that prisoners be afforded “ humane conditions of confinement,” and prison officials must ensure
that inmates receive adequate food, shelter, clothing, and medical care.23  Punishment of a pretrial
detainee posed by conditions of confinement will run afoul of the Constitution. 24  But the
Constitution is not concerned with a “de minimis level of imposition” on pretrial detainees,25 thus,
mere interference with a prisoner's desire to live as comfortably as possible does not convert
conditions of confinement into punishment.26  For that reason, “a detainee[‘s] challeng[e] [to] jail
conditions must demonstrate a pervasive pattern of serious deficiencies in providing for his basic
human needs; any lesser showing cannot prove punishment in violation of the detainee's Due
Process rights.” 27  Further, w hen a prisoner seek s compensatory damages on a conditions -of-
confinement claim, he must allege a physical injury that is more than de minimis.28

cognizable under § 1983.

1. No Supervisory Liability

plead facts showing that (1) the official ‘participated in acts that caused constitutional deprivation’
