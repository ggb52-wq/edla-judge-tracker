A. Summary Judgment Standard
Summary judgment must be granted where “there is no genuine dispute as to
any material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.”  Federal
Rule of Civil Procedure 56 .  The movant has the initial burden of “showing the
absence of a genuine issue as to any material fact.”  Adickes v. S. H. Kress & Co. ,
398 U.S. 144, 157 (1970).  The respondent must then “produce evidence or designate

Nat. Bank of Eagle Lake , 47 F.3d 1459, 1462 (5th Cir. 1995).  Evidence that is
“merely colorable” or “is not significantly probative” is not sufficient to defeat
summary judgment.  Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 249 (1986).
“An issue is material if its resolution could affect the outcome of the action.”
Daniels v. City of Arlington, Tex., 246 F.3d 500, 502 (5th Cir. 2001).  Thus, “there is
no issue for trial unless there is sufficient evidence favoring the nonmoving party for
a jury to return a verdict for that party.”  Anderson, 477 U.S. at 249.  Although this
court must “resolve factual controversies in favor of the nonmoving party,” it must
only do so “where there is an actual controversy, that is, when both parties have
submitted evidence of contradictory facts.”  Antoine v. First Student, Inc., 713 F.3d
824, 830 (5th Cir. 2013) (quoting Boudreaux v. Swift Transp. Co., 402 F.3d 536, 540
(5th Cir. 2005)).  The court must not, “in the absence of any proof, assume that the
nonmoving party could or would prove the necessary facts.” Little v. Liquid Air
Corp., 37 F.3d 1069, 1075 (5th Cir. 1994).
“Summary judgment is appropriate where critical evidence is so weak or
tenuous on an essential fact that it could not support a judgment in favor of the
nonmovant, or where it is so overwhelming that it mandates judgment in favor of
the movant.”  Armstrong v. City of Dallas, 997 F.2d 62, 67 (5th Cir. 1993).  Summary
judgment is also appropriate if the party opposing the motion fails to establish an

B. Applicable Provision of Louisiana Law
 Jones’ negligence claims  are governed by  Louisiana Civil Code article
2317.1, which states:
The owner or custodian of a thing is answerable for damage occasioned
by its ruin, vice, or defect, only upon a showing that he knew or, in the
exercise of reasonable care, should have known  of the ruin, vice, or
defect which caused the damage, that the damage could have been
prevented by the exercise of reasonable care, and that he failed to
exercise such reasonable care.  Nothing in this Article shall preclude
the court from the application of the doctrine of res ipsa loquitur in an
appropriate case.

If a plaintiff asserting a negligence claim under Louisiana law  fails to prove
any one of the required elements by a preponderance of the evidence, the defendant
is not liable.   Farrell v. Circle K Stores, Inc. , 359 So. 3d 467, 473 (La. 2023) .
Under Article 2317.1, a plaintiff must prove, among other elements, the defendant
had actual or constructive knowledge of the defect.   McDuffie v. Hillstone
Restaurant Group, Inc., No. 16-cv-6733, 2017 WL 4011205, at *2 (E.D. La. Sept.
12, 2017); Jones v. Town of Gueydan, 323 So. 3d 451, 453 (La. App. 3 Cir. 2021).
Jones does not allege why the door fell.  Significantly, there is no evidence the
door had ever fallen before.  There is no evidence Kendrick knew the door could or
would fall.  Consequently, there is no evidence Kendrick had “actual notice” that the
door was in any way defective.

“Constructive notice” requires proof that the defect existed over a sufficient
time period that reasonable diligence should have prompted its discovery and repair.
Carroll v. American Empire Surplus Lines Insurance Co. , 289 F.Supp.3d 767, 776
(E.D. La. 2017) (citing Flenner v. Sewerage & Water Bd. of New Orleans, 110 So.3d
661, 665 (La. App. 4 Cir. 2013)).
 Jones argues the alleged paint scratch and the alleged observation that the
sliding door was “wobbly” constitute constructive notice of a defect.  Connected to
this argument  is Jones’ contention that monthly cleanings and inspections were
potentially negligent for failing to determine that the door could fall .  But, again,
Jones presents no evidence why the door fell, which begs the question —what were
the inspections supposed to reveal?   Without evidence of what those inspections
would or should have revealed, the allegation that cleanings and inspections should
have revealed something is speculation.
In fact, Jones’ evidence suggests the door worked until the moment it fell.
Jones testified that after entering the bathroom, she closed the door behind her.  She
stated:

sliding -- When I slid the door and entered, the door was fine. It’s a
shower inside the bathroom. So these are two different doors. I do want
to mention that. The sliding door is to enter the bathroom. Then the
shower door is to enter the shower. So I slid the door open to enter
the bathroom. Then I opened the shower door and the shower door
opened. It doesn't slide. And I entered. I took a shower, got out, wrapped
my towel around me and put my slides on. And when I was coming

out the bathroom, I slid the door open, came out. I slid the door back
and then it fell back on me on my right side.13 (emphasis added).

Clearly, the door slid open , then slid closed, several times during the sequence of
events described by Jones.  Even Jones says when she entered “the door was fine.”
There is no evidence the door was not working at that time.
The wall scratch neither establishes a defect nor suggests the door would fall.
Jones testified no house guest reported the scratch to Kendrick either before or after
the alleged accident.  Even if the scratch is notice of a defect, which it is not on the
evidence presented, there is no evidence how long the scratch was present.  The law
requires evidence the scratch was there for a sufficient time that Kendrick should
have been put on notice of a defect.  See King v. PetSmart LLC, No. 3:21-CV-00225,
2023 WL 7930074 , at *6 (W.D. La. Nov. 16, 2023) (“ Louisiana courts have
consistently held that constructive notice is plainly defined to include a mandatory
temporal element.  A court cannot infer the existence of constructive notice absent
some showing of this temporal element.”) (citing White v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc., 699
So. 2d 1081, 1084 (La. 1997) (internal quotation marks omitted)).
Jones says someone noted the door was “kind of wobbly, but I didn’t think
nothing of it .”  Again, there is no evidence an undefined “wobbly” sliding door
suggests the door is defective, as opposed to properly functioning.  And despite the
