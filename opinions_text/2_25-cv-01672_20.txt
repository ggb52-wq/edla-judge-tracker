March 23, 2025.  (Rec. doc. 1-1 at 4).  Specifically, Plaintiff has alleged that an AT&T worker
or contractor physically entered his locked, secured backyard without consent; performed
unauthorized work; and caused visible property damage.   (Id.).  Plaintiff asserts a plethora

and industry misconduct, and emotional distress and anguish.   (Id. at 7-12).  Plaintiff seeks
monetary damages in the amount of $400,000.00 dollars.  (Id. at 13).

 II. Standard for a Motion to Dismiss Under Rule 12(b)(6)
Rule 8 of t he Federal Rules of Civil Procedure requires that a complaint contain “a
short and plain statement of the claim showing that the pleader is entitled to relief.”  Fed. R.
Civ. P. 8(a)(2).  It “does not require ‘detailed factual allegations,’ but it demands more than
an unadorned, the-defendant-unlawfully-harmed-me accusation.”  Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S.
662, 678 (2009) (quoting Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly , 550 U.S. 544, 555 (2007)).   The
statement of the claim must “‘give the defendant fair notice of what the . . . claim is and the
grounds upon which it rests.’”   Twombly, 550 U.S. at 555 (quoting Conley v. Gibson , 355 U.S.
41, 47 (1957)).   A pleading does not comply with Rule 8 if it offers “labels and conclusions,”
“a formulaic recitation of the elements of a cause of action,” or “‘naked assertions’ devoid of
‘further factual enhancement.’”   Iqbal, 556 U.S. at 678 (quoting Twombly , 550 U.S. at 555,
557) (alteration omitted).
Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure permits a party to move to
dismiss for “failure to state a claim upon which relief can be granted.”  Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(b)(6).
“To survive a motion to dismiss, a complaint must contain sufficient factual matter, accepted
as true, to ‘state a claim to relief that is plausible on its face.’”  Iqbal, 556 U.S. at 678 (quoting
Twombly, 550 U.S. at 570).  A claim is plausible on the face of the complaint “when the
plaintiff pleads factual content that allows the court to draw the reasonable inference that
the defendant is liable for the misconduct alleged.”  Id. (citing Twombly, 550 U.S. at 556).
Plausibility does not equate to probability, but rather “it asks for more than a sheer
possibility that a defendant has acted unlawfully.”  Id. (citing Twombly, 550 U.S. at 556).
“Where a complaint pleads facts that are ‘merely consistent with’ a defendant's liability, it
‘stops short of the line between possibility and plausibility of ‘entitlement to relief.’”  Id.

(quoting Twombly, 550 U.S. at 557).   Thus, if the facts pleaded in the complaint “do not permit
the court to infer more than the mere possibility of misconduct, the complaint has alleged –
but it has not ‘shown’ – ‘that the pleader is entitled to relief.’”  Id. at 679 (quoting Fed. R. Civ.
P. 8(a)(2)) (alteration omitted).
When considering a Rule 12(b)(6) motion to dismiss for failure to state a claim, the
court employs the two- pronged approach described  in Twombly.  Clofer v. Connick , No. CV
23-6268, 2024 WL 4855475 (E.D. La. Nov. 21, 2024), appeal dismissed , No. 25-30139, 2025
WL 2020968 (5th Cir. Apr. 29, 2025).  The court “can choose to begin by identifying pleadings
that, because they are no more than conclusions, are not entitled to the assumption of truth.”
Iqbal, 556 U.S. at 679.  However, “[w]hen there are well -pleaded factual allegations, a court
should assume their veracity and then determine whether they plausibly give rise to an
entitlement to relief.”  Id.  “‘[The] task, then, is to determine whether the plaintiff has stated
a legally cognizable claim that is plausible, not to evaluate the plaintiff's likelihood of
success.’”  Body by Cook, Inc. v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins., 869 F.3d 381, 385 (5th Cir. 2017)
(quoting Doe ex rel. Magee v. Covington Cty. Sch. Dist. , 675 F.3d 849, 854 (5th Cir. 2012)).   A
court's review of a Rule 12(b)(6) motion to dismiss “is limited to the complaint, any
documents attached to the complaint, and any documents attached to the motion to dismiss
that are central to the claim and referenced by the complaint .”  Lone Star Fund V (U.S.), L.P. v.
Barclays Bank PLC, 594 F.3d 383, 387 (5th Cir. 2010) (citing Collins v. Morgan Stanley Dean
Witter, 224 F.3d 496, 498-99 (5th Cir. 2000)) (emphasis added).
Courts “liberally construe briefs of pro se litigants and apply less stringent standards
to parties proceeding pro se than to parties represented by counsel.”  Grant v. Cuellar, 59 F.3d
523, 524 (5th Cir. 1995).  This does not mean, however, that a court “will invent, out of whole

imperfect, briefing.”  Jones v. Alfred, 353 F. App'x 949, 952 (5th Cir. 2009).  Even a liberally-
construed pro se complaint “must set forth facts giving rise to a claim on which relief may be
granted.”  Johnson v. Atkins, 999 F.2d 99, 100 (5th Cir. 1993).
III. Law and Analysis
  AT&T argues that each cause of action alleged by Plaintiff fails to establish factual
allegations that raise his right to relief above the speculative level.  (Rec. doc. 11 -1 at 3).
Plaintiff asserts the following causes of action against AT&T: (A) trespass; (B) negligence; (C)

The primary elements of proof for trespass are (1)  a physical invasion of (2) the
plaintiff’s property by a defendant where (3)  that invasion was unlawful.  Turner v. Murphy
Oil USA, Inc., 234 F.R.D. 597, 609 (E.D. La. 2006) .  Even taking Plaintiff’s allegations as true
(that AT&T entered Plaintiff’s property at some point), public record indicates that Plaintiff’s
property is encumbered by a utility easement both towards Highway 22 and in the rear of
his property.  (Rec. doc. 11-3).  Plaintiff’s property is identified in his Cash Deed as Lot 1 in
the Olde Mill Subdivision.  (R ec. doc. 11 -2).  The attached plat of Olde Mill Subdivision
identifies a “utility servitude” located on the side of Plaintiff’s property parallel to Highway
22 as well as a “utility parcel” in the rear 25 feet of Plaintiff’s property.  (Rec. doc. 11-3).
Access to a utility servitude is given to utility companies, such as AT&T, to install,
maintain, and repair essential infrastructure like lines, cables, and equipment on private
property for public benefit. 4  As Louisiana law provides, the owner of the dominant estate
has the right to enter with his workmen and equipment into the part of the servient estate
that is needed for the construction or repair of works required for the use and preservation
of the servitude.   La. Civ. Code art. 74 5.  The utility pole at question in the supplemental
exhibit to his opposition (rec. doc. 13 at 1-2) is located on the boundary line of the property
that falls within the  recorded utility servitude and is considered part of that easement.

Merely because the utility pole is within Plaintiff’s fenced backyard does not mean it is not
part of the utility servitude.  Any installation at any time would have been within public
utility right of way and therefore not a trespass.  As such, an installation by AT&T would be

As recognized by Plaintiff, servitude rights “are to be exercised in a way least
inconvenient for the servient estate.”   La. Civ. Code art. 743.   A utility company  entering a

failed to provide a sufficient claim for trespass because there was no unlawful invasion, and
therefore there is no entitlement to relief.
B. Negligence
Next, Plaintiff alleges that AT&T acted negligently by failing to exercise reasonable
care in verifying its right of access  before conducting fiber  optic infrastructure work.  He
further alleges that AT&T failed to notify him of its access , made no effort to confirm legal
entry, and caused direct interference to his  private property.   Plaintiff claims there is
currently a loose fiber optic wire near his residence that is unsecured and potentially
hazardous.  He also believes the area may contain live power sources, underground poles, or
improperly-routed equipment that he claims would pose a danger to individuals and a
continued disruption to his home.  (Rec. doc. 1-1 at 4).
To state a negligence claim under Louisiana law, a plaintiff must allege that (1) the
defendant had a duty to conform his conduct to a specific standard; (2) the defendant's
conduct failed to conform to that standard; (3) the defendant's conduct was the cause-in-fact
of the plaintiff's injuries; (4) the defendant's conduct was a legal cause of the plaintiff's

7
injuries; and (5) actual damages.  Alexis v. Hilcorp Energy Co., 493 F. Supp. 3d 497, 508 (E.D.
La. 2020).  Failure to plead a single element with factual plausibility warrants dismissal
under Rule 12(b)(6).  See Iqbal, 556 U.S. at 678.
Here, Plaintiff has failed to allege that actual injury or damages occurred and that they
were caused by AT&T.  Instead, he points to hypothetical harm such as a “potentially
hazardous” loose or exposed fi ber optic wire.  Additionally, h is belief that the area may
contain live power sources, underground poles, or improperly- routed equipment is a
hypothetical harm of which Plaintiff has no actual knowledge.  Accordingly, there is no actual
injury or damage found in Plaintiff’s negligence allegations.   Therefore, Plaintiff’s negligence
claim fails.

 D. Invasion of Privacy
     Plaintiff also claims that he experienced serious intrusion into the privacy and quiet
enjoyment of his home due to unauthorized installation of infrastructure and the presence
of residual EMF interference.   He believes that these actions also contributed to surveillance-

In Louisiana, the tort of invasion of privacy can occur in four ways: (1) by
appropriating an individual's name or likeness; (2) by unreasonably intruding on physical
solitude or seclusion; (3) by giving publicity which unreasonably places a person in a false
light before the public; and (4) by unreasonable public disclosure of embarrassing private
facts.  Bednyak v. Fin. Risk Mitigation, Inc ., 739 F. Supp. 3d 353, 372  (E.D. La. 2024)  (citing
Juge v. Springfield Wellness, L.L.C ., 274 So. 3d a t 8 (La. Ct. App. 2019) (citing Alessi v. Loehn,
76 So. 3d 1142, 1143 (La. 2011)).  Here, Plaintiff has failed to raise any of the four ways in
which invasion of privacy can occur.

E. Emotional Distress
    Plaintiff further alleges that he has endured prolonged mental anguish, stress, and
emotional hardship because of AT&T’s conduct, which includes the lack of transparency,
refusal to provide justification, and dismissive communication by company representatives.
“For a cause of action for intentional or negligent infliction of emotional distress,
there must be proof that the defendant violated some legal duty to the plaintiff, and the
plaintiff must meet the heavy burden of proving outrageous conduct by the defend ant.”
Succession of Harvey v. Dietzen, 716 So. 2d 911, 917 (La. Ct. App. 1998).  “[P]laintiff must show
an ‘especial likelihood of genuine and serious mental distress, arising from the special
circumstances, which serves as a guarantee that the claim is not spurious. ’”  Doerr v. Mobil
Oil Corp.,  935 So.2d 231, 237 (La. Ct. App. 2006)  (quoting Moresi v. State Through Dep't of
Wildlife and Fisheries, 567 So. 2d 1081, 1096 (La. 1990)).
Here, there is no allegation of outrageous conduct at all in Plaintiff’s Complaint. Lack
of transparency, refusal to provide justification, and dismissive communications by company

 G. Leave to Amend
The Federal Rules of Civil Procedure provide that leave to amend “shall be freely
given when justice so requires.”  Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).  However, leave to amend is “by no
means automatic.”  Addington v. Farmer's Elevator Mut. Ins. Co., 650 F.2d 663, 666 (5th Cir.
1981).  Instead, the decision to grant or deny leave is one left to the sound discretion of the
trial court.  In deciding whether leave should be granted, the district court can consider
Case 2:25-cv-01672-DJP-MBN     Document 20     Filed 02/03/26     Page 11 of 13

12
factors such as “undue delay, bad faith or dilatory motive on the part of the movant, repeated
failure to cure deficiencies by amendments previously allowed undue prejudice to the
opposing party . . . [and] futility of amendment.”  Foman v. Davis, 371 U.S. 178, 182 (1962).
“When the dismissal of a pro se complaint is appropriate, it should generally be done
without prejudice in order to allow the plaintiff an opportunity to file an amended
complaint.”  Alderson v. Concordia Par. Corr. Facility , 848 F.3d 415, 423 (5th Cir. 2017)
(emphasis added) (citation omitted).  “However, a district court may dismiss with prejudice
if the plaintiff has been given adequate opportunity to cure the inadequacies in his pleading
or if the pleadings ‘demonstrate that the plaintiff has pleaded his best case.’”  Id. (quoting
Jacquez v. Procunier, 801 F.2d 789, 792-93 (5th Cir. 1986) and citing Brewster v. Dretke, 587
F.3d 764, 768 (5th Cir. 2009)).
Here, Plaintiff was given no “opportunity to cure the inadequacies in his pleading.”  Id.
And the pleadings demonstrate that he has not pleaded his best case.  Plaintiff has not been
able to plead crucial elements of a plausible claim because he lacked the necessary  facts to
allege a claim that is plausible on its face.   “With the benefit of more specific allegations,
[Plaintiff] may be able to state a claim.”  Id. at 423-24.  Thus, “the general rule that dismissal
should be without prejudice applies.”  Id. at 424.  The Court therefore recommends that
Plaintiff be allowed to amend his Complaint no later than 30 days from any dispositive action
by the District Judge.
