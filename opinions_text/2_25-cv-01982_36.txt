II. STANDARDS OF REVIEW
A. Rule 12(b)(1) Standard
A Rule 12(b)(1) motion challenges the subject matter jurisdiction of the district court.  A
motion to dismiss for lack of subject matter jurisdiction should be granted only if “it appears certain

that the plaintiff cannot prove any set of facts in support of his claims entitling him to relief.”3
A court must address a subject matter motion before other substantive motions to avoid a
court without jurisdiction prematurely dismissing a case with prejudice.4  If the court lacks subject
matter jurisdiction, it should dismiss without prejudice.5
B. Rule 12(b)(6) Standard
Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6) provides that an action may be dismissed for
“failure to state a claim upon which relief can be granted.”  A motion to dismiss for failure to state
a claim is “ not meant to resolve disputed facts or test the merits of a lawsuit. ”6  Rather, it tests
whether, in plaintiff's best-case scenario, the complaint states a plausible case for relief.7
A complaint fails to state a claim on which relief may be granted when the factual
allegations do not rise above a speculative level, with the assumption that all factual allegations in
the complaint are true, even if doubtful. 8  “The Rule 12(b)(6) analysis is generally confined to a
review of the complaint and its proper attachments.” 9  The Fifth Circuit has summarized the
standard for Rule 12(b)(6):
“To survive a motion to dismiss, a complaint must contain sufficient factual matter,
accepted as true, to ‘state a claim to relief that is plausible on its face.’”  A claim
for relief is plausible on its face “when the plaintiff pleads factual content that
allows the court to draw the reasonable inference that the defendant is liable for the
misconduct alleged.”  A claim for relief is implausible on its face when “the well-

III. DISCUSSION
An officer’s performance of his official duties creates two potential liabilities under § 1983:
personal-capacity liability for him and official- capacity liability for the governmental entity that
employs him. 12  Personal -capacity suits seek to impose individual liability upon an officer for
actions taken under color of state law, so a victory in a personal -capacity suit is against the
individual defendant rather than the entity that employs him. 13  Official-capacity suits generally
represent another way of pleading an action against an entity of which an officer is an agent. 14
Because the governmental entity is the real party in interest, “an official -capacity suit is, in all
respects other than name, to be treated as a suit against the entity.”15  Thus, “a plaintiff seeking to
recover on a damages judgment in an official -capacity suit must look to the government entity
itself.”16

A. Official Capacity Claims under § 1983
Section 1983 creates a damages remedy for the violation of federal constitutional or
statutory rights under color of state law. “The purpose of § 1983 is to deter state actors from using
the badge of their authority to deprive individuals of their federally guaranteed rights and to
provide relief to victims if such deterrence fails.”18
1. Eleventh Amendment Immunity
The Eleventh Amendment bars “suits against state officials or agencies that are effectively
suits against a state .”19  The Eleventh Amendment provides: “The judicial power of the United
States shall not be construed to extend to any suit in law or in equity, commenced or prosecuted
against one of the United States by Citizens or Subjects of any Foreign State.”20  This language
establishes an immunity that bars actions in federal court “against state governments by anyone
other than the federal government or another state”21 for “retrospective relief in the form of a
money judgment in compensation for past wrongs.” 22  Louisiana has not waived its right to
sovereign immunity.23  Eleventh Amendment immunity, therefore, bars any suit seeking monetary

3. Ex Parte Young
Sovereign immunity is not limitless, however.  One important caveat is the Ex Parte Young
exception.29  Under Ex Parte Young, a litigant may sue a state official in his official capacity if the
suit seeks prospective injunctive relief to redress an ongoing violation of federal law. 30  To fall
within Ex Parte Young, the suit must  (1) be brought against state officers who are acting in their
official capacities; (2) seek prospective relief to redress ongoing conduct; (3) allege a violation of
federal, not state, law; and (4) seek only prospective, equitable (i.e., declaratory or injunctive )
relief, not monetary damages.31  In essence, “a court need only conduct a ‘straightforward inquiry
into whether [the] complaint alleges an ongoing violation of federal law and seeks relief properly
characterized as prospective.’”32  In Hope v. Harris, the Fifth Circuit applied the Ex Parte Young
exception to a prisoner challenging his solitary confinement conditions.33  In that case, the named

B. Individual Capacity Claims under the ADA
The ADA was created “to provide a clear and comprehensive national mandate for the
elimination of discrimination against individuals with disabilities.” 39  Unlike § 1983, the ADA
does not create two potential avenues of liability for a defendant.  Rather, Title  II of the statute
protects the rights of “qualified individual[s] with a disability” from being “excluded from
participation in or . . . denied the benefits of the services, programs, or activities of a public entity,
or be subjected to discrimination by any such entity.”40  Thus, Title II only applies to public entities
(including state prisons)—not individuals.41
Indeed, the Fifth Circuit has made clear that t he ADA does “not permit personal-capacity
suits against government officials.”42  In other words, a plaintiff suing government officials under
the ADA “must necessarily pursue official-capacity claims.”43
To the extent Plaintiff seeks to assert ADA claims asserted against Defendants in their
individual capacities, those claims should be dismissed with prejudice.
