II. Rule 12(b)(6) Standard
 Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure allows a party to move for
dismissal of a complaint for failure to state a claim upon which relief can be granted.  Such a
motion is rarely granted because it is viewed with disfavor.  See Lowrey v. Tex. A & M Univ.
Sys., 117 F.3d 242, 247 (5th Cir. 1997) (quoting Kaiser Aluminum & Chem. Sales, Inc. v.
Avondale Shipyards, Inc., 677 F.2d 1045, 1050 (5th Cir. 1982)).
Under Rule 8(a)(2) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, a pleading must contain
a “short and plain statement of the claim showing that the pleader is entitled to relief.”
Ashcroft v. Iqbal , 556 U.S. 662, 678 -79 (2009)  (citing Fed. R. Civ. P. 8).  “[T]he pleading
standard Rule 8 announces does not require ‘detailed factual allegations,’ but it demands
more than an unadorned, the- defendant-unlawfully-harmed-me accusation.”  Id. at 678
(citing Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly, 550 U.S. 544, 555 (2007)).

In considering a Rule 12(b)(6) motion, the Court “accept[s] all well -pleaded facts as
true and view[s] all facts in the light most favorable to the plaintiff.”  See Thompson v. City of
Waco, Tex ., 764 F.3d 500, 502 (5th Cir. 2014)  (citing Doe ex rel. Magee v. Covington Cnty.
Sch. Dist. ex rel. Keys, 675 F.3d 849, 854 (5th Cir. 2012) (en banc)).  But, in deciding whether
dismissal is warranted, the Court will not accept conclusory allegations in the complaint as
true.  Id. at 502-03 (citing Iqbal, 556 U.S. at 678).
To avoid dismissal, “‘a complaint must contain sufficient factual matter, accepted as
true, to state a claim to relief that is plausible on its face.’”   Gonzalez v. Kay , 577 F.3d 600,
603 (5th Cir. 2009)  (quoting Iqbal, 556 U.S. at 678)  (internal quotation marks omitted).
“Factual allegations must be enough to raise a right to relief above the speculative level, on
the assumption that all the allegations in the complaint are true (even if doubtful in fact).”
Twombly, 550 U.S. at 555 (citations and footnote omitted).  “A claim has facial plausibility
when the plaintiff pleads factual content that allows the court to draw the reasonable
inference that the defendant is liable for the misconduct alleged.”  Iqbal, 556 U.S. at 678
(“The plausibility standard is not akin to a ‘probability requirement,’ but it asks for more
than a sheer possibility that a defendant has acted unlawfully.”). This is a “context-specific
task that requires the reviewing court to draw on its judicial experience and common
sense.”    Id.  at 679.  “Where a complaint pleads facts that are merely consistent with a
defendant's liability, it stops short of the line between possibility and plausibility of
entitlement to relief.”  Id. at 678 (internal quotations omitted) (citing Twombly, 550 U.S. at
557).  “[A] plaintiff's obligation to provide the ‘grounds’ of his ‘entitle[ment] to relief’,” thus,
“requires more than labels and conclusions, and a formulaic recitation of the elements of a

cause of action will not do.”   Twombly, 550 U.S. at 555 (alteration in original) (citation
omitted).
Finally, “[w]hen reviewing a motion to dismiss, a district court ‘must consider the
complaint in its entirety, as well as other sources ordinarily examined when ruling on Rule
12(b)(6) motions to dismiss, in particular, documents incorporated into the complaint by
reference, and matters of which a court may take judicial notice. ’”  Funk v. Stryker Corp. ,
631 F.3d 777, 783 (5th Cir. 2011)  (quoting Tellabs, Inc. v. Makor Issues & Rights, Ltd. , 551
U.S. 308, 322 (2007)).  If the Court considers materials outside of the pleadings, the motion
to dismiss must be treated as a motion for summary judgment under Rule 56.  See Causey v.
Sewell Cadillac-Chevrolet, Inc., 394 F.3d 285, 288 (5th Cir. 2004); Phoenix on behalf of S.W. v.
Lafourche Par. Gov't, No. CV A. 19-13004, 2021 WL 184909, at *3 -4 (E.D. La. Jan. 19, 2021) ;
see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(d).
III. Law and Analysis
A. Motion to Dismiss
The pertinent language of 42 U.S.C. § 1983 provides:
Every person who, under color of any statute,
ordinance, regulation, custom, or usage of any State . . .
subjects, or causes to be subjected, any . . . person
within the jurisdiction thereof to the deprivation of any
rights, privileges, or immunities secured by the
Constitution and laws, shall be liable to the party
injured in an action at law. . . .

The statue provides plaintiffs a damages remedy for the violation of constitutional
or statutory rights under color of state law.   Martin v. Dep’t of Children & Family Servs. , 500
F. Supp. 3d 527, 539 (E.D. La. 2020).  In order for a plaintiff to succeed in bringing a S ection
1983 claim, he  must satisfy the following elements of liability: (1) deprivation of a right

secured by the U.S. Constitution or federal law; (2) that occurred under color of state law;
and (3) was caused by a state actor.  Id.  A p laintiff is required to identify both the
Constitutional violation and the responsible person acting under color of state law.  Victoria
W. v. Larpenter , 369 F.3d 475, 482 (5th Cir. 2004) (citation omitted).   “In the context of a
12(b)(6) motion in a section 1983 suit, the focus should be whether the complaint properly
sets forth a claim of deprivation of rights, privileges, or immunities secured by the
Constitution or laws of the United States caused by persons acting under color of state law.
If there is no deprivation of any protected right, the claim is properly dismissed.”  S.
Christian Leadership Conf. v. Supreme Ct. of State of La., 252 F.3d 781, 786 (5th Cir. 2001).
 Williams first contends that Plaintiff fails to establish th at he was deprived of a
Constitutional or statutory right.   While Williams notes that Plaintiff alleges that he was
“assaulted without justification” – which allegedly violated his Fourteenth Amendment
rights – Williams liberally construes Plaintiff’s C omplaint as more properly alleging a
violation of his Eighth Amendment right to be free from cruel and unusual punishment.
Indeed, “the Eighth Amendment, which is specifically concerned with the unnecessary and
wanton infliction of pain in penal institutions, serves as the primary source of substantive
protection to convicted prisoners in cases such as this one, where the deliberate use of
force is challenged as excessive and unjustified.”  Whitley v. Albers , 475 U.S. 312, 327
(1986).
The Eighth Amendment prohibits cruel and unusual punishment.  Greenwald v.
Cantrell, 675 F. Supp. 3d 709, 716 (E.D. La. 2023), appeal dismissed as moot sub nom.
Greenwald v. Murrill, No. 23 -30429, 2024 WL 1929012 (5th Cir. May 2, 2024).    Claims of
excessive force used to subdue a convicted prisoner are analyzed under the Eighth

Amendment.  Whitley, 475 U.S. at 327.  When considering whether force was excessive, and
therefore unconstitutional, the Supreme Court has instructed that “the core judicial inquiry
is . . . whether force was applied in a good -faith effort to maintain or restore discipline, or
maliciously and sadistically to cause harm.”   Hudson v. McMillan , 503 U.S. 1, 6- 7 (1992); see
Martin v. Seal , 510 F. App’x 309, 312 (5th Cir. 2013).   An excessive force claims involves
both an objective and subjective inquiry.   Mosley v. White , 464 F. App’x 206, 211- 12 (5th
Cir. 2010) (per curiam).
To prevail on an excessive use of force claim, a Plaintiff must prove the subjective
component by establishing that the correctional officer “acted maliciously and sadistically
in an ‘unnecessary and wanton infliction of pain.’”  Id.   A court should consider the
following: (1) the need for the application of force; (2) the relationship between the need
and the amount of force used; (3) the threat reasonably perceived by the responsible
official; (4) any efforts made to temper the severity of a forceful response; and (5) the
extent of the injury suffered.  Baldwin v. Stadler, 137 F.3d 836, 839 (5th Cir. 1998); Hudson ,
962 F.2d at 523.
Only when Plaintiff  began to yell obscenities at Nichols and Wallace and spit on
Wallace did Nichols and Wallace use any amount of force.  Williams was not involved in this
initial application of force as he was not present at the scene .   Williams was involved only
as backup to secure Plaintiff  as he resisted the control of Wallace and Nichols.   Williams
noted that Plaintiff  was “twisting his upper body in a[n] aggressive manner in attempt to
break free of officer’s grasp.”   (Rec. doc. 22 -2 at 3).   In order to bring  Plaintiff into
compliance to prevent his escape from Wallace and Nichols , Williams was forced to take

Considering only his own allegations in the pleadings  and his opposition – which parrots

Williams joined in to gain control and bring Plaintiff  into compliance.  This evidence and

behavior on the part of Defendant Williams.

The Fifth Circuit has concluded that injuries similar to Plaintiff’s are de minimis.  In
Siglar v. Hightower, the Fifth Circuit held that a prisoner’s sore and bruised ear was a de
minimis injury.  112 F.3 191, 193- 94 (5th Cir. 1997).  Siglar was discovered sneaking food,
and as a result, was subject to a use of force event that resulted in a sore, bruised ear.  Id.  at
193.  The Court found that the use of force was justified to bring Siglar into compliance, and
the injury was therefore de minimis .  Id.  at 193- 94.  In Lee v. Wilson , the Fifth Circuit
affirmed the dismissal of plaintiff Lee’s excessive force claim, when Lee received a lip
injury, holding that the injury was de minimis, and the conduct was not repugnant to the
conscience of mankind.  237 F. App’x 965, 966 (5th Cir. 2017).
The Court finds  that Williams’ assistive actions were justified and within reason
given that all of the officers were reacting to Plaintiff’s  blatantly disrespectful outburst and
noncompliance towards Wallace and Nichols.   Considering Plaintiff’s manageable injury in
light of the surrounding circumstances , Williams’ actions were justified , and Plaintiff’s
injuries are therefore de minimis .  Thus, Plaintiff is unable to meet the objective standard
for a successful excessive use of force claim.  Because Plaintiff is unable to establish that a
Constitutional right was violated, his excessive force claim against D efendant Williams
should be dismissed for failure to state a claim for excessive force under Section 1983.
Plaintiff also raises a vague allegation that the four correctional officers fabricated
their reports to suggest that Plaintiff attempted to spit on Wallace.  Given that Williams was
not present during this event, and his report makes no mention of it, this Section 1983
claim is irrelevant to Defendant Williams individually.8

B. Motion for Leave
This motion, filed by Plaintiff, is largely incomprehensible, but loosely interpreted, it
appears to be some sort of a motion to amend  his Complaint and/or pleadings .  Plaintiff
seems to seek to add a conspiracy claim against all four original Defendants and a claim
regarding the grievance procedure.
The Court finds that any attempt to amend is futile.  Under Rule 15(a) of the Federal
Rules of Civil Procedure, “[a] party may amend its pleading once as a matter of course”
within 21 days of service or 21 days after service of a responsive pleading or motion under
Rule 12(b), (e), or (f).  Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a)(1)(a), (b).  In all other cases, a party may amend
its pleading with the opposing party's written consent or leave of court, which leave should
be freely granted when justice so requires.  Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a)(2).  As Plaintiff failed to
exercise his opportunity to amend as of right within 21 days after service of the Rule 12
motion in this case, and because Williams does not consent, Plaintiff’s request to amend is
governed by Rule 15(a)(2), which provides that the “court should freely give leave [to
amend] when justice so requires.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a)(2).
The five relevant factors considered in determining whether leave to amend is
proper or there is substantial reason to deny the request are: (1) undue delay, (2) bad faith
or dilatory motive, (3) repeated failure to cure deficiencies by previous amendments, (4)
undue prejudice to the opposing party, and (5) futility of the amendment.  Gregory v.
Mitchell, 634 F.2d 199,  203 (citing Foman v. Davis , 371 U.S. 178, 182 (1962)); see also
Strickland v. Bank of New York Mellon , 838 F. App'x 815, 821 (5th Cir. 2020) (“Denying a
motion to amend is not an abuse of discretion if allowing an amendment would be futile.”)

(quoting Marucci Sports, L.L.C. v. Nat'l Collegiate Athletic Ass'n , 751 F.3d 368, 378 (5th Cir.

To prevail on a conspiracy claim under Section 1983, Plaintiff must show 1) the
existence of a conspiracy involving state action and 2) the deprivation of civil rights in
furtherance of the conspiracy.  Morales v. Carrillo, 625 F. Supp. 3d 587, 598 (W.D. Tex.2022)
(citing Shaw v. Villanueva, 918 F.3d 414, 419 (5th Cir. 2019)).  Plaintiff must bring forward
“facts tending to show that defendants entered into an agreement to deprive him of his
[rights].”  Leggett v. Williams , 277 F. App’x 498, 501 (5th Cir. 1984).  A conspiracy may be
proved by circumstantial evidence.  Mack v. Newton , 737 F.2d 1343, 1350 (5th Cir. 1984).
However, “a mere scintilla of evidence is insufficient to present a question for the jury.
Unreasonable inferences, speculation, and conjecture are also inadequate.”  Imani v. City of
Baton Rouge , 614 F. Supp.  3d 306, 374 (M.D. La. 2022) (citing Mack v. Newton , 737 F.2d
1343, 1350 (5th Cir. 1984)).
Plaintiff’s motion for leave seeks to amend his original C omplaint by adding
speculative and conclusory allegations of conspiracy against D efendants in furtherance of
discipline imposed on Plaintiff at a correctional facility for a putative rules violation.
Plaintiff offers no evidence supporting his claims for conspiracy except for the statement
“[a]ll officer committed conspiracy to commit a lawful act by unlawful means a conspiracy
to injure the plaintiff.”  (Rec. doc. 34 at 1).  “Merely conclusory allegations of conspiracy
cannot, absent reference to material facts, state a substantial claim of federal conspiracy
under 42 U.S.C. § 1983.”  Imani, 614 F. Supp. 3d at 374 (M.D. La. 2022) (citing Leggett , 277
F. App’x at 501).  Accordingly, any amendment to add a conspiracy claim is futile because
Plaintiff can provide no material facts to support such a claim.

373-74).  Thus, allegations that a prison official has failed to follow particular

support a Section 1983 claim without an independent constitutional
violation.  McFaul v. Valenzuela, 684 F.3d 564, 579 (5th Cir. 2012).

 Neither is Plaintiff entitled to a default judgment.  Under Rule 55 of the Federal
Rules of Civil Procedure, federal courts have the authority to enter a default judgment
against a defendant who has failed to plead or otherwise defend upon motion of the
plaintiff.  Fed. R. Civ. P. 55(a) -(b).  That being said, “[d]efault judgments are a drastic
remedy, not favored by the Federal Rules and resorted to by courts only in extreme
situations.”  Sun Bank of Ocala v. Pelican Homestead & Sav. Ass'n, 874 F.2d 274, 276 (5th Cir.
1989).  A party is not entitled to a default judgment merely because the defendant is
technically in default.  Ganther v. Ingle, 75 F.3d 207, 212 (5th Cir. 1996).  “Rather, a default
judgment is generally committed to the discretion of the district court.”  United States v.
1998 Freightliner Vin #: 1FUYCZYB3WP886986 , 548 F.Supp.2d 381, 384 (W.D. Tex. 2008)
(citing Mason v. Lister, 562 F.2d 343, 345 (5th Cir. 1977)).
