FINDINGS OF FACT AND CONCLUSIONS OF LAW

This case was tried to the Court sitting without a jury on April 27-29, 2026. The
matter was taken under advisement on August 17, 2026, upon the filing of the parties=
post-trial memoranda. Having considered the testimony and evidence at trial, the
deposition testimony of Dr. G. Randolph Rice taken on May 13, 2026, the arguments of
counsel, and the applicable law, the Court now enters the following Findings of Fact and
Conclusions of Law in accordance with Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 52(a). To the
extent that any finding of fact may be construed as a conclusion of law, the Court
hereby adopts it as such. To the extent that any conclusion of law constitutes a finding
of fact, the Court adopts it as such.
I. FINDINGS OF FACT
This is an action under the Federal Tort Claims Act, 28 U.S.C. '' 2671, et seq.,
(AFTCA@) resulting from a collision between a vehicle being driven by a United States
Coast Guard (AUSCG@) employee, Chief Petty Officer George P. Harmansky, and a
vehicle in which the plaintiff, Mr. Moncell Collins, was a passenger.
The two-car collision occurred on April 14, 2021, at approximately 1:37 a.m. in

Harmansky had rented this vehicle from Enterprise (using a government travel card)

Three other USCG members were passengers in the Yukon: Brian Scialabba,

Collins was a restrained rear passenger (riding behind the front passenger) in the
second vehicle involved in the collision, a 1999 Suburban owned by Candis Guillot.
At the time of the accident, Harmansky and the other USCG members were
returning from an emergency Coast Guard search and rescue mission, and Harmansky
was driving the rented Yukon northbound on Louisiana Highway 1 (“LA 1”) in Port
Fourchon, Louisiana. The Suburban was traveling southbound on LA 1. LA 1 is a two-
lane highway with a single lane for each direction of travel.
The Suburban was company-provided transportation to bring Collins from
Lafayette, Louisiana to Port Fourchon where he was scheduled to go offshore for work.
Collins was employed as an offshore cook and usually worked a 28-on/14-off shift.
When the Suburban left Lafayette, Rodney Martin was driving the vehicle. Candis
Guillot, the owner of the 1999 Suburban and Martin’s then-girlfriend, was a passenger in
the vehicle.
As Harmansky approached a slight right curve in the road, a disabled Acme “hot
shot” truck with a trailer was stopped ahead on the right shoulder of the road and
partially occupying the roadway in the lane in which Harmansky was traveling. The road

was curved in the area where the disabled truck was located. Harmansky was
attempting to pass the disabled truck and trailer when the accident occurred. The
disabled truck was one to two feet in Harmansky’s lane of travel.
Collins was asleep when the accident occurred. Collins recalls going forward and
backward due to the impact, and he surmises that he might have lost consciousness.
When he came to there was glass all over the back seat and no one was in the car.
Collins left the scene in an ambulance and was brought to the emergency room. Collins

Harmansky had worked a 16 hour day when the accident occurred. Harmansky
crossed over the center line into the oncoming lane of traffic when he was attempting to
pass the Acme truck.
Although Harmansky testified that the truck did not have its hazard lights on, and
later complained that the truck was not sufficiently pulled over to the side of LA 1, none
of these factors contributed to the accident because Harmansky knew that the truck was
in his lane of travel, and rather than wait until the opposite lane of traffic was clear, he
decided to proceed nonetheless and crossed over the center line into the oncoming
lane of traffic to avoid hitting the Acme truck. Harmansky successfully avoided hitting
the Acme truck but he misjudged how long he would have to travel in the opposite lane
of travel in order to successfully avoid hitting it. In taking steps to avoid hitting the Acme
truck, Harmansky caused the collision with the Suburban.
The disabled truck had its hazard flashers on when the accident occurred.
The front driver’s side of the Yukon and the front driver’s side of the Suburban
made impact.

The heavily-damaged Suburban came to rest in its own lane of travel. The

The road was newly-paved with the centerline clearly visible.
Trooper Mark Charpentier was on duty that night and responded at the scene,

Charpentier observed gouge marks in the pavement which generally will indicate

misjudged how fast the Suburban was approaching him. Harmansky assumed that the
Suburban would pull over to avoid hitting him since he was driving in the Suburban’s
lane in order to avoid making contact with the disabled truck.
Candis Guillot was called as a witness by the defense. Guillot claimed that

when he received the call to pick Collins up to drive him to Port Fourchon. Guillot did not

Guillot’s testimony was unreliable and unconvincing for various reasons. The

Collins was not at fault for the collision. Whether Collins had been drinking prior

The defense’s theory is that Martin was actually driving and Guillot lied about

Alcohol did not play a role in the accident.
Harmansky was not driving in a careful, prudent manner when the accident
occurred. Harmansky was 100% at fault for the accident. There is no bona fide dispute
as to liability.

II. CONCLUSIONS OF LAW
The Court has subject matter jurisdiction over this action pursuant to 28 U.S.C. '
1346(b). Venue is proper in this district and is not contested.
Collins filed a timely administrative tort claim and exhausted his administrative
remedies under the FTCA prior to filing this civil action.
Harmansky was acting within the course and scope of his federal employment
with the USCG on April 14, 2021, when the accident occurred.
Louisiana law applies to the incident in this case.
Louisiana courts have adopted a duty-risk analysis in determining whether to
impose liability under the general negligence principles of Civil Code article 2315.1
James v. Landry, 417 So. 3d 666, 672–73 (La. App. 1st Cir. 2025). For liability to attach

1 “Every act whatever of man that causes damage to another obliges him by whose fault it
happened to repair it.” La. Civ. Code art. 2315(A).

under a duty-risk analysis, a plaintiff must prove five separate elements: (1) the
defendant had a duty to conform his or her conduct to a specific standard of care (the
duty element); (2) the defendant failed to conform his conduct to the appropriate
standard of care (the breach of duty element); (3) the defendant's substandard conduct
was a cause in fact of the plaintiff's injuries (the cause in fact element); (4) the
defendant's substandard conduct was a legal cause of the plaintiff's injuries (the scope
of protection element); and (5) actual damages (the damage element). Id. (citing
Pinsonneault v. Merchants & Farmers Bank & Trust Co., 816 So. 2d 270, 275-76 (La.
2002)).
A motorist has a duty to keep his vehicle under proper control and to maintain a
proper lookout for hazards, which by the use of ordinary care and observation one
should be able to see. Johnson, H. Alston, La. Civ. Law Treatise Civil Jury Ins. § 4:1 (3d
ed. 2011).
A motorist shall not move his vehicle from the designated line of traffic until he
has first ascertained that such movement can be made with safety. La. R.S. §§ 32:78(B)
& 79(1). No vehicle shall cross the painted continuous centerline of any multiple lane
highway, except for the purpose of making a turn. La. R.S. § 32:82(B).
When a vehicle on the wrong side of the road collides with another car which is in
its correct lane of travel, there is a presumption that the driver on the wrong side of the
road was negligent. Johnson, H. Alston, La. Civ. Law Treatise Civil Jury Ins. § 4:18 (3d
ed. 2011).
Harmansky breached the motorist duties imposed by Louisiana law.
Harmansky’s breach was the cause in fact and legal cause of Collins’s injuries.

Collins sustained actual damages as a result of Harmansky’s substandard
conduct.
