II. APPLICABLE LAW
A. Rule 12(b)(6) Standard
To survive a Rule 12(b)(6) motion to dismiss, a plaintiff must plead enough facts “to state
a claim for relief that is plausible on its face.”10  A claim is “plausible on its face” when the pleaded
facts allow the court to “draw the reasonable inference that the defendant is liable for the
misconduct alleged.”11  A court must accept the complaint’s factual allegations as true and must
“draw all reasonable inferences in the plaintiff's favor.”12  The court need not, however, accept as
true legal conclusions couched as factual allegations.13  To be legally sufficient, a complaint must
establish more than a “sheer possibility” that the plaintiff’s claims are true.14  If it is apparent from
the face of the complaint that an insurmountable bar to relief exists, and the plaintiff is not entitled
to relief, the court must dismiss the claim. 15  The court’s review is limited to the complaint and
any documents attached to the motion to dismiss that are central to the claim and referenced by
the complaint.16
B. Required Elements of a § 1983 Claim
Section 1983 creates a damages remedy for the violation of federal constitutional or
statutory rights under color of state law:
Every person who, under color of any statute, ordinance, regulation, custom, or
usage, of any State . . . subjects, or causes to be subjected, any . . . person within
the jurisdiction thereof to the deprivation of any rights, privileges, or immunities
secured by the Constitution and laws, shall be liable to the party injured in an action
at law . . . .17

“The purpose of § 1983 is to deter state actors from using their badge of  authority to deprive
individuals of federally guaranteed rights and to provide relief to victims if such deterrence fails.”18
A plaintiff must satisfy three elements to establish § 1983 liability:
(1) deprivation of a right secured by the U.S. Constitution or federal law;
(2) that occurred under color of state law; and
(3) was caused by a state actor.19
Because § 1983 merely provides a remedy for designated rights, rather than creating any
substantive rights, “an underlying constitutional or statutory violation is a predicate to liability.”20
This requires the plaintiff to identify both the constitutional violation and the responsible person
acting under color of state law.21  “The traditional definition of acting under color of state law
requires that the defendant in a § 1983 action have exercised power ‘possessed by virtue of state
law and made possible only because the wrongdoer is clothed with the authority of state law.’”22

 A plaintiff states a claim for relief under Title II of the ADA, which prohibits discrimination
in services, programs, or activities of a public entity, if he alleges: (1) that he has a qualifying
disability; (2) that he is being denied the benefits of services, programs, or activities for which the

such discrimination is by reason of his disability.29
 As a threshold issue, the parties dispute whether the OPDA Defendants, as individual state
employees, are subject to suit under the ADA.  As the Fifth Circuit has made cle ar, ADA and
Rehabilitation Act claims are not cognizable against state actors in their individual capacities. 30

capacities in certain limited circumstances, Plaintiff’s Second Amended Complaint does not
involve those circumstances.

C. Leave to Amend
For the reasons set forth above, the undersigned has recommended that Plaintiff’s  § 1983
claims against the OPDA Defendants in their individual capacities be dismissed on the basis of
absolute prosecutorial immunity.  Likewise, the § 1983 claims against these defendants in their
official capacities should be dismissed because Plaintiff does not allege that these Defendants are
final parish policymakers, nor does he identify any parish policy or custom that allegedly caused
the deprivation of his constitutional rights, and asserts claims based only on one past encounter .
Similarly, Fifth Circuit precedent is clear that Plaintiff cannot state a claim against the OPDA
Defendants in their individual capacities under either the ADA or the Rehabilitation Act of 1973.
Moreover, to the extent Plaintiff characterizes the ADA and Rehabilitation Act claims as against
these Defendants in their official capacities, he seeks mone tary damages rather than only
prospective injunctive relief; therefore, he does not fall within the Ex Parte Young exception.
Before dismissing a complaint, a court should give a pro se  plaintiff an opportunity to
amend to remedy any deficiencies.32  “In other words, leave to amend should be liberally granted,
when the plaintiff might be able to state a claim based on the underlying facts and
circumstances.”33  When leave to amend would be futile, however, dismissal is appropriate.34
  Amendment of Plaintiff’s § 1983 claims against the OPDA Defendants in their individual
capacities would be futile because the Defendants enjoy prosecutorial immunity from § 1983 suits
