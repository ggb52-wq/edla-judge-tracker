Before the C ourt is a Motion for Preliminary Injunction (R. Doc. 4) filed by Plaintiffs,
Revius and Valeria Norris, on behalf of their minor daughter, L.L.N . (collectively, “Plaintiffs”).
Plaintiffs seek preliminary injunctive relief prohibiting Defendant, the Jefferson Parish School
Board (“Defendant” or “the School Board”) from enforcing L.L.N.’s expulsion from Thomas
Jefferson High School after she allegedly brought a knife to school. R. Doc. 4; R. Doc. 4-1, pp. 1-

Court finds that Plaintiffs’ Motion should be DENIED for the reasons stated below.

I. BACKGROUND
This litigation arises from a March 2026 incident at Thomas Jefferson High School
(“Thomas Jefferson”) , in which a knife was discovered in the sports bag of fourteen- year-old
honors student L.L.N. during a routine security screening. R. Doc. 4 -1, pp. 2-3. According to
Plaintiffs, L.L.N.’s father had given her the knife for her protection while attending Mardi Gras
festivities. Id. at p. 3. She subsequently forgot that it remained in her sports bag and brought the
bag to school approximately one month later. Id. Plaintiffs maintain that L.L.N. never intended to
bring the knife to school nor to harm anyone. Id.
An expulsion hearing took place before the superintendent’s designee on April 2, 2026. R.
Doc. 8, p. 11. Plaintiffs’ account of that hearing reflects that L.L.N.’s father explained the
circumstances under which the knife ended up in her bag, and the principal presented L.L.N.’s
explanation that she had accidentally left the knife in the bag. R. Doc. 4-1, pp. 3, 11. The principal
also described L.L.N. favorably and stated that school officials did not believe she brought the
knife to campus with an intent to harm. Id. at 11.
The hearing officer’s April 8, 2026, decision imposed an expulsion for t wo complete
semesters, ending in May 2027, and provided for L.L.N. to continue her studies at Strehle

Board, and they contend that they appeared with witnesses a t the School Board’s May 6, 2026,

Board has since explained that its review was confined to the administrative record and that it
affirmed the decision through its consent agenda. R. Doc. 8, pp. 10-11. Plaintiffs clarify that their
present challenge concerns the constitutional adequacy of that review procedure, rather than

II. LAW AND ANALYSIS
“A preliminary injunction is an extraordinary remedy.” Atchafalaya Basinkeeper v. United
States Army Corps of Eng’rs, 894 F.3d 692, 696 (5th Cir. 2018). See also SO Apartments, L.L.C.
v. City of San Antonio, Texas , 109 F.4th 343, 348 (5th Cir. 2024) ; Bluefield Water Ass’n, Inc. v.
City of Starkville, Miss., 577 F.3d 250, 253 (5th Cir. 2009). Thus, courts do not grant relief unless
a plaintiff carries the onerous burden of demonstrating “(1) a substantial likelihood of success on
the merits, (2) a substantial threat of irreparable injury if the injunction is not issued, (3) that the
threatened injury if the injunction is denied outweighs any harm that will result if the injunction is
granted, and (4) that the grant of an injunction will not disserve the public interest.” Janvey v.
Alguire, 647 F.3d 585, 595 (5th Cir. 2011)  (quoting Byrum v. Landreth, 566 F.3d 442, 445 (5th
Cir. 2009)). Although the decision to grant or deny a preliminary injunction is within the sound
discretion of the district court, see Miss. Power & Light Co. v. United Gas Pipeline Co., 760 F.2d
618, 621 (5th Cir. 1985), the Fifth Circuit has “cautioned repeatedly that a preliminary injunction
. . . should not be granted unless the party seeking it has clearly carried the burden of persuasion
on all four requirements.” Bluefield, 577 F.3d at 253 (quotation and citation omitted). T he Fifth
Circuit “has applied a ‘ sliding-scale’ analysis to the four preliminary injunction requirements,”
under which a strong showing on the other three factors may justify temporary injunctive relief
upon “a showing of some  likelihood of success on the merits.” TitleMax of Texas, Inc. v. City of
Dallas, 142 F.4th 322, 328 (5th Cir. 2025) (emphasis added). However, it has also cautioned that
a mandatory preliminary injunction “should not be granted except in the rare instances in which
the facts and law are clearly in favor of the moving party.” Id.
 Here, Plaintiffs have not demonstrated that this is one of those rare instances. Plaintiff s
seek preliminary injunctive relief prohibiting the School Board from (1) enforcing L.L.N.’s

expulsion from Thomas Jefferson, (2) excluding her from Thomas Jefferson, (3) requiring her to
attend an alternative school, and (4) maintaining a weapons -related disciplinary offense on her
educational record. R. Doc. 4- 1, p. 1. They argue that absent such relief, “L.L.N. will suffer
ongoing violations of her constitutional rights, together with immediate and irreparable
educational, reputational, and emotional harm for which there is no adequate remedy at law.” Id.
Although Plaintiffs allege that each of the Rule 65 factors is satisfied, see R. Doc. 4-1, pp.

they have not demonstrated irreparable harm or a likelihood of success on the merits. R. Doc. 8,
pp. 2, 12-25. The Court addresses the parties’ arguments concerning satisfaction of the  necessary
elements for a preliminary injunction below.
A. Substantial Likelihood of Success
Plaintiffs contend that L.L.N.’s expulsion violated her rights to procedural and substantive
due process, deprived her of a liberty interest in her reputation, and interfered with her parents’
right to direct her upbringing. R. Doc. 4- 1, pp. 9-15. For the reasons below, the Court finds t hat
Plaintiffs have not shown a substantial likelihood of success on any asserted claim.
i. Protected Educational Interest
A student’s entitlement to public education may constitute a property interest protected by
the Due Process Clause of the United States Constitution. Goss v. Lopez , 419 U.S. 565, 573- 74
(1975). The existence and scope of that entitlement depend on state law. Board of Regents v. Roth,
408 U.S. 564, 577 (1972).
The Fifth Circuit distinguishes exclusion from a particular school from total exclusion from
public education, and a transfer to an alternative educational program does not ordinarily deprive
a student of her  protected entitlement to public education. See, e.g. , Nevares v. San Marcos

6

Consolidated Independent School District , 111 F.3d 25, 26- 27 (5th Cir. 1997). In Swindle v.
Livingston Parish School Board, the Fifth Circuit applied that distinction to Louisiana’s statutory
scheme and recognized a protected entitlement to alternative education during an expulsion. 655
F.3d 386, 394–95 (5th Cir. 2011). More recently, in James v. Cleveland School District, the Fifth
Circuit reaffirmed that particular components of an education, including class ranking and course-
related benefits, do not themselves establish a protected property interest. 45 F.4th 860, 865- 66,
865 n.2 (5th Cir. 2022).
Here, L.L.N. remains eligible to receive public education at Strehle . R. Doc. 8, p. 12.
L.L.N.’s asserted loss of honors courses and other opportunities at Thomas Jefferson do not,
standing alone, establish a deprivation of a constitutionally protected property interest. Although
Louisiana law requires alternative  schoolwork to be aligned with a  student’s former curriculum,
see La. R.S. 17:416(A)(3)(e), Plaintiffs have not shown that this requirement guarantees continued
enrollment at Thomas Jefferson or identical course offerings. Moreover, even if Plaintiffs could
demonstrate deprivation of a protected educational interest, they have not shown the requisite
likelihood of success on their contention that the procedures afforded L.L.N. were constitutionally
inadequate. Thus, Plaintiffs have not shown a substantial likelihood of success on their property-
based claims.
ii. Procedural Due Process
Procedural due process requires notice and a meaningful opportunity to respond to the
charged conduct. E.g., Goss, 419 U.S. at 579. The process required depends on the circumstances,
and longer exclusions may require more formal procedures than the brief suspensions the Goss
Court considered. Id. at 584; Brewer v. Austin Independent School District, 779 F.2d 260, 262-63
(5th Cir. 1985). Nevertheless, an adequate initial hearing does not create a constitutional

7

entitlement to another evidentiary hearing on appeal. Brewer, 779 F.2d at 263- 64 (holding that,
where the initial hearing afforded due process, the reviewing official was not required to repeat it
because “[d]ue process need only be provided once” and “is not undone by appellate procedures
that do not independently provide all the elements of due process”).
The present record reflects that Plaintiffs received an initial hearing before the
superintendent’s designee, the official authorized to determine whether expulsion or other
discipline was warranted. See R. Doc. 8, p. 11; La. R.S. 17:416(C)(1). Plaintiffs’ own submissions
reflect that the hearing included their explanation of the knife’s presence, the father’s testimony,
and the principal’s favorable account of L.L.N.’s character and lack of harmful intent. R. Doc. 4-
1, pp. 3, 11. The hearing officer then issued a written decision addressing the evidence and
imposing the sanction. R. Doc. 8, p. 12.
Plaintiffs argue that officials refused to consider the discretion available under Louisiana
law. R. Doc. 4-1, pp. 11-12. That contention warrants consideration because the Fifth Circuit under
Brewer does not permit the initial decisionmaker simply to accept the accuser’s recommended
punishment automatically. 779 F.2d at 263–64. The materials cited by Plaintiffs , however, show
that mitigating circumstances were presented to the hearing officer.  R. Doc. 4-1, pp. 3, 11. The
principal’s statements about the statutory penalty included stating “the superintendent may modify
the length of such minimum expulsion requirements on a case-by-case basis,” R. Doc. 4-4, p. 2,
confirming the hearing officer knew modification of the penalty was permitted, and the decision
to impose the statutory minimum does not itself demonstrate that the hearing officer refused to
consider a lesser sanction. On this showing, Plaintiffs have not established a likely procedural
violation in the initial hearing.

Plaintiffs’ principal remaining objection is that the School Board, as the final
administrative decisionmaker, did not permit them to present their case again. R. Doc. 11, p. 3.
Brewer forecloses the premise that constitutionally adequate process must be repeated at every
level of administrative review. 779 F.2d at 263 -64. The fact that the Board could affirm, modify,
or reverse the decision does not independently require a second evidentiary hearing. Nor does the
availability of such hearings in other circumstances establish that the record-review procedure used
here violated due process.
Plaintiffs’ reliance on Doe v. Rector & Visitors of George Mason University, 149 F. Supp.
3d 602 (E.D. Va. 2016), does not change this conclusion. Doe involved a student who was deprived
of notice of the specific charges against him, denying him  an adequate opportunity to answer. Id.
at 615-20. Here, Plaintiffs challenge the Board’s refusal to receive additional presentations after
the initial hearing . Unlike the student in Doe , Plaintiffs have not identified a comparable new
charge or evidentiary basis introduced on appeal.  Thus, Plaintiffs have not shown a substantial
likelihood of success on their procedural due process claim.
iii. Substantive Due Process and Statutory Discretion
Louisiana law provides a minimum expulsion of two complete school semesters for a
student in grades six through twelve found guilty of possessing a knife with a blade of at least two
and one-half inches on school property, while authorizing the superintendent to modify that period
on a case-by-case basis in writing. La. R.S. 17:416(C)(2)(a)(i). The Board may also affirm, modify,
or reverse the superintendent’s decision. Id. § 17:416(C)(4).
The existence of discretion, however, does not establish a federal constitutional right to its
exercise in Plaintiffs’ favor. Even assuming a protected interest, substantive due process review of
school discipline is available only in a rare case where a “rational relationship between the

9

punishment and the offense ” is lacking. Brewer, 779 F.2d at 264 (quoting Mitchell v. Board of
Trustees, 625 F.2d 660, 664 n.8 (5th Cir. 1980)).
Plaintiffs principally rely on Seal v. Morgan, 229 F.3d 567 (6th Cir. 2000), a Sixth Circuit
case which held that expulsion for weapons possession without knowledge of the weapon’s
presence would lack a rational relationship to a legitimate state interest. Id. at 575-76. That decision
is not binding on this Court. To the contrary, district courts within the Fifth Circuit have upheld
discipline where a student allegedly mistakenly brought a weapon to campus. See Bundick v. Bay
City Independent School District , 140 F. Supp. 2d 735, 740- 41 (S.D. Tex. 2001)  (upholding
discipline where student previously placed a machete in his vehicle and asserted that he had
forgotten it remained there).
For purposes of this Motion, the Court accepts Plaintiffs’ account that L.L.N. forgot the
knife remained in her bag and that she had no intention of bringing it to school or harming anyone.
But Plaintiffs’ account also establishes that L.L.N. had previously possessed the knife for personal
protection and later brought the bag containing it to school. R. Doc. 4- 1, p. 3. Requiring students
to exercise care concerning weapons previously placed in their belongings bears a rational
relationship to school safety, even when a particular student lacks harmful intent. The Southern
District of Texas’s decision in Bundick to uphold student discipline where a student allegedly
forgot a weapon was in his vehicle provides persuasive support for that conclusion.
The sanction of expulsion is substantial, and the mitigating circumstances could support a
different disciplinary judgment. The constitutional inquiry, however, does not authorize the Court
to substitute its preferred sanction merely because it would weigh those circumstances differently.
“It is not the role of the federal courts to set aside decisions of school administrators which the

Court may view as lacking a basis in wisdom or compassion.” Wood v. Strickland, 420 U.S. 308,
326 (1975). The Wood Court further stated:
[Section] 1983 does not extend the right to relitigate in federal court
evidentiary questions arising in school disciplinary proceedings or the
proper construction of school regulations. The system of public education
that has evolved in this Nation relies necessarily upon the discretion and
judgment of school administrators and school board members and § 1983
was not intended to be a vehicle for federal court correction of errors in the
exercise of that discretion which do not rise to the level of violations of
specific constitutional guarantees.

Plaintiffs have not shown that imposing the statutory minimum, while making alternative
education available, likely crossed the narrow constitutional boundary recognized in Brewer. This
conclusion does not establish a categorical rule that knowledge is irrelevant to every weapons -
possession case.
iv. Reputational and Parental Interests
Plaintiffs also emphasize the stigma of a weapons -related charge on L.L.N.’s disciplinary
record. R. Doc. 4-1, p. 14; R. Doc. 11, p. 3. The Court’s property-interest analysis does not dispose
of that separate contention: Goss recognizes that school discipline may implicate a student’s liberty
interest in reputation. 419 U.S. at 574–75. Even assuming that interest is implicated here, Plaintiffs
have not shown that L.L.N. lacked a meaningful opportunity to contest the charge and explain the
circumstances giving rise to the incident. The same hearing addressed the underlying incident and
L.L.N.’s asserted absence of intent. Plaintiffs have therefore not shown a likely deprivation of any
liberty interest in reputation without due process.
Finally, a parent’s liberty to direct their child’s upbringing and education does not give
them an unrestricted right to prevent the application of reasonable public-school rules. See Pierce
v. Society of Sisters , 268 U.S. 510, 534–35 (1925);  Littlefield v. Forney Independent School

District, 268 F.3d 275, 288–91 (5th Cir. 2001). The challenged action concerns the knife’s
presence at school, rather than the parents’ decision to provide it for use elsewhere. Plaintiffs have
not shown that applying a school weapons restriction in these circumstances lacks a rational
relationship to the School Board’s  safety interests.  Accordingly, Plaintiffs have not shown a
substantial likelihood of success on the merits of their claims.
B. Remaining Injunction Factors and Evidentiary Hearing
Having found that Plaintiffs have not shown a substantial likelihood of success on the
merits, the Court need not address the remaining preliminary injunction factors. Accordingly,
IT IS ORDERED that the Motion for Preliminary Injunction (R. Doc. 4) filed by
Plaintiffs, Revius Norris and Valeria Norris, on behalf of their minor child, L.L.N., is DENIED.
