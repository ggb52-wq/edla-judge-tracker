must possess a “substantial reason” to deny leave to amend. 26 The “substantial
reason” standard is a high bar that is only met when, for instance, a plaintiff has
acted with “undue delay, bad faith or dilatory motive” in seeking leave to amend, the
plaintiff has made “repeated failures to cure deficiencies by amendm ents previously
allowed,” “undue prejudice [will result] to the opposing party by virtue of allowance
of the amendment,” or the amendment would be completely futile.27
 The Court finds that Stubbs presents  “potentially curative facts” 28 and thus
there is not a substantial reason to deny his request to amend. Moreover, Stubbs’s
pro se pleadings are held to “less stringent standards than formal pleadings drafted
by lawyers.” 29 The Court should be “appropriately lenient” with pro se parties. 30
Because Stubbs identifies areas of his first amended complaint that he can potentially
adequately address, the Court finds that Defendants will not suffer undue prejudice
at this stage of the pleadings.  The Court also cannot conclude that a second
amendment would be futile, as it is possible Stubbs’s amended allegation s will cure
defects and adequately state claims upon which relief may be granted.
