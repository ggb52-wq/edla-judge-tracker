II. LAW AND ANALYSIS
A. Legal Standard
Rule 8 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure requires a complaint to contain “a short and
plain statement of the claim showing that the pleader is entitled to relief.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 8(a)(2).
This standard does not require “detailed factual allegations” but does require more than a recitation
of the elements of a claim in the form of conclusory statements. Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662,
663 (2009) (citing Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly, 550 U.S. 544, 555 (2007)). A plaintiff’s complaint
may survive a Rule 12(b)(6) motion to dismiss where the complaint contains sufficient facts, which
accepted as true, “state a claim to relief that is plausible on its face.” Id. (citing Bell Atl. Corp. v.
Twombly, 550 U.S. 544, 555 (2007)).
In deciding a motion to dismiss, the court is restricted and can generally only consider the
face of the pleadings. Scanlan v. Texas A&M Univ., 343 F.3d 533, 536 (5th Cir. 2003). However,
contracts that are attached to a motion to dismiss may be considered part of the pleadings if they
were referenced in the plaintiff’s complaint and are central to the claim. In re Katrina Canal
Breaches Litig., 495 F.3d 191, 205 (5th Cir. 2007). Motions to dismiss for failure to state a claim
are disfavored. Scanlan, 343 F.3d at 536.

1. Economic Damages  - Gross Negligence
Anadarko attempts to save its economic damages claim by arguing that MPOG was grossly
negligent. Texas law prohibits pre-injury waivers of liability arising from gross negligence. Zachry
Const. Corp. v. Port of Houston Auth of Harris Cnty ., 449 S.W. 3d 98 (Tex. 2014) (“We have
indicated that pre-injury waivers of future liability for gross negligence are void as against public
policy. Generally, a contractual provision ‘exempting a party from tort liability for harm caused
intentionally…is unenforceable on grounds of public policy.’ We think the same may be said of
contract liability.”). The Court now determines whether Anadarko adequately ple aded gross
negligence in its complaint.
Under Texas Law, gross negligence has an objective and a subjective component. Medina
v. Zuniga, 593 S.W.3d 238, 247 (Tex. 2019).1 The first objective prong is that the act or omission
must involve an extreme degree of risk. Id. To establish an extreme risk, there must be a likelihood
of serious injury to the plaintiff. Mobil Oil Corp. v. Ellender , 968 S.W.2d 917, 921 (Tex. 1998).
The second subjective prong is that the defendant must have subjective awareness of that risk and
proceed regardless with conscious indifference to the rights, safety, or welfare of others. Id. The
second, subjective element of awareness “means that the defendant knew about the peril, but its
acts or omissions demonstrated that it did not care.” Id.

Contrary to MPOG’s arguments, Anadarko has plead ed both prongs of a gross negligence
claim. Anadarko pleaded that the likelihood of substantial injury was high due to the nature of the
oil release event. Rec. Doc. 37 at 5. Anadarko has also alleged facts that indicate MPOG was aware
of the risk and proceeded with conscious indifference by continuing to operate its business during
the oil release event . Id. at 5-6. Anadarko has also alleged acts which could indicate MPOG’s
conscious indifference to Anadarko’s rights, including MPOG’s failure to inform Anadarko that
anything was wrong during the oil release event. Id. More specifically, Anadarko has alleged that
(1) the lack of a leak detection system increased the likelihood of an oil release event which would
cause serious injury to Anadarko, (2) MPOG knew sea floor movement posed a risk to its pipelines
yet nevertheless utilized an ineffective pipeline integrity program, (3) MPOG proceeded with its
business despite these failures, and (4) MPOG failed to inform Anadarko that anything was amiss
during the oil release event. Rec. Doc. 38 at 3-4. After review of the complaint, the Court concludes
that Anadarko has adequately ple aded MPOG’s gross negligence. Therefore, because Texas law
prohibits pre -injury waivers of liability for gross negligence, Anadarko’s claims for economic
damages are not barred under the contracts between the parties. See Zachry, 449 S.W.3d at 116;
Hill v. Fitness Int'l, LLC , No. 02-22-00142- CV, 2023 WL 2607646 at *8 (Tex. App. Mar. 23,
2023).
2. Economic Damages – Force Majeure
Also at issue is whether the force majeure provisions in the contracts between the parties
bar recovery for economic damages. MPOG argues that the force majeure provision s in the
contracts bar recovery  because pipeline breakage is a force majeure event that excuses non -
performance. Rec. Doc. 27- 1 at 6. MPOG continues that Anadarko has already alleged that the
cause of the oil release event was the failure of the Subsea 7 Connector; in other words, the cause
was an event included within the parties’ force majeure provisions. Id. This argument fails. The

machinery or lines of pipe, . . . or any other causes not r easonably within the control of the Party

3. Economic Damages – Causation
MPOG alternatively argues that Anadarko’s claim for economic damages is too vague and
not causally linked to the oil release event to the level required under OPA. Rec. Doc. 27-1 at 7-8.
In its complaint, Anadarko pleads that (1) it was required to shut in the Marlin and Horn Mountain
platforms and was unable to produce oil and lost profits as a result, and (2) that it incurred costs to
man and maintain both platforms during the shut-in. Rec. Doc. 37 at 13. MPOG argues that
Anadarko pled only categories of damages, but Anadarko’s pleadings go beyond this, specifically
mentioning costs to maintain platforms and lost profits due to the shut-in. The causal link between
the incident and injury meets the standard required under the OPA. In In re Deepwater Horizon,

4. Removal Costs under OPA
Anadarko’s claim for removal costs under OPA must follow certain requirements to be
recoverable. Under the  OPA, recoverable removal costs are those that are consistent with the
National Contingency Plan. 33 U.S.C.A. § 2702(b)(1)(B) (“Covered removal costs . . . any removal
costs incurred by any person for acts taken by the person which are consistent with the National
Contingency Plan.”). While Anadarko has pled that it mobilized helicopters and vessels to assist
MPOG and the FOSC to conduct inspections along the pipeline and located the leak, Anadarko
has not pled that its actions were consistent with the NCP. Rec. Doc. 37 at 15. As this is a
requirement for removal costs to be recoverable under OPA, MPOG’s motion to
dismiss Anadarko’s claim for removal costs under OPA will b e gr anted unless Anadarko
files an amended complaint correcting the foregoing deficiency  relating to compliance with
the National Contingency Plan (“NCP”) within 14 days of entry of this order.
