Background
 The plaintiffs in this lawsuit are a church and two environmental justice organizations:
Mount Triumph Baptist Church, Inclusive Louisiana, and RISE St. James. They allege that “[t]he
Black population makes up a total of 50% of St. James Parish as a whole, while the 4th and 5th
Districts of St. James Parish – where petrochemical sites are most concentrated – are
overwhelmingly majority Black districts: the 5th District is 89% Black pop ulation and the 4th
District is 52% Black.” Rec. Doc. 104, at 12. Plaintiffs initiated this civil rights action on March
21, 2023, against St. James Parish, the St. James Parish Council, and the St. James Parish Planning
Commission (collectively, the “Parish”) , to challenge the allegedly discriminatory siting of
industrial facilities in the majority Black 4th and 5th Districts . Rec. Doc. 1. Plaintiffs filed their
First Amended Complaint in July 2023. Rec. Doc. 29.
In November 2023, t he District Court dismiss ed Plaintiffs’ claims under the “substantial
burden” provision of the Religious Land Use and Institutionalized Persons Act (“RLUIPA”)  and
under the Louisiana Constitution, Article XII, Section 4, which recognizes “the right of the people
to preserve, foster, and promote their respective historic linguistic and cultural origins” for lack of
standing. The Court also dismissed as prescribed Plaintiffs’ remaining claims under the Thirteenth
and Fourteenth Amendment s of the United States Constitution, 42 U.S.C. § 1982, and the
RLUIPA’s religious discrimination provision.
The Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals reversed, finding that the Plaintiffs had alleged
discriminatory acts that fell within the applicable limitations period and that the Plaintiffs have
standing because they have pleaded cognizable property injuries traceab le to the conduct of the
Parish and stigmatic injury sufficient for Article III adjudication. Inclusive Louisiana v. St. James

Law and Analysis
1. Scope of Discovery
The Federal Rules of Civil Procedure provide that “parties may obtain discovery regarding
any nonprivileged matter that is relevant to any party's claim or defense and proportional to the
needs of the case.” Fed. R. Civ. Proc. 26(b)(1). “Information within this scope of discovery need
not be admissible in evidence to be discoverable.” Id.
“[I]information is relevant if it ‘bears on, or that reasonably could lead to other matters that
could bear on, any issue related to the claim or defense of any party.’” Leonard v. Martin, 38 F.4th

Relevant information falls within the scope of discovery only if it is proportional to the

discovery in resolving the issues, and whether the burden or expense of the proposed discovery
outweighs its likely benefit.” Fed. R. Civ. Proc. 26(b)(1).
2. Plaintiff’ s Motion to Compel Redistricting Information
a. Relevance – Arlington Height Factors
During oral argument, the plaintiffs invoked the Arlington Heights factors to argue that the
discovery they seek is relevant to their Equal Protection claim . To prove an Equal Protection
violation, plaintiffs must show a “racially discriminatory intent or purpose.” Vill. of Arlington
Heights v. Metro. Hous. Dev. Corp., 429 U.S. 252, 265 (1977). Of course, this can be difficult, and
discriminatory impact alone is rarely sufficient. See id. at 266. In Arlington Heights, the Supreme
Court offered a non-exclusive list of factors courts should consider in addition to the discriminatory
impact:  “ historical background of the decision ”, “specific sequence of events leading up to the
challenged decision” , “[d]epartures from the normal procedural sequence”, “[s]ubstantive
departures”,  and “legislative or administrative history.” Id. at 267.

Plaintiffs’ pattern and practice argument must be rejected because this lawsuit concerns
allegedly discriminatory land use decisions, not allegedly discriminatory redistricting decisions.
The only “pattern s” alleged in the operative complaint concern land use decisions .5 Had the
redistricting decision been part of the discriminatory pattern of decision making  alleged in the
complaint, plaintiff s could have included those allegations. Indeed, t he redistricting plan had
already been adopted six weeks before the original complaint was filed, and founding members of
plaintiff Inclusive Louisiana were present at the September 2022 public hearing on the redistricting
plan. Moreover, plaintiffs have amended their complaint twice in the intervening two and a half
years without incorporating any allegations about patterns of decision making in redistricting.6
This weighs against finding redistricting information relevant to plaintiffs’ claims.

3. The Parish’ s Motions to Compel
The Parish’s motions to compel concern member identity, member property value, member

a. First Amendment and Discovery
Among other things, plaintiffs argue that the discovery  sought by the Parish  is barred by
the First Amendment. Compelled disclosure of membership information “ can seriously infringe
on privacy of association and belief guaranteed by the First Amendment.” Hastings v. N. E. Indep.
Sch. Dist., 615 F.2d 628, 631 (5th Cir. 1980)  (quoting Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1, 64 (1976) ).
Thus, when “ constitutionally protected rights are infringed by the disclosure order, the party
seeking discovery must demonstrate ‘an interest in obtaining the disclosures . . . which is sufficient
to justify the deterrent effect which . . . these disclosures may well have.’” Id. (quoting Nat'l Ass'n
for Advancement of Colored People v. State of Ala. ex rel. Patterson, 357 U.S. 449, 463 (1958)).
The Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals has adopted a two-part framework for assessing a claim
of First Amendment privilege. 13 Perry v. Schwarzenegger, 591 F.3d 1147, 1160 (9th Cir. 2010) .
First, the party asserting the privilege must make a “ prima facie showing of arguable first
amendment infringement.” Id.  (quoting Brock v. Loc. 375, Plumbers Int'l Union of Am., AFL -
CIO, 860 F.2d 346, 349 (9th Cir. 1988)). This means the party must “demonstrate that [compelled
disclosure] will result in (1) harassment, membership withdrawal, or discouragement of new
members, or (2) other consequences which objectively suggest an impact on, or ‘chilling’ of, the
members' associational rights. ” Brock, 860 F.2d at 350 (quoting O'Neal v. United States , 601 F.

13 District Courts in the Fifth Circuit have used this test, noting that the Fifth Circuit in Hastings, cited above, indicated
a balancing test would be appropriate. E.g., NetChoice v. Murrill, No. CV 25-231-JWD-RLB, 2025 WL 2656063, at
*10 (M.D. La. Sept. 16, 2025).

18

Supp. 874, 878 (N.D. Ind. 1985)). If a prima facie showing is made, “ the evidentiary burden will
then shift to the government ... [to] demonstrate that the information sought through the [discovery]
is rationally related to a compelling governmental interest ... [and] the ‘least restrictive means' of
obtaining the desired information.” Perry, 591 F.3d at 1161 (quoting Brock, 860 F.2d at 350).
b. Membership and Property Ownership Information
The Parish’s first motion implicates numerous interrogatories and requests for production.

As to identity of the members and the property they own, the Parish argued in its briefing
that the information is relevant to determining whether the plaintiffs actually have standing. At
oral argument, though, they conceded that plaintiffs can establish standing via just one injured

plan to rely on evidence of general decrease in property values near the industrial sites. In other
words, it submits that if certain members buck the trends that will purportedly be revealed by
plaintiffs’ general property value studies,14 the Parish should be allowed to present evidence that
some members of plaintiffs’ organizations experienced property value increases.

come into play. In any event, to the extent plaintiffs’ financial condition is later put at issue —
perhaps as a defense to an attorneys’ fee award—the Court can revisit the relevance of plaintiffs’
financial records for this purpose.
