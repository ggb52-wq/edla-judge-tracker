LEGAL STANDARD
Summary judgment is appropriate when “the pleadings, depositions, answers
to interrogatories, and admissions on file, together with the affidavits, if any, show
that there is no genuine issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is
entitled to a judgment as a matter of law.” Celotex Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317, 322
(1986) (quoting Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(c)); Little v. Liquid Air Corp. , 37 F.3d 1069, 1075
(5th Cir. 1994). When evaluating whether a dispute as to any material fact exists, a
court considers “all of the evidence in the record but refrain[s] from making credibility
determinations or weighing the evidence.” Delta & Pine Land Co. v. Nationwide
Agribusiness Ins. Co. , 530 F.3d 395, 398– 99 (5th Cir. 2008) (citations omitted). All
reasonable inferences are drawn in favor of the nonmoving party, but a party cannot
defeat summary judgment with “conclusory allegations” or “unsubstantiated
assertions.” Little, 37 F.3d at 1075 (citations omitted). To grant a motion for summary
judgment, a court ultimately must be satisfied that “a reasonable jury could not
return a verdict for the nonmoving party.” Delta, 530 F.3d at 399 (citing Anderson v.
Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 248 (1986)).

DISCUSSION
 First, Defendants maintain that Plaintiff B Y Equities, LLC’s motion for
summary judgment on their counterclaim is procedurally improper because the Court
stayed and administratively closed this matter on July 29, 2025. The Court issued
this stay in response to Defendant Eugene Oppman’s notice of filing bankruptcy. (Rec.
Doc. 178). Defendants urge the Court not to consider the merits of the instant motion
based on this automatic stay.
 Conversely, Plaintiff relies on the Fifth Circuit’s holding  in Matter of U.S.
Abatement Corp. that the “automatic stay of the Bankruptcy Code extends only to
actions ‘against the debtor.’” 39 F.3d 563, 568 (5th Cir. 1994) (quoting 11 U.S.C.
§ 362(a)). The court concluded by holding that “[i]f a debtor’s offensive claims are not
subject to the automatic stay, a fortiori a creditor’s motion to reinstate and seek
summary judgment of such non-stayed claims is not subject to the automatic stay.”
Id. Plaintiff is correct. Accordingly, the Court will lift the stay in this matter for the
limited purpose of considering Plaintiff’s motion for summary judgment on
Defendants’ counterclaim.
Next, Plaintiff argues that issue preclusion bars Defendants from pursuing
their counterclaim . Specifically, in their second amended answer, Defendants
asserted a counterclaim against Plaintiff for fraudulent inducement , forced default,
and violations of the Louisiana Unfair Trade Practices Act . Defendants had also
raised these issues (fraudulent inducement, forced default, and unfair trade
practices) in affirmative defenses.  The Court found, after granting additional

discovery, that Defendants’ evidence to support their affirmative defenses was
insufficient to overcome the D’Oench, Duhme  doctrine and thereby defeat summary
judgment. (Rec. Doc. 135, at 5).  Accordingly, the Court must determine whether its
prior judgment concerning the affirmative defenses precludes the issues raised in
Defendants’ counterclaim.
Federal common law determines the preclusive effect of a federal-court
judgment. Taylor v. Sturgell , 553 U.S. 880,  891 (2008) (citing Semtek Int ’l Inc. v.
Lockheed Martin Corp., 531 U.S. 497, 508 (2001) ); In re Par., 81 F.4th 403, 414 (5th
Cir. 2023). “As  a matter of federal common law, federal courts sitting in diversity
apply the preclusion law of the forum state unless it is incompatible with federal
interests.” Anderson v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A. , 953 F.3d 311, 314 (5th Cir. 2020)
(citing Semtek, 531 U.S. at 508); In re Par. , 81 F.4th at 414. Accordingly, Louisiana
law will apply in this case.
Louisiana Revised Statutes Section 13:4231 governs both claim and issue
preclusion. Concerning issue preclusion, also known as collateral estoppel, the
statute provides that a “judgment in favor of either the plaintiff or the defendant is
conclusive, in any subsequent action between them, with respect to any issue actually
litigated and determined if its determination was essential to that judgment. ” La.
Stat. Ann. § 13:4231. Under Louisiana law, the following four requirements must be
met for a judgment to have issue-preclusive effect: “(1) the parties must be identical;
(2) the issue to be precluded must be identical to that involved in the prior action;
(3) the issue must have been actually litigated; and (4) the determination of the issue

in the prior action must have been necessary to the resulting judgment.” In re Keaty,
397 F.3d 264, 270–71 (5th Cir. 2005) (citations omitted).
Here, Defendants argue that issue preclusion does not apply because (1) the
issues raised in Defendants’ counterclaim are not identical to issues that the Court
has already decided; (2) the issues raised in the counterclaim were not actually
litigated; and (3) no determination concerning fraud, interference, or unfair business
practices was essential to the Court’s prior judgment. The Court will address each of
these arguments in turn.
Defendants presented three affirmative defenses that relate to their
counterclaim. First, in the Sixth Affirmative Defense, Defendants claimed that “[a]ny
alleged default with respect to the notes sued on by Plaintiff in this proceeding were
caused not by any act or omission of these Defendants but rather by acts or omissions
of the Plaintiff and/or its predecessors in interest.” (Rec. Doc. 6, at 7). Next, in the
Thirteenth Affirmative Defense, which Defendants pleaded in their first amended
answer, Defendants alleged that former executives of FNBC, BY’s predecessor in
interest, made fraudulent misrepresentations that induced Defendants to sign the
note. (Rec. Doc. 20, at 3). Lastly, in the Fourteenth Affirmative Defense, Defendants
accused Plaintiff of acquiring the note “through fraud and conspiracy to commit fraud,
and/or violations of the Louisiana Unfair Trade Practices and Consumer Protection
Act.” (Rec. Doc. 130, at 2). When the Court granted summary judgment for Plaintiff
on all claims, it explicitly concluded that Defendants had not met their burden with

respect to their affirmative defenses , which the Court found were barred by the
D’Oench, Duhme doctrine.
Defendants’ counterclaim  accuses Plaintiff of fraud and violation of the
Louisiana Unfair Trade Practices Act. Specifically, Defendants alleged in their
counterclaim that “BY entered into a conspiracy to defraud and to in fact defraud
[Defendants] and, unknown to [Defendants], com pete with [Defendants] for
acquisition of the Note in question in this litigation.” (Rec. Doc. 130, at 3).
According to Defendants, the issues raised in the affirmative defenses are not
identical to those raised in the counterclaim. Defendants contend that the “prior
proceedings addressed ONLY whether Defendants’ allegations of fraud, interference,
and unfair conduct were sufficient to defeat enforcement of a promissory note.”
(Rec. Doc. 200, at 5). The counterclaim, they argue, “presents a distinct, new issue:
whether BY Equities is affirmatively liable for damages arising from that same
misconduct.” Id. Defendants also maintain that their counterclaim involves different
legal standards, different burdens of proof, and different forms of relief. However,
they do not explain how or why this is the case.
Concerning Defendants’ allegations that Plaintiff committed fraud,  for
example, Defendants would have to prove the same elements to prevail on either an
affirmative defense or a counterclaim. This Court already ruled, and the Fifth Circuit
affirmed, that Defendants did not present sufficient evidence to prove their
affirmative defenses and thereby defeat Plaintiff’s motion for summary judgment.
Defendants have not presented any new evidence concerning their counterclaim ,

which rests on the same allegations of “fraud, interference, and unfair conduct” that
composed Defendants’ affirmative defenses. Therefore, the Court finds that the issues
raised in the counterclaim are identical to those already addressed in the Court’s
previous order and judgment.
Next, Defendants assert that these issues were not actually litigated when the
Court and the Fifth Circuit reached decisions concerning Plaintiff’s motion for
summary judgment. Their opposition reads as follows: “The prior rulings make NO
factual findings that BY Equities did not engage in fraud, interference, or unfair trade
practices. Instead, the Fifth Circuit expressly framed its decision in terms of
evidentiary insufficiency, emphasizing the ‘absence of competent material evidence’
and characterizing D efendants’ position as ‘speculation.’” (Rec. Doc. 200, at 6).
Defendants claim that this alleged distinction is both “critical” and “dispositive.” Id.
However, the Court holds that it is a distinction without a difference. The Court does
not have to find that BY conclusively did not engage in fraud, interference, and unfair
trade practices in order to grant summary judgment on Defendants’ counterclaim .
That is not the legal standard or test at issue. Instead, Defendants bore the burden
to provide sufficient evidence that would indicate a genuine dispute of material fact
concerning BY’s alleged fraud, interference, and trade practices. They failed to do so,
and this failure to provide adequate evidence on their affirmative defenses clearly
demonstrates that they could not provide sufficient evidence on their counterclaim,
either.

Additionally, Defendants assert that with respect to their counterclaim, the
Fifth Circuit reached “a determination about the sufficiency of the record, not a
finding that the underlying facts do  not exist.” Id. at 2. They proceed to argue that
“the very evidentiary gap alluded to by the Fifth Circuit is the result of Defendants’
inability to conduct discovery, particularly in light of the bankruptcy stay.” Id.
However, according to the final scheduling order in this matter (Rec. Doc. 89), which
was issued on July 27, 2023, the Court ordered that discovery should be completed
not later than August 27, 2024. Moreover, the scheduling order provided that all pre-
trial motions should be filed and served in sufficient time to permit hearing thereon
no later than September 4, 2024. The Court granted Plaintiff’s motion for summary
judgment after this deadline on September 17, 2024.
Even more relevant, in November of 2020, the Court denied a motion for
summary judgment filed by Plaintiff based on the Court’s determination that
Defendants’ affirmative defense of fraudulent inducement would benefit from
additional discovery. (Rec. Doc. 31, at 7). Four years later, when the Court granted
Plaintiff’s motion for summary judgment, the Order and Reasons explicitly
referenced this previous order and concluded that the evidence Defendants obtained
through additional discovery was not sufficient “to surmount the D’Oench, Duhme
doctrine.” (Rec. Doc. 135, at 6). Although Defendants do not dispute that the D’Oench,
Duhme doctrine precluded their affirmative defenses, they suggest that the result
would be different for their counterclaim. This is simply not the case.  This doctrine
would apply equally to a counterclaim . Despite Defendants’ contention to the
