II.  LEGAL STANDARD
A. Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(1)
A party may move to dismiss a case for lack of subject -matter jurisdiction.
See FED. R. CIV. P. 12(b)(1). “If the court determines at any time that it lacks subject
matter jurisdiction, the court mu st dismiss the action.” FED. R. CIV. P. 12(h)(3). The
Court may assess subject -matter jurisdiction based on “(1) the complaint alone; (2)
the complaint supplemented by undisputed facts in the record; or (3) the complaint
supplemented by undisputed facts plus the [C]ourt’s resolution of disputed facts.”
Ramming v. United States , 281 F.3d 158, 161 (5th Cir. 2001) (internal citation
omitted). “The burden of proof for a Rule 12(b)(1) motion to dismiss is on the party
asserting jurisdiction,” so “the plaintiff c onstantly bears the burden of proof that
jurisdiction does in fact exist.” Id. (internal citations omitted).

B. Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6)
Rule 8(a)(2) requires “a short and plain statement of the claim showing that
the pleader is entitled to relief.” FED. R. CIV. P. 8(a)(2). A complaint that does not
satisfy Rule 8(a)(2)’s pleading standard fails to state a claim upon which relief can
be granted. See generally FED. R. CIV. P. 12(b)(6). “[T]he pleading standard Rule 8
announces does not requir e ‘detailed factual allegations,’ but it demands more than
an unadorned, the -defendant-unlawfully-harmed-me accusation.” Ashcroft v. Iqbal ,
556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009) (quoting Bell. Atl. Corp. v. Twombly , 550 U.S. 544, 555
(2007)). “A pleading that offers ‘labels and conclusions’ or ‘a formulaic recitations of
the elements of a cause of action will not do.’” Id. (quoting Twombly, 550 U.S. at
555). “Nor does a complaint suffice if it tenders ‘naked assertion[s]’ devoid of
‘further factual enhancement.’” Id. (quoting Twombly, 550 U.S. at 557).
“To survive a motion to dismiss” under Rule 12(b)(6), “a complaint must
contain sufficient factual matter, accepted as true, to ‘state a claim to relief that is
plausible on its face.’” Iqbal, 556 U.S. at 678 (quoting Twombly, 550 U.S. at 570). “A
claim has facial plausibility when the plaintiff pleads factual content that allows
the court to draw the reasonable inference that the defendant is liable for the
misconduct alleged.” Id. (citing Twombly, 550 U.S. at 556). “Alth ough ‘[courts]
accept all well -pled facts as true, construing all reasonable inferences in the
complaint in the light most favorable to the plaintiff, conclusory allegations
unwarranted factual inferences, or legal conclusions are not accepted as true.’”
Hodge v. Engleman , 90 F.4th 840, 843 (5th Cir. 2024) (quoting Allen v. Hays , 65
F.4th 736, 743 (5th Cir. 2023)).

C. Motion to Compel Arbitration
“The FAA was Congress’s way of responding to the general ‘hostility of
American courts to the enforcement of arbitr ation agreements.’” Lopez v. Cintas
Corp., 47 F.4th 428, 431 (5th Cir. 2022) (quoting Circuit City Stores, Inc. v. Adams ,
532 U.S. 105, 111 (2001)). “Contrary to that prior practice, the FAA establishes ‘a
liberal federal policy favoring arbitration agreements.’” Id. (quoting Epic Sys. Corp.
v. Lewis , 584 U.S. 497, 505 (2018)). “To that end, courts must ‘rigorously enforce
arbitration agreements according to their terms.’” Id. (quoting Am. Express Co. v.
Italian Colors Rest., 570 U.S. 228, 233 (2013)).
Section 3 of the FAA is straightforward: If “any issue” is “referable to
arbitration” under a written agreement to arbitrate, the Court “shall . . . stay the
trial of the action until such arbitration has been had in accordance with the terms
of the agreement[.]” 9 U.S.C. § 3. The “plain statutory text” of Section 3 of the FAA
“requires” the Court to “stay [this] proceeding” if “any issue” must be arbitrated.
Smith v. Spizzirri, 601 U.S. 472, 476 (2024) (citation omitted).
III.  ANALYSIS
A. Caulfield’s Rule 12(b)(1) Motion to Dismiss
Caulfield moves to dismiss D.R. Horton’s complaint under Fed. R. Civ. P.
12(b)(1) for lack of subject matter jurisdiction, contending there is no ripe
controversy between D.R. Horton and Caulfield. “[T] he district court must have
jurisdiction in the first instance to compel arbitration, and a ripe controversy is a
necessary component of subject matter jurisdiction. ” Lower Colorado River Auth. v.
Papalote Creek II, L.L.C. , 858 F.3d 916, 923 (5th Cir. 2017) . “As a general rule, an

actual con troversy exists where ‘a substantial controversy of sufficient immediacy
and reality [exists] between parties having adverse legal interests.’” Orix Credit
All., Inc. v. Wolfe, 212 F.3d 891, 896 (5th Cir. 2000). “[W]hether particular facts are
sufficiently immediate to establish an actual controversy yields answers on a case -
by-case basis.” Shields v. Norton, 289 F.3d 832, 835 (5th Cir. 2002).
Caulfield argues that because the state court has not yet ruled on h is motion
for leave to be added as a named co -plaintiff in Alicia R. Dixon and West J. Dixon v.
D.R. Horton, Inc. – Gulf Coast, et al. , No. 716640 , there is no live controversy , and
therefore no subject matter jurisdiction . The Court disagrees. “The threat of
litigation can establish a justiciable controversy if it is specific and concrete.”
Shields, 289 F.3d at 835  (citation omitted). Caulfield filed a motion for leave and
associated pleadings in state court seeking to be joined as an additional plaintiff
and asserting claims against D.R. Horton  arising out of h is contract with D.R.
Horton. The threat of litigation from Caulfield—who is actively seeking to join a live
controversy in state court against D.R. Horton and has filed pleadings —is
sufficiently “specific and concrete” to establish a controversy. See, e.g., Woodmen of
the World Life Ins. Soc ’y v. Mayo , 594 F. Supp. 3d 736, 741 (S.D. Miss. 2022)
(holding that the threat of litigation was “specific and concrete” where the plaintiff
was not yet a party to the underlying state court litigation but a  party had “made a
specific threat to join” it to the case); StrucSure Home Warranty, LLC v. Sulzbach ,
No. 4:20-CV-2915, 2021 WL 4240887, at *1 n.1 (S.D. Tex. Jan. 25, 2021) (granting
the motion to compel arbitration pursuant to 9 U.S.C. § 4, and finding controversy
Case 2:26-cv-00082-BSL-MBN     Document 31     Filed 09/30/26     Page 6 of 20
7

ripe because the defendant had initiated litigation against the plaintiff  in state
court in violation of arbitration clause); First Fam. Fin. Servs., Inc. v. Fairley, 173 F.
Supp. 2d 565, 572 (S.D. Miss. 2001) (“The Court cannot conceive of a mo re explicit
refusal to arbitrate than the bringing of an arbitrable claim in state court that one
has contractually agreed to arbitrate.”) ; see also Orix Credit All., Inc. v. Wolfe , 212
F.3d 891, 897 (5th Cir. 2000) (collecting cases).
Accordingly, the Court finds that the threat of litigation posed by Caulfield’s
filing of a motion for leave to join a state court action as an additional plaintiff and
associated pleadings creates a justiciable controversy. Caulfield’s motion to dismiss
for lack of subject matter jurisdiction is therefore denied.
B. Caulfield’s Rule 12(b)(6) Motion to Dismiss
Caulfield next argues that D.R. Horton’s complaint seeking to compel
arbitration pursuant to the FAA fails to state a claim upon which relief can be
granted because the FAA is not applicable. According to Caulfield, D.R. Horton’s
complaint fails to plead allegations sufficient to establish that the transaction at
issue (Caulfield’s purchase of a new home) involved interstate commerce.
Caulfield reads the statute—and the Contract—too narrowly. Section 2 of the
FAA covers arbitration provisions in “a contract evidencing a transaction involving
commerce to settle by arbitration.” 9 U.S.C. § 2. The Supreme Court interprets
“‘involving commerce’ in the FAA as the functional equivalent of the more familiar
term ‘affecting commerce ’—words of art that ordinarily signal the broadest
permissible exercise of Congress’ Commerce Clause power.” Citizens Bank v.
Alafabco, Inc., 539 U.S. 52, 56 (2003) (citing Allied-Bruce Terminix Cos. v. Dobson,
Case 2:26-cv-00082-BSL-MBN     Document 31     Filed 09/30/26     Page 7 of 20
8

513 U.S. 265, 273 –74 (1995)). “‘[E]nforcement of arbitration agreements [is thus]
within the full reach of the Commerce Clause, ’” which “encompasses a wider range
of transactions than those actually ‘in commerce ’—that is, ‘within the flow of
interstate commerce[.]’” Id. (citations omitted). As such, a party invoking the FAA
does not have to show its own contract had any “specific effect upon interstate
commerce.” Id. at 56-57. It is sufficient that “in the aggregate the economic activity
in question would represent ‘a general practice . . . subject to federal control.’” Id.
(quoting Mandeville Island Farms, Inc. v. Am. Crystal Sugar Co. , 334 U.S. 219, 236
(1948)).
Here, as alleged in the Complaint, “[t]he Contract and Arbitration Clause,
executed by and between a Delaware and Texas corporation and a Louisiana
citizen, . . . involv[es] the construction and agreement to purchase a home[.]”  The
relevant contract involves a non -Louisiana builder’s construction of a home in
Louisiana. “Citizens of differen t states engaged in performance of contractual
operations in one of those states are engaged in a contract involving commerce
under the FAA.” See Mesa Operating Ltd. P’ship v. Louisiana Intrastate Gas Corp. ,
797 F.2d 238, 243 (5th Cir. 1986), abrogated on other grounds sub nom. by Dr.
Kenneth Ford v. NYLCare Health Plans of Gulf Coast, Inc. , 141 F.3d 243 (5 th Cir.
1998). Caulfield and D.R. Horton’s contract thus involves commerce and necessarily
falls within the scope of Section 2.
Consider Allied-Bruce Terminix Cos. v. Dobson , 513 U.S. 265 (1995). There,
the Alabama Supreme Court held that the home -protection plan signed in Alabama

between Alabama homeowners and the local office of a pest control company
involved a transaction that was “primarily local and not ‘substantially’ interstate.”
513 U.S. at 268 -69 (observing that the pest control company was a multistate firm

Supreme Court reversed, holding that “‘involving,’ like ‘aff ecting,’ signals an intent
to exercise Congress’ commerce power to the full[,]” and that “even if the parties did

the transaction involved commerce. Id. at 277, 281 -82. Alafabco is in accord . The

broad. See id.

C. D.R. Horton’s Motion to Compel Arbitration
The Court next considers D.R. Horton’s motion to compel arbitration  under
the FAA, 9 U.S.C.§ 4.
Compelling arbitration under the FAA  proceeds in “two analytical steps.”
Kubala v. Supreme Prod. Servs., Inc., 830 F.3d 199, 201 (5th Cir. 2016). At step one,
the Court “appl[ies] state law to determine whether the parties formed ‘ any
arbitration agreement at all .’” Matter of Willis , 944 F.3d 577, 579 (5th Cir. 2019)
(italics omitted) (quoting Kubala, 830 F.3d at 201). At step two, the Court
“interpret[s] the contract ‘to determine whether this claim is covered by the
arbitration agreement.’” Id. (italics omitted) (quoting Kubala, 830 F.3d at 201). But
the step -two analysis changes if the arbitration agreement “delegates to ‘the
arbitrator the primary power to rule on the arbitra bility of a specific claim.’” Id.
(quoting Kubala, 830 F.3d at 201). “In such a case,” the Court “ask[s] only whether

there is a valid delegation clause.” Id. “If there is a delegation clause, the motion to
compel arbitration should be granted in almost all cases.” Kubala, 830 F.3d at 202.
1. The Arbitration Provision is a Valid Agreement to
Arbitrate.
Step one  of the two -part analysis  is satisfied because the arbitration
provision in the contract between D.R. Horton and Caulfield is a valid agreement to
arbitrate under Louisiana law.
In determining this first step —whether there is a valid agreement to
arbitrate—the Court considers whether the parties “entered into any arbitrati on
agreement at all.” Kubala, 830 F.3d at 201. This requires an analysis of the
“agreement’s existence rather than its validity.” Hill v. Jackson Offshore Holdings,
L.L.C., 175 F.4th 324, 33 9 (5th Cir. 2026) (Douglas, J., concurring) (citing , inter
alia, Arnold v. Homeaway, Inc. , 890 F.3d 546, 550 (5th Cir. 2018) (“In conducting
[an] inquiry [into whether the parties formed an arbitration agreement], we
distinguish between ‘validity’ or ‘enforceability’ challenges and ‘formation’ or
‘existence’ challenges.”)). “This formation versus validity distinction is critical to the
court’s inquiry because the court has authority and responsibility to decide the
matter when the very existence of a contract containing the relevant arbitration
agreement is called into question.” Id. (cleaned up).
The Court applies “ordinary principles of state contract law” to determine
whether a valid agreement to arbitrate exists. Halliburton Energy Servs., Inc. v.
Ironshore Specialty Ins. Co. , 921 F.3d 522, 530 (5th Cir. 2019)  (citations omitted).

to read and understand the contrac t before signing on to the delegation clause,
arguing that D.R. Horton “did not explain the delegation structure ” and stating in
his declaration that he “never saw anything about a delegation clause.” 20 But the
Fifth Circuit has rejected challenges to delegation clauses where the party merely
argues “that the contract as a whole (including its arbitration provision) is rendered
invalid, but [does] not make any arguments beyond attacking the Agreement
generally.” Hill, 175 F.4th at 334 ( compelling dispute to arbitration because the
plaintiff did not explain “ how he was fraudulently induced into agreeing to the
delegation clause specifically or how economic duress compelled him to agree to that
clause specifically ”); Brown v. Pacific Life Insurance Co. , 462 F.3d 384 (5th Cir.
2006) (holding that the parties did not specifically challenge the severable
arbitration agreement because their arguments did not “distinguish between their
attacks on the validity of the [broader] agreements and the arbitration clauses
