II. STANDARDS OF LAW
A. Rule 12(b)(6) Motion to Dismiss
Pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6), a district court may dismiss a complaint or part of
a complaint when a plaintiff fails to set forth well -pleaded factual allegations that
“raise a right to relief above the speculative level. ” See Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly ,
550 U.S. 544, 555 (2007); Cuvillier v. Taylor, 503 F.3d 397, 401 (5th Cir. 2007). The
complaint “must contain sufficient factual matter, accepted as true, to ‘state a claim
to relief that is plausible on its face. ’” Ashcroft v. Iqbal , 556 U.S. 662 , 678
(2009) (quoting Twombly, 550 U.S. at 547).

that allows the court to draw the reasonable inference that the defendant is liable for
the misconduct alleged.” Id. If the well-pleaded factual allegations “do not permit the
court to infer more than the mere possibility of misconduct,” then “the complaint has
alleged—but it has not ‘show[n]’—‘that the pleader is entitled to relief. ’” Id. at
679 (quoting Fed. R. Civ. P. 8(a)(2)) (alteration in original).
In assessing the complaint , a court must accept all well -pleaded facts as true

B. Federal Preemption
Federal preemption is derived from the Supremacy Clause of the United States
Constitution, which states: “This Constitution, and the Laws of the United States,
which shall be made in Pursuance thereof . . . shall be the supreme Law of the Land.”
U.S. Const. art. VI, § 2. Therefore, when a state law conflicts with a federal law, the
federal law preempts the state law and negates its effect. See Gade v. Nat ’l Solid
Wastes Mgmt. Ass ’n, 505 U.S. 88, 106 –07 (1992). Federal preemption is “either
express or implie d.” Id. at 98. Express preemption occurs when a federal statute
directly conflicts with state law. See id. Implied preemption  arises where “federal

The Supreme Court has found that, in many cases, products liability claims
arising from the use of medical devices covered by the Medical Devices Amendments
of 1976 ( “MDA”) to the Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act ( “FDCA”) are expressly
preempted by federal law . See Riegel v. Medtronic, Inc. , 552 U.S. 312, 330 (2008);
Medtronic, Inc. v. Lohr, 518 U.S. 470 (1996).
The MDA centralized and delegated the regulation of medical devices to the
FDA. Riegel, 552 U.S. at 315–16. To help the FDA maintain regulatory authority over
medical devices, Congress explicitly preempted state oversight by enacting 21 U.S.C.
§ 360k(a). Section 360k expressly preempts state-law tort claims for injury caused by
a medical device if “(1) the Federal Government has established requirements
applicable to [the device]; and (2) the claims are based on state-law requirements that
are different from, or in addition to, the federal ones, and that relate to safety and
effectiveness.” Bass v. Stryker Corp., 669 F.3d 501, 507 (5th Cir. 2012) (quoting Riegel,
552 U.S. at 321–22) (citation modified).
The Supreme Court has concluded that Class III devices that undergo the PMA
process satisfy the first prong of this analysis because the FDA requires them “to be
made with almost no deviations from the specifications in [the relevant] approval

to as § 510(k) approval , where the FDA reviews the device to determine if it is
“‘substantially equivalent’ to another device exempt from the PMA process.” Id. at
507. In contrast to the rigorous PMA process, the § 510(k) approval process does not
impose federal requirements on a device. Id. (citing Lohr, 518 U.S. at 493–94).
However, under certain circumstances, issues with devices that satisfy the first
prong of the Reigel preemption inquiry may still form the basis of a products liability
claim. When a manufacturer of such a device violates FDA regulations, a plaintiff
may recover under state tort law theories provided that those theories “parallel” the

federal violation. Riegel, 552 U.S. at 330. For a parallel claim, the state-law duty must
not impose any requirements that differ from or exceed  the FDA requirements. See
id. “Essentially, in a parallel claim, relief is sought under state law alleging that
certain conduct of the defendant resulted in a breach of a state duty, that this conduct
also resulted in a breach of an FDA -imposed federal regulation, and that certain
damages resulted from this breach. ” Chiasson v. Medtronic Inc , No. 16 -3552, 2016
WL 4191837 , at *4  (E.D. La. Aug. 9, 2016)  (Fallon, J.) . “If the [p]laintiff fails to
properly plead her parallel claim, that is , she pleads a violation of a state law that
imposes different or additional regulations . . . her claim will be preempted.” Id.
This parallel-claim exception to express § 360k preemption is narrow. See id.;
see also Bush v. Thoratec Corp., 837 F. Supp. 2d 603, 608 (E.D. La. 2011) (Fallon, J.)
(describing the act of pleading a viable parallel claim as “threading the needle”). Even
if the MDA does not expressly preempt a parallel state law claim , it may still be
impliedly preempted if it “exist[s] solely by virtue” of FDA regulations. Buckman, 531
U.S. at 352–53.
Congress granted the FDA the sole authority to enforce violations of the MDA.
See id. at 347–51. Because the federal government retains this exclusive authority,
private litigants cannot sue for noncompliance based solely on FDA regulations. Id.
at 353. Therefore, state law cannot give a plaintiff a right of action to enforce the
MDA. See id. Such claims “exist[] solely by virtue ” of the FDA ’s requirements, and
private litigants cannot step into a regulatory role explicitly given to the federal
government. Id. at 348 –51; see also In re Taxotere (Docetaxel) Products Liability

manufacturing process failed, or how it deviated from the FDA approved

B. Count Two – Unreasonably Dangerous in Design
To plead an unreasonably dangerous in design claim, plaintiffs must show that

existed; “and (2) [t]he likelihood that the product ’s design would cause [her injury]

Cir. 2014) (quoting La. Stat. § 9:2800.56). “Failure to connect an alleged violation of
federal design requirements to the LPLA claims is fatal to those claims.” See Reddick,
2021 WL 798294, at *8.

of inappropriate discharge ” and Norma ’s resulting injury.  Boiled down, plaintiffs

design of the Class III FDA-approved [LifeVest].” Id.
Accordingly, as § 360k preempts plaintiffs’ unreasonably dangerous in design
claim, and as previously stated, because plaintiffs have amended their complaint once
and have not requested to do so again, count two will be dismissed with prejudice.
C. Count Three – Unreasonably Dangerous Because of Inadequate
Warning
To plausibly allege a claim for inadequate warning, plaintiffs must plead facts
showing that (1) Norma’s LifeVest “was unreasonably dangerous because an
adequate warning was not provided for a potentially dangerous characteristic
existing at the time the product left [Zoll’s] control;” (2) Zoll “failed to use reasonable
care to provide an adequate warning of such characteristic; ” and (3) “these failures
deviated from FDA warning requirements.” Reddick, 2021 WL 798294, at *9 (quoting
La. Rev. Stat. Ann. § 9:2800.57) (citing Gomez, 442 F.3d at 930).

Additionally, with respect to a claim that a medical device is unreasonably
dangerous due to inadequate warning, Louisiana applies the learned intermediary
doctrine. See Willett v. Baxter Int’l, Inc., 929 F.2d 1094, 1098–99 (5th Cir. 1991). The
learned intermediary doctrine provides that a manufacturer ’s obligation to
consumers is “fulfilled when the . . . treating physician is informed of any potential
side effects or risks from the [device’s] use so that they may intelligently decide on its
use and advise the patient.” Stahl v. Novartis Pharms. Corp., 283 F.3d 254, 267 (5th
Cir. 2002) (quoting McCarthy v. Danek Med., Inc., 65 F. Supp. 2d 410, 413 (E.D. La.
1999) (Lemelle, J.)). “The premise underlying a failure-to-warn claim in the learned
intermediary context is that the patient is claiming that the manufacturer failed to
adequately warn the treating physician.” Id. at 268 (emphasis retained).
In such circumstances, a medical device may be unreasonably dangerous
because of an inadequate warning if: (1) the defendant failed to warn
the plaintiff ’s doctor of a risk associated with the product that was
otherwise unknown to the doctor; and (2) the failure to warn the doctor
was both a cause in fact and the proximate cause of the plaintiff’s injury
. . . To prove causation, the plaintiff must show that “a proper warning
would have changed the decision of the treating physician,  i.e. that but
for the inadequate warning, the treating physician would not have used
. . . the product.”
Celino v. Biotronik, Inc., 536 F. Supp. 3d 89 (E.D. La. 2021) (Africk, J.) (citing Willett,
929 F.2d at 1098–99).

additional monitoring or safeguards during use ,” the “specific circumstances or
activities that increase the risk of malfunction,” “the violent physical force of

D. Count Four – Failure to Conform with an Express Warranty

caused by the breach because the express warranty was untrue .” Reddick, 2021 WL
798294, at *10 (citing La. Rev. Stat. Ann. § 9:2800.58).
