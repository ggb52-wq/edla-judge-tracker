and Assistant District Attorney Kellie Rish (“ADA Rish” or “Defendant Rish”). Id. at 5-10.

II. Standard of Review
The Louisiana Constitution Article V, Section 34, states that the secrecy of grand jury
proceedings shall be provided by law. La. Const. Ann. art. V, § 34. Further, Article 434(A) of the
Louisiana Code of Criminal Procedure requires “[m]embers of the grand jury, all other persons
present at a grand jury meeting, and all persons having confidential access to information
concerning grand jury proceedings” to “keep secret the testimony of witnesses and all other matters
occurring at, or directly connected with, a meeting of the grand jury.” LA. CODE CRIM. PROC. ANN.
art. 434(A).
“It is a long established policy that the secrecy of grand jury proceedings should be
carefully maintained.” State v. Trosclair , 443 So.2d 1098, 1102 (La. 1983). However, there are
circumstances where justice may demand release of portions of such proceedings. Id. (quoting
Douglas Oil Co. v. Petrol Stops Northwest, 441 U.S. 211, 219-20 (1979)).
In determining whether to permit discovery of grand jury transcripts, the Supreme Court
of Louisiana has adopted the approach used by the federal courts. In re Grand Jury , 737 So.2d 1,
5 (La. 1999). This includes the United States Supreme Court ’s conclusion in Douglas Oil Co. v.
Petrol Stops Northwest , 441 U.S. at 226, that “in general, requests for disclosure of grand jury
transcripts should be directed to the court that supervised the grand jury’s activities.” However,
this does not mean that the court which presided over the grand jury proceedings should make the
final determination. Id.
Federal courts have the discretion to order release of state grand jury materials  upon a
showing of compelling necessity for the materials. See Thibodeaux v. Normand, No. 13- 5903,
2016 WL 7033674, at *6-7 (E.D. La. Dec. 1, 2016). However, comity dictates that federal courts
should defer action on disclosure requests for state grand jury materials until the presiding state

court has considered the request and ruled on the continuing need for secrecy. In re Grand Jury ,
737 So.2d at 9-10 (quoting Socialist Workers Party v. Grubisic, 619 F.2d 641 (7th Cir. 1980)); see
also Douglas Oil Co., 441 U.S. at 225- 26. Thus, r equests for disclosure of state grand jury
materials by government officials and private litigants should first be directed to the state court
which presided over the criminal proceedings for an evaluation of the continued need for secrecy.
In re Grand Jury, 737 So.2d at 10.

III. Analysis
Defendants DA Connick and ADA Rish seek to quash Plaintiff’s April 17, 2026, subpoena
duces tecum in part because Plaintiff failed to first seek release of the grand jury materials from
the 24th Judicial District Court, the court which presided over the criminal proceedings for which
Plaintiff was later acquitted. R. Doc. 133-1 at 9. Defendants rely on In re Grand Jury, 737 So. 2d
1, 10 (La. 1999), which stands for the principle that a party seeking release of state grand jury
materials should, according to comity, first direct their request to the state court which supervised
the criminal proceedings so that court may evaluate the continuing need for secrecy.
Plaintiff argues that comity does not require her to first seek disclosure of the state grand
jury materials from the 24th Judicial District Court. R. Doc. 138 at 11. Plaintiff further argues that
this approach is “inconsistent with controlling federal law,” relying on In re Babin, 2022 WL
1658701 (5th Cir. 2022).
The facts of the present case are comparable to those discussed in In re Grand Jury , 737
So.2d 1 (La. 1999) , where the Supreme Court of Louisiana held that absent a federal grand jury
subpoena, state grand jury materials would not be released to federal authorities without a
particularized showing of compelling need at a contradictory hearing. Id. at 10.  There, a United

States Attorney requested state grand jury transcripts from a Baton Rouge Parish District Attorney,
who wrote up a motion and order for their release. Id. at 4. The 19th Judicial District Court entered
the order without further inquiry. Id. at 4. The subjects of the state grand jury proceedings
requested a fact-finding inquiry, which was denied. Id. The Supreme Court of Louisiana reasoned
that release of the transcripts was not appropriate because comity prescribes that requests for the
release of state grand jury proceedings made by government attorneys and private litigants alike
must first be directed to the court which presided over the grand jury proceedings for an evaluation
of the continuing need for secrecy. Id. at 10.
In the present case, like the government attorney in In re Grand Jury, the Plaintiff erred in
directing her request for the release of the grand jury transcripts to D.A. Connick. The principles
of comity prescribe that Plaintiff should first direct her request to the court which presided over
the disputed grand jury proceedings, the 24th Judicial District Court, for an evaluation of the
continued need for secrecy. Should the 24th Judicial District deny release, only then should this
Court consider the issue.
Finally, Plaintiff’s reliance on In re Babin, 2022 WL 1658701 (5th Cir. 2022), to attempt
to refute this conclusion is misplaced. In In re Babin, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
upheld the release of Texas state grand jury transcripts despite the state court which presided over
the grand jury proceedings not having evaluated the continuing need for secrecy. Id. at *6.
In In re Babin, a  video-streaming company sued a state district attorney in federal court
and sought emergency discovery into state grand jury proceedings to respond to the district
attorney’s motion for summary judgment. Id. at *1. The district court approved release of the
transcripts, but the district attorney appealed in part on the argument that the Texas Code of

Criminal Procedure requires release to first be sought from the state court which presided over the
grand jury proceedings. Id. at *4 (citing TEX. CODE CRIM. PROC. art. 20A.205(a)-(b)).
“The district court concluded that there was ambiguity in the statutory language concerning
where the petition was to be filed.” Id.  at *5. The Fifth Circuit affirmed because it reasoned that
the video-streaming company demonstrated a particularized need for the transcripts but did not
further analyze the breadth of the Texas statute. Id.
In re Babin is distinguishable from the present case because it relies on the district court
and the Fifth Circuit’s deference to Texas law. The Texas Code of Criminal Procedure article cited
states that “[t]he defendant may petition a court to order the disclosure of information made secret
by Article . . . 20A.204 . . . as a matter preliminary to or in connection with a judicial proceeding.
The court may order disclosure of the information if the defendant shows a particularized need.”
TEX. CODE CRIM. PROC. art. 20A.205(a). Further, such a petition “must be filed in the district court
in which the case is pending.”  Id. at (b). This language allowed the district court and the Fifth
Circuit to approve and affirm the release of Texas grand jury materials without any action from
the Texas court which presided over the proceedings.
Conversely, Louisiana has no equivalent statute indicating “where” a petition for the
release of state grand jury proceedings must be filed. See LA. CODE CRIM. PROC. ANN. art. 434.1
(establishing the exceptions to grand jury secrecy, but not where such request must first be
directed). Instead, Louisiana case law-- In re Grand Jury —requires that absent a grand jury
subpoena, any request for state grand jury materials must first be directed to the court which
presided over such proceedings. 737 So.2d at 10. Following the principles of comity, this Court
declines to grant release of the subject state grand jury materials before the 24th Judicial District
Court has had the opportunity to evaluate the continuing need for secrecy.
