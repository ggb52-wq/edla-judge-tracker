II. Standards for Frivolousness Review
Pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1915(e)(2) and § 1915A  and 42 U.S.C. § 1997e(c), the Court is
required to sua sponte  dismiss cases filed by prisoners proceeding in forma pauperis  upon a
determination that they are frivolous.  The Court has broad discretion in determining the frivolous
nature of the complaint.  See Cay v. Estelle, 789 F.2d 318, 325 (5th Cir. 1986), modified on other
grounds, Booker v. Koonce, 2 F.3d 114 (5th Cir. 1993).  However, the Court may not sua sponte
Case 2:24-cv-01997-SM     Document 8     Filed 12/13/24     Page 3 of 17
4

dismiss an action merely because of questionable le gal theories or unlikely factual allegations in
the complaint.
Under this statute, a claim is frivolous when it lacks an arguable basis either in law or fact.
Neitzke v. Williams , 490 U.S. 319, 324-25 (1989); Talib v. Gilley , 138 F.3d 211, 213 (5th Cir.
1998).  “A [claim] lacks an arguable basis in law if  it is based on an indis putably meritless legal
theory, such as if the complaint alleges the viol ation of a legal interest which clearly does not
exist.”  Harper v. Showers, 174 F.3d 716, 718 (5th Cir. 1999) (quoting Davis v. Scott, 157 F.3d
882, 889 (5th Cir. 1998)).  It lacks an arguable fact ual basis only if the fact s alleged are “clearly
baseless,” a category encompassing fancif ul, fantastic, and delu sional allegations.  Denton v.
Hernandez, 504 U.S. 25, 32-33 (1992) (citing Neitzke, 490 U.S. at 327-28).  Thus, the Court must
determine whether plaintiff’s claims are based on an indisputably meritless legal theory or clearly
baseless factual allegations.  Reeves v. Collins , 27 F.3d 174, 176 (5th Cir. 1994); Jackson v.
Vannoy, 49 F.3d 175, 176-77 (5th Cir. 1995); Moore v. Mabus, 976 F.2d 268, 269 (5th Cir. 1992).
III. Discussion
A. Improperly Named Defendants
Among the named defendants, Smith included inmate Davenport, the STPJ, the STPJ
security supervisors, all STPJ staff/officers, a nd the STPJ medical nurses.   For the reasons that
follow, these are not proper defendants under § 1983.
a. St. Tammany Parish Jail
Smith sued the STPJ as a defendant.  A plaintiff bringing a § 1983 complaint is required to
identify both a constitutional violation and the responsible person acting under color of state law.
Flagg Bros., Inc. v. Brooks, 436 U.S. 149, 156 (1978).  The STPJ, however, is not recognized as
Case 2:24-cv-01997-SM     Document 8     Filed 12/13/24     Page 4 of 17
5

a “person” for purposes of suit under § 1983.  See Douglas v. Gusman, 567 F. Supp. 2d 877, 892
(E.D. La. Jun. 9, 2008) (Order adopting attached Report and Recommendation).
In Louisiana, a jail facility is not an entity “legally empowered to do” anything independent
of either the responsible parish officials or the parish sheriff.  Roberts v. Sewerage and Water Bd.
of New Orleans, 634 So. 2d 341, 347 (La. 1994).  Because a jail is not a juridical entity under state
law, “a prison or jail or its administrative depa rtments are not entities that can be sued under
Section 1983 . . .”  Douglas, 567 F. Supp. 2d at 892; see Fed. R. Civ. P. 17(b).  As a division of
this Court has noted, a parish jail is  “not an entity, but a building.”  See Jones v. St. Tammany
Parish Jail, 4 F. Supp.2d 606, 613 (E.D. La. May 8, 1998) (dismissing with pr ejudice the St.
Tammany Parish Jail as an improper defendant); see also Kerr v. Orleans Par. Sheriff’s Off.
Prison, No. 15-0746, 2015 WL 4755174, at *1 (E.D. La. Aug. 10, 2015).
Consequently, Smith’s claims against the STPJ should be dismissed under 28 U.S.C.
§ 1915 and § 1915A as frivolous and for failure to state a claim for which relief can be granted.
b. Inmate Davenport
Smith asserts a number of grievances against Jacquelen Davenport, who is a trustee inmate
at STPJ and the inmate who brought the PREA charge against her.  A plaintiff seeking to recover
damages under § 1983 must establish that a defe ndant has acted under color of state law in
violating her rights.  Flagg Bros., Inc. , 436 U.S. at 156.  The federal courts, however, have
consistently held that an inmate, even a trustee inmate, is not a state actor or a person acting under
the color of state law for purposes of stating a claim under § 1983.  See Batiste v. Gusman, No. 13-
96, 2013 WL 6095833, at *3 (E.D. La. Nov. 20, 2013); Bland v. Terrebonne Par. Crim. Just.
Complex, No. 09-4407, 2009 WL 3486449, at *3 (E.D. La. Oct. 23, 2009).  Davenport, as an
inmate, is a private person not a state actor.

A plaintiff can establish that a defendant acted  under color of state law if the defendant
exercised power “‘possessed by virtue of state law and made possible only because the wrongdoer
is clothed with the authority of state law.’”  West v. Atkins, 487 U.S. 42, 49 (1988); accord Johnson
ex rel. Wilson v. Dowd, 305 F. App’x 221, 223-24 (5th Cir. 2008); Cornish v. Corr. Servs. Corp.,
402 F.3d 545, 549 (5th Cir. 2005).  A pr ivate person may only be cons idered a state actor if the
plaintiff establishes that their actions are attributable to the State in this way.  See Priester v.
Lowndes Cnty., 354 F.3d 414, 420 (5th Cir. 2004 ) (actions may be attribut able to the state if the
private citizen is a willful participant in joint activ ity with a State or its ag ents).  Otherwise, “the
under-color-of-state-law element of § 1983 exclude s from its reach merely private conduct, no
matter how discriminatory or wrongful.”  Am. Mfrs. Mut. Ins. Co. v. Sullivan, 526 U.S. 40, 49-50
(1999) (internal quotation marks omitted).
Smith has presented no facts to establish that  Davenport’s alleged actions are attributable
to the State or that she acted jo intly with any state official to violate a constitutionally protected
right.1  Her conclusory suggestion that Davenport r eceives favoritism from  officers and reports
other inmates’ contraband to them is insufficient to establish th e necessary level of joint activity
or attribution to the State or that any action by Davenport or the correctional officers amounted to
a constitutional violation. 2  Without such a showing, Davenpor t is not a state actor or proper
defendant for purposes of § 1983.

Smith also references as defendants all of STPJ  “staff/officers” and the medical nurses as

action must be filed against an actual identified person who violated a constitutional right, not a

690 (5th Cir. 1986)).  In pleading specific facts of conspiracy, a plaintiff must allege the operative facts of the alleged

complaint, Smith has not met her burden of pleading a non-conclusory, non-frivolous conspiracy claim or a claim

B. Claims Against Officers Burgess, Sparrow, Winn, and Maguire
Broadly construing her complaint, Smith alleges that she is mentally and emotionally upset
by the fact that, on a regular basis, Davenport is escorted to Dorm 300 to work as a trustee by
officers, including Officers Burgess, Sparrow, a nd Winn and that contrary to policy, they allow
Davenport to be near her and the other inmates wh en she should not be.  Smith’s allegations fail
to state a non-frivolous claim of a constitutional violation.
As an initial matter, an allegation that offi cers or other personnel failed to follow prison
policies is insufficient to state a § 1983 claim.  Samford v. Dretke , 562 F.3d 674, 681 (5th Cir.
2009) (citing Richardson v. Thornton, 299 F. App’x 461, 462-63 (5th Cir. 2008) (“The failure of
the prison to follow its own policies ... is not sufficient to make out a civil rights claim.”)); Myers
v. Klevenhagen, 97 F.3d 91, 94 (5th Cir. 1996) (“Our case law is clear, however, that a prison
official's failure to follow the prison's own policie s, procedures or regulations does not constitute
a violation of due process, if constitutional minima are nevertheless met.” (citations omitted)).  It
is true that the prison officials owe Smith a reasonable duty of protection from known risks to her
safety at the hands of another inmate.  Johnson v. Lucas , 786 F.2d 1254, 1259 (5th Cir. 1986)
(citations omitted); see also  Hare v. City of Corinth, Miss. , 74 F.3d 633, 650 (5th Cir. 1996)
(extending the Eighth Amendment’ s protection requirement to pretrial detainees under the

11

Fourteenth Amendment).3  However, Smith has not identified any such risk of physical harm from
Davenport’s mere presence on the dormitory.  As Smith repeatedly alleges, Davenport is always
either escorted into the dormitory by a guard or is monitored by guards standing at the door to the
dormitory for both her protection and Davenport’ s safety.  Smith otherwise presents only
speculative and conclusory concerns about Davenport which have caused her emotional distress.
This distress, however, is not a basis for a pr isoner to recover under § 1983.  It is well
settled that absent a “physical injury resulting from the prison officials’ purported failure to
protect,” a failure to protect claim fails.  Jones v. Greninger, 188 F.2d 322, 326 (5th Cir. 1999);
Walzier v. McMullen , 333 F. App’x 848, 851 (5th Cir. 2009).  Furthermore, under 42 U.S.C.
§ 1997e(e), “[n]o federal civil ac tion may be brought by a prisoner co nfined in a jail, prison, or
other correctional facility, for mental or emotional injury suffered while in custody without a prior
showing of physical injury.”  Smith essentially alleges that Davenport’s presence on the tier with
the guards to conduct her trustee duties has caused  her mental and emotional injury.  She has
alleged no physical injury as a result of any of her allegations in this complaint.
For these reasons, Smith’s allegations fail to state a non-frivolous claim against any of the
guards and their roles in escorting Davenport in the dormitory.  Smith’s claims should be dismissed
pursuant to § 1915(e) and § 1915A as frivolous and otherwise for failure to state a claim for which
relief can be granted.

C. Claims Against Officer Mizzel
Smith alleges that, on August 1, 2024, Officer Mizzel handed her a peanut butter and jelly
sandwich that tasted like roach spray.  She also alleges that another inmate smelled the roach spray
on the sandwich and her hands.  She was given a different sandwich by another inmate that did not
smell.  Smith also claims that, after tasting the tainted sandwich, she had an isolated episode of
vomiting and diarrhea.  For the reasons that follow, Smith’s  claim of excessive force against
Officer Mizzel arising from this incident is frivolous.
When food has been inadvertently contaminated  with substances or items, the conditions
of the prison environment are at  issue and the inquiry is whet her the prison official had a
sufficiently culpable state of mind as to the nature of those conditions and took appropriate
measures to remedy them.  See, e.g., Wesley v. Varand , 505 F. App'x at 91, 94 (3d Cir. 2012)
(examining Eighth Amendment appl icability upon bullet casings inadvertently tainting inmate’s
food).  However, when the contamination is alle ged to be intentional by a prison official, the
affirmative conduct done to the plaintiff calls for an analysis of excessive force.  Byrd v. Brittain,
No. 20-164, 2020 WL 8173521, at *4 (M.D. Pa. Dec. 2, 2020), report and recommendation
adopted by 2021 WL 134203, at *1 (M.D. Pa. Jan. 14, 2021).
Pretrial detainees have a due process right  under the Fourteenth Amendment to be free
“from the use of excessive force that amounts to punishment.”  Kingsley v. Hendrickson, 576 U.S.
389, 397, 400 (2015).  Punishment “can consist of ac tions taken with an ‘expressed intent to
punish.’”  Id. at 398 (quoting Bell v. Wolfish, 441 U.S. 520, 538 (1979)).  But even “in the absence
of an expressed intent to punish, a pretrial de tainee can nevertheless prev ail by showing that the
actions are not ‘rationally related to a legiti mate nonpunitive governmental purpose’ or that the
actions ‘appear excessive in relation to that purpose.’”  Id. (quoting Bell, 441 U.S. at 561).  To
Case 2:24-cv-01997-SM     Document 8     Filed 12/13/24     Page 12 of 17
13

meet this standard, the pretrial detainee must sh ow that “the force purposely and knowingly used
against him was objectively unreasonable.”  Id. at 397.  To determine whether an officer’s use of
force was objectively reasonable, courts look to the “fac ts and circumstances of each particular
case.”  Id. (quoting Graham v. Connor, 490 U.S. 386, 396 (1989)).  In  determining the objective
reasonableness of an officer’s use of force, a court should consider th e following non-exclusive
factors: (1) the relationship between the need for the use of force and the amount of force used; (2)
the extent of the plaintiff's injury; (3) any effort made by the officer to temper or limit the amount
of force; (4) the severity of the security problem  at issue; (5) the threat  reasonably perceived by
the officer; and (6) whether the plaintiff was actively resisting.  Id.
A number of courts have resolved that a guard’s intentional tampering with an inmate’s
food so as to cause injury upon the inmate  eating it constitutes excessive force.  Byrd, 2020 WL
8173521, at 84 (plaintiff stated a claim of excessi ve force alleging that the defendant guard
intentionally handed him a tray of poisoned food that made him immediately ill) (citing Adams v.
Tenn. Dep’t of Corrs., No. 12-787, 2012 WL 3402174, at *4  (M.D. Tenn. Aug. 14, 2012)); see
also, Williams v. Klem , No. 07-1044, 2011 WL 2970303, at *11 n.2 (M.D. Pa. May 10, 2011)
(plaintiff stated an excessive form claim against correctional officer for spitting in inmate’s food);
Young v. Medden, No. 03-5432, 2006 WL 456274, at *24 (E.D. Pa. Feb. 23, 2006) (plaintiff stated
an excessive force claim by alleging defendants placed something in his food to make him urinate).
While the physical effect s of this type of tainted or pois oned food may be minimal and not long
lasting, determining whether plaintiff's injuries are de minimis requires consideration of the overall
context and reasonableness of  the officer’s actions.  Solis v. Serrett, 31 F. 4th 975, 981 (5th Cir.
2022) (citing Buehler v. Dear , 27 F. 4th 969, 982 (5th Cir. 2022); Scott v. White , 810 F. App’x
297, 300-01 (5th Cir. 2020); and Alexander v. City of Round Rock , 854 F.3d 298, 309 (5th Cir.
Case 2:24-cv-01997-SM     Document 8     Filed 12/13/24     Page 13 of 17

2017)).  In other words, “although a de minimis  injury is not cognizable, the extent of injury
necessary to satisfy the injury requirement is ‘directly related to the amount of force that is
constitutionally permissibl e under the circumstances.’”  Alexander, 854 F.3d at 309 (quoting
Brown v. Lynch, 524 F. App’x 69, 79 (5th Cir. 2013)).
Although Smith’s complaint details her other gr ievances about various incidents in her

D. Request for Compensatory Damages for Defamation
Although she provided no specific derogations  made by any particular defendant,
Davenport seeks relief for defamation.  Defamation claims do not involve the deprivation of any
right, privilege, or immunity secured by the C onstitution or laws of the United States.  See
Mowbray v. Cameron Cnty., 274 F.3d 269, 277 (5th Cir. 2001) (allegations of slander by a former
prisoner, resulting in public hum iliation, scorn, and ridicule, do not state a claim under § 1983);
Cook v. Houston Post , 616 F.2d 791, 794 (5th Cir. 1980); Castillo v. Bowles, 687 F. Supp. 277,
282 (N.D. Tex. Aug. 8, 1988) (dismissing inmate’s defamation claim against jail guards because,
even if his allegations were true, plaintiff only alleged harm to his reputation, a claim not protected
by the Constitution).  Further, the Fifth Circuit has held that a plaintiff’s conclusory allegations
that “slanderous statements resu lted in lost friendships, lost livelihood, lost time, and physical
injuries are insufficient to stat e a claim under 42 U.S.C. § 1983.”  Gill v. State of Tex. , 153 F.
App’x 261, 263 (5th Cir. 2005) (citing Arnaud v. Odom, 870 F.2d 304, 307 (5th Cir. 1989)).  Thus,
Smith’s defamation claim is not sufficient to state a § 1983 claim.  See Estelle v. Gamble, 429 U.S.
97, 106 (1976); Gibson v. Collier, 920 F.3d 212, 219-20 (5th Cir. 2019).
In sum, because Smith has al leged absolutely no facts to  suggest that defamation has
occurred, defamation is not cognizable under § 1983, and she has not invoked the Court’s
supplemental jurisdiction, Smith fails to state a claim for which relief can be granted.  Her request
for an award of damages for defamation must be dismissed pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1915 and
§ 1915A.
