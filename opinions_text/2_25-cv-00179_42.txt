II. LAW & ANALYSIS
A. Legal Standard
Summary judgment is appropriate when the “pleadings, depositions, answers to
interrogatories, and admissions on file, together with any affidavits, if any, show that there is no
genuine issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter
of law.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(a) & (c); see Celotex Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317, 322 (1986). Material
in support or opposition of a motion for summary judgment may be considered if it is “capable of
being ‘presented in a form that would be admissible in evidence.’” LSR Consulting, LLC v. Wells
Fargo Bank, N.A., 835 F.3d 530, 534 (5th Cir. 2016) (emphasis in original) (quoting Fed. R. Civ.
P. 56(c)(2)). Courts view all facts and evidence in the light most favorable to the non-moving party,
but “refrain from making credibility determinations or weighing the evidence.” Delta & Pine Land
Co. v. Nationwide Agribusiness Ins. Co., 530 F.3d 395, 398 (5th Cir. 2008) ; see, e.g., Liedtke v.
City of Austin, 185 F.4th 560, 570 (5th Cir. 2026).

Where the movant bears the burden of proof, it must “demonstrate the absence of a genuine
issue of material fact” using competent summary judgment evidence. Celotex, 477 U.S. at 323.
However, “where the non-movant bears the burden of proof at trial, the movant may merely point
to an absence of evidence.” Lindsey v. Sears Roebuck & Co., 16 F.3d 616, 618 (5th Cir. 1994)
(citing Celotex, 477 U.S. at 322, 106 S.Ct at 2553-54; Moody v. Jefferson Par. Sch. Bd., 2 F.3d
604, 606 (5th Cir.1993);  Duplantis v. Shell Offshore, Inc., 948 F.2d 187, 190 (5th Cir.1991) ).
Should the movant meet its burden, the burden then shifts to the non-movant, who must show by
“competent summary judgment evidence” that there is a genuine issue of material fact. See
Matsushita Elec. Indus. Co., Ltd. v. Zenith Radio Corp., 475 U.S. 574, 586 (1986); Lindsey, 16
F.3d at 618. This burden is not satisfied with “’some metaphysical doubt as to the material facts, ’
Matsushita, 475 U.S. at 586, by ‘conclusory allegations,’  Lujan v. National Wildlife
Federation, 497 U.S. 871, 885-86 (1990), by ‘unsubstantiated assertions ,’ Hopper v. Frank, 16
F.3d 92 (5th Cir.1994), or  by only a ‘scintilla’ of evidence , Davis v. Chevron U.S.A.,
Inc., 14 F.3d 1082 (5th Cir.1994)”. See Little v. Liquid Air Corp ., 37 F.3d 1069, 1075 (5th Cir.
1994). There is no genuine issue of material fact if, when  viewing the evidence in the light most
favorable to the non-moving party, no reasonable trier of fact could find for the non-moving party,
thus entitling the moving party to judgment as a matter of law. Smith v. Amedisys , 298 F.3d 434,
440 (5th Cir. 2002).
In a discrimination case, the plaintiff bears the ultimate burden of proving that the
defendant intentionally discriminated against him because of his race. St. Mary’ s Honor Ctr. v.
Hicks, 509 U.S. 502, 508 (1993). A plaintiff may establish intentional discrimination through either
direct or circumstantial evidence. Wallace v. Methodist Hosp. Sys., 271 F.3d 212, 219 (5th Cir.
2001). However, “‘direct evidence is rare’ for discrimination cases.” Coleman v. Chevron Phillips

Chem. Co., L.P ., No. 24-20244, 2025 WL 880534, *1, at *2 (5th Cir. 2025) (quoting Portis v. First
Nat'l Bank of New Albany, 34 F.3d 325, 328 (5th Cir. 1994) . The Fifth Circuit has defined direct
evidence as “ evidence which, if believed, proves the fact without inference or presumption. A
statement or document which shows ‘on its face that an improper criterion served as a basis—not
necessarily the sole basis, but a basis —for the adverse employment action [is] direct evidence of
discrimination.” Clark v. Champion Nat'l Sec., Inc., 952 F.3d 570, 579  (5th Cir. 2020)  (citing
Brown v. E. Miss. Elec. Power Ass'n, 989 F.2d 858, 861 (5th Cir. 1993) ; Herster v. Bd. of
Supervisors of La. State Univ., 887 F.3d 177, 185 (5th Cir. 2018)).
Where a defendant moves for summary judgment on an employment discrimination claim
based on circumstantial evidence, the Court utilizes the burden- shifting framework established
in McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green, 411 U.S. 792 (1973). See Turner v. Kan. City S . Ry.
Co., 675 F.3d 887, 892 (5th Cir. 2012). The first step of the  McDonnell Douglas  framework
requires “[t]he plaintiff [to] establish a  prima facie case that the defendant made an employment
decision that was motivated by a protected factor.”  Id. (quoting Mayberry v.  Vought Aircraft
Co., 55 F.3d 1086, 1089 (5th Cir.1995)). To establish a prima facie  case of discrimination based
on disparate treatment, the Fifth Circuit requires a plaintiff to show: “‘ (1) [he] is a member of a
protected class; (2 ) [he] was qualified for the position she sought; (3) [he]  suffered an adverse
employment action; and (4) others similarly situated but outside the protected class were treated
more favorably.’” Saketkoo v. Adm'rs of Tulane Educ. Fund, 31 F.4th 990, 997-98 (5th Cir. 2022)
(quoting Alvarado v. Tex. Rangers, 492 F.3d 605, 611 (5th Cir. 2007)). Under the fourth factor, the
plaintiff must show  that they were treated “less favorably than a similarly situated employee
outside of their protected class in nearly identical circumstances.” Id.at 998 (citing Lee v. Kan. City

S. Ry. Co., 574 F.3d 253, 259- 60 (5 th Cir. 2009); McDonell Douglas , 411 U.S. at 802). “The
similarly situated employee is known as a comparator. Id.
If the plaintiff establishes a prima facie case, the court proceeds to the second stage of the
analysis, where “the defendant bears the burden of producing evidence that its employment
decision was based on a legitimate nondiscriminatory reason.” Turner v. Kan. City S. Ry. Co., 675
F.3d at 892; see also Watkins v. Tregre, 997 F.3d 275 (5th Cir. 2021) (citing Alverado, 492 F.3d at
611 (the defendant’s burden is “one of production--not persuasion.”)).  If the defendant satisfies
its burden, the analysis moves to the third McDonnell Douglas stage, where “[t]he burden…shifts
back to the plaintiff to prove that the defendant's proffered reasons were a pretext for
discrimination.” Id. Notably, “the burden of proof remains with the employee throughout ” the
analysis. Saketkoo, 31 F.4th (citing Lee v. Kan. City S. Ry. Co., 574 F.3d at 259; St. Mary’s Honor
Ctr., 509 U.S. at 518 (quoting Tex. Dep’t of Cmty. Affs. v. Burdine, 450 U.S. 248, 253 (“The ultimate
burden of persuading the trier of fact that the defendant intentionally discriminated against the
plaintiff remains at all times with the plaintiff.”)).
At the third stage , the plaintiff must produce “substantial evidence” that the proffered
reason is a pretext for discrimination. Watkins, 997 F.3d at 283 (citing Laxton v. Gap Inc., 333 F.3d
572, 578 (5th Cir. 2003)). “Evidence is substantial if it is of such quality and weight that reasonable
and fair-minded men in the exercise of impartial judgment might reach different conclusions.” Id.
(citing Laxton, 333 F.3d at 579). The plaintiff may establish pretext of discrimination “either
through evidence of disparate treatment or by showing that [the defendant’s] proffered explanation
is false or ‘unworthy of credence.’” Id. (quoting Laxton, 333 F.3d at 578).
To survive summary judgment in a discrimination case, the plaintiff must first be able to
establish a prima facie case of discrimination. Saketkoo, 31 4th 990 at 998-99. Where the plaintiff

B. Analysis
1.Mr. Hearn has Failed to Establish Direct Evidence.
At the outset, Mr. Hearn has failed to establish direct evidence for his Title VII
discrimination claim. He alleged that there was ample direct evidence of discrimination, such as a
racially charged comment and false statements regarding knowledge of his race. (Rec. Doc. 37 at
6-7.) However, the evidence submitted by him requires an inferential step or fails to prove
discrimination on its face. See Clark, 952 F.3d at 579.
Beginning with the statement, Mr. Hearn only offers  his own deposition as  proof of
discrimination. He proffers  that Special Agent Delgado told him  prior to the start of his first
polygraph exam, “Please excuse my manner of speaking because I grew up in the Ninth Ward of
New Orleans.” (Rec. Doc. 37-3 at 11.) According to him, Special Agent Delgado subjected him to
unnecessary and offensive racial code by referencing the Ninth Ward. (Rec. Doc. 37 at 7.)
Next, Mr. Hearn alleges that the polygraph examiners denied his conditional offer letter
based on his race, as  they had access to documents listing his race or color. (Id.) He argues that
DEA personnel had access to documents listing his race or color, and the examiners denial of such
access illustrate discriminatory intent. (Id.) The relevant documents are his driver’s license (Rec.
Doc. 37-2) and Pre-Test Interview Worksheet (Rec. Doc.37-5), both of which indicate he is African

American. It is worth noting that Defendant refutes access to such documents  during the relevant
period. (Rec. Doc. 38 at 9.) (“Contrary to Plaintiff’s assertions in his opposition, the DEA
confirmed that during the relevant timeframe of June 1, 2022, through December 31, 2022, none
of the quality control reviewers accessed either Hearn’s driver’s license or the Form-74.”)
In response, Defendant has cited Clark v. Champion Nat’l Sec., Inc., providing this Court
with illustrations of direct evidence in the Fifth Circuit. For example, in Portis v. First Nat. Bank
of New Albany, Miss., 34 F.3d 325 (5th Cir. 1994), the Fifth Circuit found direct evidence where
the plaintiff’s supervisor told her on multiple occasions that she “wouldn’t be worth as much as
the men would be to the bank.” Clark, 952 F.3d at 579-80 (citing Portis, 34 F.3d at 329). Similarly,
in Etienne v. Spanish Lake Truck & Casino Plaza, L.L.C., 778 F.3d 473 (5th Cir. 2015), the Fifth
Circuit explicated that multiple statements by the plaintiff’s general manager stating she was “too
black to do various tasks” was also direct evidence. Id. (citing Etienne, 778 F.3d at 476).
After considering the evidence brought forward by Mr. Hearn and the precedential
illustrations by Defendant, both the statement and the alleged false knowledge  fail to reach the
threshold of direct evidence. Each instance requires an inferential step and fails to prove
discrimination on their face. Without any further evidence, this case is not one of the rare instances
of direct evidence, Coleman v. Chevron Phillips Chem. Co., L.P ., No. 24-20244, 2025 WL 880534,
*1, at *2 (5th Cir. 2025)  (quoting Portis, 34 F.3d at  328) , and this Court will now apply the
McDonell Douglas framework.

r. Hearn has failed to provide this Court with evidence of a comparator, thereby failing
to demonstrate a prima facie case of discrimination , and resulting in summary judgment in favor
of Defendant. This past Summer, in Bravo v. Dallas Indep. Sch. Dist., the Fifth Circuit held that a

plaintiff remains required to prove a similarly situated comparator during a McDonell Douglas
analysis. See Bravo v. Dallas Indep. Sch. Dist., 177 F.4th at 607. In Bravo, the plaintiff “failed to
offer any evidence of a similarly situated comparator,” resulting in the Fifth Circuit affirming
summary judgment in favor of the defendant. Id. at 606.
Here, Mr. Hearn proposed that the Fifth Circuit does not require comparator evidence at
the prima facie stage. (Rec. Doc. 37 at 8.)  He relies on Garcia v. Pro. Cont. Servs., Inc., 938 F.3d
236 (5th Cir. 2019) and Russell v. McKinney Hosp. Venture, 235 F.3d 219 (5th Cir. 2000) for his
interpretation. (Id.) In Garcia, the Fifth  Circuit analyzed a Title VII retaliation claim and
elucidated that the plaintiff demonstrated a sufficient comparator. Garcia, 938 F.3d at 244-46. In
Russell, the p arties stipulated that the plaintiff estab lished a prima facie case, which included
the plaintiff establishing a sufficient comparator. Russell, 235 F.3d at fn.5; (Rec. Doc. 38 at 6.)
Like the plaintiff in Bravo, Mr. Hearn has failed to provide any evidence of a comparator.
Without evidence of a comparator, he cannot carry his prima facie burden under the
McDonell Douglas framework. The absence of comparator evidence  prevents moving forward
with instant claims . Lindsey v. Sears Roebuck & Co., 16 F.3d 616, 618 (5th Cir. 1994) (citing
Celotex, 477 U.S. at 322, 106 S.Ct at 2553-54; Moody v. Jefferson Par. Sch. Bd., 2 F.3d 604, 606
(5th Cir.1993); Duplantis v. Shell Offshore, Inc., 948 F.2d 187, 190 (5th Cir.1991). For the
reasons set forth above, Defendant's motion for summary judgment on the discrimination
claim for disparate treatment is granted.
