III. LEGAL STANDARD
Summary judgment is proper when “there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact and
the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(a). The court must view
the evidence in the light most favorable to the nonmovant. Coleman v. Hous. Indep. Sch. Dist .,
113 F.3d 528, 533 (5th Cir. 1997). Initially, the movant bears the burden of presenting the basis
for the motion; that is, the absence of a genuine issue as to any material fact or facts. Celotex Corp.
v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317, 323 (1986). The burden then shifts to the nonmovant to come forward
with specific material  facts showing there is a genuine dispute for trial. Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(c);
Matsushita Elec. Indus. Co. v. Zenith Radio Corp ., 475 U.S. 574, 586 –87 (1986). A fact is
“material” if its resolution in favor of one party may affect the outcome of the case. Saketkoo v.
Adm’rs of Tulane Educ. Fund, 31 F.4th 990, 997 (5th Cir. 2022). “A dispute about a material fact
is ‘genuine’ if the evidence is such that a reasonable jury could return a verdict for the nonmoving
party.” Bodenheimer v. PPG Indus., Inc., 5 F.3d 955, 956 (5th Cir. 1993).

Fraud in Louisiana comes in two forms: contractual fraud under art. 1953 and tort (or
“delictual”) fraud pursuant to La. Civ. Code art. 2315. Griffin v. BSFI W. E & P, Inc., 2000-2122, p.
8 (La. App. 1 Cir. 2/15/02), 812 So.2d 726, 734. Art. 1953 fraud is at issue here.
Art. 1953 fraud concerns fraud in the formation of a contract and is considered a “vice to
consent,” operating as a ground to seek nullity of a contract. La. Civ. Code art. 1948 (“Consent
may be vitiated by . . . fraud . . ..”); see also Stutts v. Melton, 2013-0557, p. 9 (La. 10/15/13), 130
So.3d 808, 814 (art. 1953 “[f]raud vitiates consent”); Robinson v. Wayne & Beverly Papania &
Pyrenees Investments, LLC, 2015-1354, pp. 8–9 (La. App. 1 Cir. 10/31/16), 207 So.3d 566, 574,
writ denied sub nom. Robinson v. Papania, 2016-2113 (La. 3/13/17), 216 So.3d 808 (a contract
declared null through fraud “is deemed never to have existed”).2 Art. 1953 defines fraud as “a
misrepresentation
or a suppression of the truth made with the intention either to obtain an unjust
advantage for one party or to cause a loss or inconvenience to the other.”
Art. 1953 fraud is not breach of contract: breach of contract and art. 1953 fraud are distinct
causes of action, grounded in separate Code articles, with different elements. DA Exterminating Co.,
Inc. v. Discon , 24-116, p. 10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 10/30/24), 398 So.3d 1239, 1248 (distinguishing
between fraud and breach of contract); Keenan v. Donaldson, Lufkin & Jenrette, Inc., 575 F.3d
483, 490 (5th Cir. 2009) (“A breach of promise, standing alone, is not enough for a fraud claim.”) .
The nature of the wrongdoing in an art. 1953 fraud action is the fraudster’s specific intent to
deceive in order to gain an advantage over another, see Sanga v. Perdomo, 14-609, p. 6 (La. App. 5
Cir. 12/30/14), 167 So.3d 818, 821; the nature of the wrong in a breach of contract claim is
underperformance. Fraud provides grounds for immediate relief. Stutts, 130 So.3d at 814. The
cause of action does not depend on a party having ever breached an agreement.

A. Plaintiff’s Art. 1953 Fraud Claim under Louisiana Substantive Law is Not
“Malpractice” under LMMA.
The first issue presented is whether Plaintiff’s fraud claim pursuant to art. 1953 falls within
the scope of the Louisiana Medical Malpractice Act (“LMMA”). The Court previously addressed
this question in resolving a motion to dismiss and concluded that Pl aintiff’s art. 1953 fraud
allegations fall outside the purview of the LMMA. The Court now revisits this issue in light of the
recent Louisiana Supreme Court decision Broden v. Priority Management Group.
The LMMA applies only to “malpractice,” Coleman v. Deno , 2001 -1517, p . 16  (La.
1/25/02), 813 So. 2d 303, 315, which the Act defines as “any  unintentional tort or any breach of
contract based on health care or professional services rendered, or which should have been
rendered, by a health care provider, to a patient . . . .” La. R.S. 40:1231.1(A)(13). In Coleman v.
Deno, the Louisiana Supreme Court set forth six factors to help determine whether a qualified
healthcare provider’s conduct qualifies as “malpractice” under the Act:
(1) whether the particular wrong is “treatment related” or caused by a dereliction
of professional skill;
(2) whether the wrong requires expert medical evidence to determine whether the
appropriate standard of care was breached;
(3) whether the pertinent act or omission involved assessment of the patient's
condition;
(4) whether an incident occurred in the context of a physician-patient relationship,
or was within the scope of activities which a hospital is licensed to perform;
(5) whether the injury would have occurred if the patient had not sought treatment;
(6) whether the tort alleged was intentional.

Broden sits comfortably within settled precedent. Where a claim is neither an unintentional
tort nor breach of contract, it falls outside the LMMA. McDowell, 345 So. 3d at 511 (“The statutory
law leaves no doubt that ‘malpractice’ under the MMA does not include intentional acts;
malpractice is specifically defined as ‘any unintentional tort.’”). And where a plaintiff advances
an intentional tort claim based on health care or professional services, they must demonstrate the
defendant intended to injure or that the injury was substantially certain to flow from the
defendant’s actions. Nothing in Broden suggests there is anything unique about nursing home
staffing shortage cases that would warrant a departure from these principles. See Demouchet v.
Nexion Health Mgmt., Inc., 2025-438, p. 11 (La. App. 3 Cir. 5/6/26), 433 So. 3d 291, 301 (applying
Broden and finding that the plaintiff’s  art. 1953 fraud claim fell outside the LMMA). If anything,
Broden appeared to contemplate a scenario in which the plaintiff’s staffing shortage allegations
could fall outside the LMMA, provided the intent standard was satisfied. 427 So. 3d at 731 n.7.
Based on Broden, the Court sees no reason to depart from its prior conclusion that
Plaintiff’s art. 1953 fraud claim is not governed by the LMMA. Under Louisiana law, art. 1953
fraud is neither a tort 6 nor a breach of contract claim , and therefore plainly falls outside the Act’s
definition of malpractice. See Riley, 396 So. 3d at 479 (art. 1953 fraud claim outside the LMMA);
Andrews, 2025 WL 634348, at *5 (same).7

C. Genuine Issues of Material Fact Preclude Summary Judgment
Finally, Defendants maintain that dismissal is warranted because Plaintiff has failed
to produce any evidence showing that Defendants intentionally misrepresented the level of
staffing at Marrero Healthcare Center. R. Doc. 114-1 at 12.
Contractual fraud in Louisiana is comprised of three elements: “(1) a misrepresentation,
suppression, or omission of true information; (2) the intent  to obtain an  unjust advantage or to
cause damage or inconvenience to another; and (3) the error induced by a fraudulent act must relate
to a  circumstance substantially influencing the victim’s  consent to the contract.”  Robinson v.
Papania, 2022-1010, p. 6 (La. App. 1 Cir. 3/6/23), 368 So. 3d 74, 80. “Fraud may also result from
silence or inaction ” provided the defendant  had a duty to disclose the information.  Art.  1953;

to speak and to convey information which may influence the conduct of the other party, he is bound
to disclose the whole
truth and ensure that the inform ation volunteered is correct.” Id. (internal
quotation marks omitted).

his agent or mandatary). Aside from Ms. Bush’s representation, Plaintiff’s claim also rests on
Defendants’ concealment of Marrero’s staffing levels. The record leaves open the possibility that
Defendants intentionally remained silent on this issue to avoid deterring prospective residents. 11
Because a genuine dispute remains regarding Defendants’ intent to deceive Plaintiff, summary
judgment is inappropriate for the reason raised by Defendants.
V. CONCLUSION
For the foregoing reasons,
IT IS ORDERED that Defendants’ Motion for Summary Judgment, R. Doc. 114,
is hereby DENIED.
