II. Standard of Review
A) Protective Orders
According to Rule 26(c)(1) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, “[a] party or any person
from whom discovery is sought may move for a protective order in the court where the action is
pending.” FED. R. CIV. P. 26(c)(1).
The district court is entrusted wide discretion to decide whether to and to what extent to
issue a protective order. Nguyen v. Excel Corp., 197 F.3d 200, 209 n.27 (5th Cir. 1999) (citations
omitted). For example, the court may order that the parties conduct discovery by a particular
method. FED. R. CIV. P. 26(c)(1)(C).
If the movant demonstrates “good cause,” the court may issue an order to protect a party
or person from annoyance, embarrassment, oppression, or undue burden or expense.” FED. R. CIV.
P. 26(c)(1). Rule 26(c)’s “good cause” requirement indicates that the party seeking a protective
order has the burden “to show the necessity of its issuance, which contemplates a particular and
specific demonstration of fact as distinguished from stereotyped and conclusory statements.” In re

Terra Int’l, Inc. , 134 F.3d 302, 306 (5th Cir. 1998) (quoting United States v. Garrett , 571 F.2d
1323, 1326 n.3 (5th Cir. 1978)).
“Harassment” is not  mentioned by Rule 26, but it is reasonably contemplated. For the
purposes of this Order, “Harassment” is defined as “[u]nwarranted speech or behavior causing
annoyance, alarm, distress, or intimidation, usually occurring persistently over a period of time.”
Harassment, OXFORD ENGLISH DICTIONARY ONLINE (3d ed. 2023).
B) Pro Se Litigants
The Fifth Circuit has held that “ pro se pleadings are held to less stringent standards than
pleadings drafted by lawyers,” and “pro se pleadings must be treated liberally.” U.S. v. Robinson,
78 F.3d 172, 174 (5th Cir. 1996); Priester v. Lowndes County, 354 F.3d 414, 418 (5th Cir. 2004).
However, pro se litigants are not exempt from complying with the Federal Rules of Civil
Procedure and the Local Rules. NCO Fin. Sys., Inc. v. Harper -Horsley, No. 07-4247, 2008 WL
2277843, at *3 (E.D. La. May 29, 2008) (quoting Birl v. Estelle, 660 F.2d 592, 593 (5th Cir. 1981).
Further, pro se status does not “relieve [a litigant] of the obligation to comply with the governing
rules addressing the proper means of obtaining discovery.” Jackson v. Navy Fed. Credit Union,
No. 25-1821, 2026 WL 2937668, at *1 (E.D. La. Sept. 30, 2026).
This also means that pro se litigants must follow the applicable ethical rules, which in this
District are the Louisiana Rules of Professional Conduct. See E.D. La. LR 83.2.3, 83.2.7. Rule 4.2
of the Louisiana Rules of Professional Conduct provides that unless the other lawyer consents,  a
lawyer or pro se  litigant shall not communicate about “ the subject of the representation ” with a
person the lawyer or pro se  litigant knows to be represented by a lawyer in the matter. La. Rules
of Prof’l Conduct R. 4.2. This necessarily includes communications with individuals the lawyer
or pro se litigant knows to be a  “director, officer, employee, member, shareholder or other

constituent of a represented organization” who either (1) “supervises, directs or regularly consults
with the organization’s lawyer” about the matter; (2) “has the authority to obligate the organization
with respect to the matter; or (3) whose act or omission in connection with the matter may be
imputed to the organization for purposes of civil or criminal liability.” Id.
C) Methods of Discovery
Discovery in federal court is governed by the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure. The scope
of discovery is governed by Rule 26, which provides that “[p]arties may obtain discovery regarding
any nonprivileged matter that is relevant to any party’s claim or defense and proportional to the
needs of the case.” FED. R. CIV. P. 26(b)(1). Information within this scope need not be admissible
at trial to be discoverable Id.
If a party is represented by an attorney, service of discovery requests must be made on the
attorney unless the court orders otherwise. FED. R. CIV. P. 5(b)(1).
Parties seeking to gather discoverable information may do so by multiple methods,
including oral or written depositions (Rule 30 and Rule 31), interrogatories (Rule 33), requests for
production of documents or tangible things or to permit entry onto land (Rule 34), physical and
mental examinations (Rule 35), and requests for admission (Rule 36). FED. R. CIV. P. 30, 31, 33,
34, 35, 36. Any deficiencies with discovery responses are governed by Rule 37.

C) Plaintiff’s Ex Parte Communications
As a pro se  litigant, Lewis is not exempt from complying with relevant procedural and
substantive rules, including the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, this District’s Local Rules, and
the Louisiana Rules of Professional Conduct. NCO Fin. Sys., Inc., 2008 WL 2277843, at *3.
Although Lewis is not a licensed and practicing attorney, she consents to proceed under these
Rules by representing herself in the instant litigation. See Birl, 660 F.2d at 593 (“One who proceeds
pro se with full knowledge and understanding of the risks involved acquires no greater rights than
a litigant represented by a lawyer . . . [r]ather, such a litigant acquiesces in and subjects himself to
the established rules of practice and procedure.”).
Along with its allegations of harassment and inappropriate communications, Tulane argues
that Plaintiff is abusing her status as a pro se litigant by participating in ex parte communications
in violation of Rule 4.2 of the Louisiana Rules of Professional Conduct.
To support this, Tulane attempts to draw comparisons to Eustice v. Louisiana Through Bd.
Of Supervisors of Louisiana State Univ., 2020 WL 3520298, at *8 (M.D. La. June 29, 2020). In
Eustice, the U.S. District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana granted the defendant -
university’s motion for sanctions and dismissed the plaintiff-student’s claims against the university
with prejudice due to the plaintiff’s abhorrent communications used to harass defense counsel. Id.
at *7.
  Previously, the court had entered a protective order in favor of the defendant -university
which required the pro se plaintiff to halt all direct communication s with university faculty and
administrators, and to route all communication to the defendant through defense counsel. Id. at *2.
The protective order was entered to protect university faculty members and administrators based
on plaintiff’s violations of the university Code of Student Conduct, including being placed on

disciplinary probation, providing falsified academic records, and plaintiff’s arrest for cyberstalking
a fellow student with sexual advances. Id. at *1.  However, the plaintiff used this instruction as an
excuse to harass and indirectly threaten defense counsel. Id. at *2.
  The court focused on the plaintiff’s history of formally charged violations of university
rules and the sexually explicit nature of many of the communications sent to defense counsel and

violation of a Tulane internal rule, nor has she been arrested for stalking Tulane employees or
