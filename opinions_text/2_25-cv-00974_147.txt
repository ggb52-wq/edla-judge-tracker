II.  Standard of Law
 A. Rule 11
 Rule 11 is intended to “deter baseless filings” and thus “streamline the
administration and procedure of the federal courts.” Cordova v. Univ. Hosp. & Clinics,
Inc., 92 F.4th 266, 273 (5th Cir.) (quoting Cooter & Gell, 496 U.S. at 393), cert. denied
sub nom. Mire v. Univ. Hosp. & Clinics, Inc., 144 S. Ct. 2608 (2024). Rule 11 requires
attorneys to conduct a “reasonable inquiry” and determine that “any papers filed with
the court are well grounded in fact, legally tenable, and not interposed for any

improper purpose.” See id.  (quoting Cooter & Gell , 496 U.S. at 393) . An attorney’s
conduct is measured as of the time of his signature on the filing and against an
objective reasonableness standard.  See id. “When evaluating the reasonableness of
an attorney's factual inquiry under Rule 11, courts assess various factors, including
the time available to the signer for investigation . . . and the feasibility of a prefiling
investigation.” Id. at 276 (quotation omitted).
 Rule 11 requires that the offending party be provided a 21 -day “safe harbor”
before relief can be sought from the Court. Fed. R. Civ. P. 11(c)(2). A Rule 11 motion
be served on an opposing party “ but it must not be filed or be presented to the court
if the challenged paper, claim, defense, contention, or denial is withdrawn or
appropriately corrected within 21 days after service or within another time the court
sets.” Id. “The purpose of this safe harbor period is to ‘give the parties at whom the
motion is directed an opportunity to withdraw or correct the offending contention.’”
Ports Am. Gulfport, Inc. v. Johnson, No. CV 22-455, 2023 WL 35776, at *3 (E.D. La.
Jan. 3, 2023) (quoting Elliott v. Tilton, 64 F.3d 213, 216 (5th Cir. 1995)).
 B. Inherent Power
 Courts may impose attorneys’ fees as part of their inherent power in certain
“narrowly defined circumstances.” Chambers v. NASCO, Inc., 501 U.S. 32, 45 (1991)
(quotation and citation omitted). This power extends to sanctioning a party who has
“acted in bad faith, vexatiously, wantonly, or for oppressive readings.” See id.  at 45–
46 (quotations and citation omitted) . The Court’s inherent power is perhaps most
helpful when applied to fill gaps between statutes and the Fe deral Rules of Civil

III. Analysis
A. Rule 11

 Many of the pleadings and contentions that HF Hunger challenges under Rule
11 arose in the context of HF Hunter ’s motion to vacate the attachment. Rule 11
relief, however,  is available only while  the challenged document or contention
remains under consideration. “ Because this period of ‘ safe harbor’ is dependent on
the ability to withdraw or correct the challenged document or contention, it follows
that ‘a party cannot delay serving its Rule 11 motion until conclusion of the case (or
judicial rejection of the offending contention). ’” Margetis v. Furgeson , 666 F. App ’x
328, 331–32 (5th Cir. 2016)  (quoting Fed. R. Civ. P. 11(c) advisory committee's note
to 1993 amendment ). “At that point, there is nothing left for the opposing party to
withdraw or correct, and the ‘ policies and procedural protections’ provided by Rule
11(c)(2) cannot be given effect. ” Id. at 332 (quoting 5A Charles Alan Wright et al.,
Federal Practice and Procedure § 1337.2 (3d ed. 2016)).
 Between May 15  and August 9, 2025, HF Hunter engaged in a hard -fought
battle to vacate the attachment of the M/V HF Hunter and secure the release of the
vessel. HF Hunter ultimately prevailed. Given the unequivocal and repeated judicial
rejection of Atlantic’s “offending contention[s],” there is no longer a n allegation
relative to the vessel attachment for which Rule 11 relief is available. Thus, Rule 11’s

B. Inherent Power

 “[I]n order to impose sanctions against an attorney under its inherent power,
a court must make a specific finding that the attorney acted in bad faith.” See Elliott
v. Tilton, 64 F.3d 213, 217 (5th Cir. 1995) (citation omitted). For reasons that overlap
with those stated above, the record does not support the conclusion that Atlantic
acted in bad faith in prosecuting this action such that sanctions are appropriate under
the Court’s inherent power.
IV. Conclusion
 Atlantic’s attachment of the M/V HF Hunter required HF Hunter to engage in
costly, time -pressured, and high- stakes litigation. Yet any vessel  owner that
successfully challenges an attachment will likely incu r steep legal fees and costs  in
the pro cess. Moreover, cases in which vessel owners prevail necessarily involve
relatively little evidence in support of the attachment. That is because a mere
showing of probable cause is sufficient to support attachment. Finally, the time -
pressured nature of the  case is a necessary consideration in deciding whether to
award sanctions. See also Indus. Mar. Carriers, LLC v. Dantzler, Inc., 611 F. App ’x
600, 607 (11th Cir. 2015) (observing, in the context of wrongful arrest, that there are
certain “mistakes that our admiralty procedures anticipate and accept as a necessary
evil to be suffered in the interests of preventing parties from fleeing a court's
jurisdiction before the dispute can be adjudicated”).42 Thus, while Atlantic was
