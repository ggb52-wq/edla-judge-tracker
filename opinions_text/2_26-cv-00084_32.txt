A. Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6)
Rule 8(a)(2) requires “a short and plai n statement of the claim showing that
the pleader is entitled to relief.” F ED. R. CIV. P. 8(a)(2). A complaint that does not
satisfy Rule 8(a)(2)’s pleading standard fails to state a claim upon which relief can
be granted. See generally FED. R. CIV. P. 12(b)(6). “[T]he pleading standard Rule 8
announces does not require ‘detailed factual allegations,’ but it demands more than

moving her family, including her unemancipated mino r child, [T.R.] into what she believed to be a
well-designed and well-constructed home that was free of defects and suitable and comfortable to live
and raise a family in.” See id. ¶ 10.

an unadorned, the-defendant-unlawfully-harmed-me accusation.” Ashcroft v. Iqbal ,
556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009) (quoting Bell. Atl. Corp. v. Twombly , 550 U.S. 544, 555
(2007)). “A pleading that offers ‘labels and conclusions’ or ‘a formulaic recitations of
the elements of a cause of action will not do.’” Id. (quoting Twombly, 550 U.S. at
555). “Nor does a complaint su ffice if it tenders ‘naked  assertion[s]’ devoid of

“To survive a motion to dismiss” under  Rule 12(b)(6), “a complaint must
contain sufficient factual matter, accepted as true, to ‘state a claim to relief that is
plausible on its face.’” Iqbal, 556 U.S. at 678 (quoting Twombly, 550 U.S. at 570). “A
claim has facial plausibility when the plaintiff pleads factual content that allows
the court to draw the reasonable inference that the defendant is liable for the
misconduct alleged.” Id. (citing Twombly, 550 U.S. at 556). “Although ‘[courts]
accept all well-pled facts as true, constr uing all reasonable inferences in the
complaint in the light most favorable to the plaintiff, conclusory allegations
unwarranted factual inferences, or legal conclusions are not accepted as true.’”
Hodge v. Engleman , 90 F.4th 840, 843 (5th Cir. 2024) (quoting Allen v. Hays , 65
F.4th 736, 743 (5th Cir. 2023)).
B. Motion to Compel Arbitration
“The FAA was Congress’s way of responding to the general ‘hostility of
American courts to the enforceme nt of arbitration agreements.’” Lopez v. Cintas
Corp., 47 F.4th 428, 431 (5th Cir. 2022) (quoting Circuit City Stores, Inc. v. Adams ,
532 U.S. 105, 111 (2001)). “Contrary to that prior practice, the FAA establishes ‘a
Case 2:26-cv-00084-BSL-MBN     Document 32     Filed 09/30/26     Page 4 of 21
5

liberal federal policy favoring arbitration agreements.’” Id. (quoting Epic Sys. Corp.
v. Lewis , 584 U.S. 497, 505 (2018)). “To that end, courts mu st ‘rigorously enforce
arbitration agreements according to their terms.’” Id. (quoting Am. Express Co. v.
Italian Colors Rest., 570 U.S. 228, 233 (2013)).
Section 3 of the FAA is straightforward: If “any issue” is “referable to
arbitration” under a written agreement to ar bitrate, the Court “shall . . . stay the
trial of the action until such arbitration has been had in accordance with the terms
of the agreement[.]” 9 U.S.C. § 3. The “plain statutory text” of Section 3 of the FAA
“requires” the Court to “stay [this] procee ding” if “any issue” must be arbitrated.
Smith v. Spizzirri, 601 U.S. 472, 476 (2024) (citation omitted).
III.  ANALYSIS
A. Royal’s Rule 12(b)(6) Motion to Dismiss
Royal contends that D.R. Horton’s compl aint seeking to compel arbitration
pursuant to the FAA fails to state a clai m upon which relief can be granted because
the FAA is not applicable. According to Royal, D.R. Horton’s complaint fails to plead
allegations sufficient to establish that the transaction at issue (Ms. Royal’s
purchase of a newly constructed home) involved interstate commerce.
Royal reads the statute—and the Cont ract—too narrowly. Section 2 of the
FAA covers arbitration provisions in “a contract evidencing a transaction involving
commerce to settle by arbitration.” 9 U.S.C. § 2. The Supreme Court interprets
“‘involving commerce’ in the FAA as the functional equivalent of the more familiar
term ‘affecting commerce’—words of art that ordinarily signal the broadest
Case 2:26-cv-00084-BSL-MBN     Document 32     Filed 09/30/26     Page 5 of 21
6

permissible exercise of Congress’ Commerce Clause power.” Citizens Bank v.
Alafabco, Inc., 539 U.S. 52, 56 (2003) (citing Allied-Bruce Terminix Cos. v. Dobson ,
513 U.S. 265, 273–74 (1995)). “‘[E]nforcement of arbitration agreements [is thus]
within the full reach of the Commerce Cl ause,’” which “encompasses a wider range
of transactions than those actually ‘i n commerce’—that is, ‘within the flow of
interstate commerce[.]’” Id. (citations omitted). As such, a party invoking the FAA
does not have to show its own contract had any “specific effect upon interstate
commerce.” Id. at 56-57. It is sufficient that “in the aggregate the economic activity
in question would represent ‘a general prac tice . . . subject to federal control.’” Id.
(quoting Mandeville Island Farms, Inc. v. Am. Crystal Sugar Co. , 334 U.S. 219, 236
(1948)).
Here, as alleged in the Complaint, “[t]he Contract and Arbitration Clause,
executed by and between a Delaware and Texas corporation and a Louisiana
citizen, . . . involv[es] the construction and agreement to purchase a home[.]” The
relevant contract involves a non-Louisiana builder’s constructi on of a home in
Louisiana. “Citizens of different states  engaged in performance of contractual
operations in one of those states are engaged in a contract involving commerce
under the FAA.” See Mesa Operating Ltd. P’ship v. Louisiana Intrastate Gas Corp. ,
797 F.2d 238, 243 (5th Cir. 1986), abrogated on other grounds sub nom. by Dr.
Kenneth Ford v. NYLCare Health Plans of Gulf Coast, Inc. , 141 F.3d 243 (5th Cir.
1998). Ms. Royal’s and D.R. Horton’s cont ract thus involves commerce and
necessarily falls within the scope of Section 2.

Consider Allied-Bruce Terminix Cos. v. Dobson , 513 U.S. 265 (1995). There,
the Alabama Supreme Court held that the home-protection plan signed in Alabama
between Alabama homeowners and the local office of a pest control company
involved a transaction that was “primarily local and not ‘substantially’ interstate.”
513 U.S. at 268-69 (observing that the pest control company was a multistate firm
that shipped treatment and repair material from out of state). The United States
Supreme Court reversed, holding that “‘involving,’ like ‘affecting,’ signals an intent
to exercise Congress’ commerce power to the full[,]” and that “even if the parties did
not contemplate an interstate commerce connection,” what is required is whether
the transaction involved commerce. Id. at 277, 281-82. Alafabco is in accord. The
debt-restructuring agreements “were ex ecuted in Alabama by Alabama residents”
and still they satisfied the involving-commerce test. 539 U.S. at 57. The Supreme
Court also found that the state court’s search for proof that the loans “originated
out-of-state” was “misguided.” Id. at 56. Make no mistake, “the FAA’s reach” is
broad. See id.
Royal seeks to downplay the interstate nature of the contract, arguing that
Ms. Royal merely locally purchased Louisiana property. Considering the correct
framing, however, D.R. Horton’s complaint states a claim. As the Complaint and
associated filings make clear, the Contract  is an agreement for more than merely
the sale of an existing home—it was entere d into for D.R. Horton’s construction and
sale to Ms. Royal of a newly-built single-fa mily residence. As alleged in the state-
court petition, Royal challenges D.R. Hort on’s conduct as builder of the home and

Critically, the Contract contains both  a warranty and a dispute-resolution

activity, in the aggregate, that impacts su bstantially on interstate commerce as it

Compelling arbitration under the FAA pr oceeds in “two analytical steps.”
Kubala v. Supreme Prod. Servs., Inc., 830 F.3d 199, 201 (5th Cir. 2016). At step one,
the Court “appl[ies] state law to determine whether the parties formed ‘any

the step-two analysis changes if the ar bitration agreement “delegates to ‘the

1. The Arbitration Provision is a Valid Agreement to
Arbitrate.
Step one of the two-part analysis is satisfied because the arbitration
provision in the contract between D.R. Ho rton and Ms. Royal is  a valid agreement
to arbitrate under Louisiana law.
In determining this first step—wheth er there is a valid agreement to
arbitrate—the Court considers whether the parties “entered into any arbitration
agreement at all.” Kubala, 830 F.3d at 201. This requ ires an analysis of the
“agreement’s existence rather than its validity.” Hill v. Jackson Offshore Holdings,
L.L.C., 175 F.4th 324, 339 (5th Cir. 2026) (Douglas, J., concurring) (citing, inter
alia, Arnold v. Homeaway, Inc. , 890 F.3d 546, 550 (5th Cir. 2018) (“In conducting
[an] inquiry [into whether the parties formed an arbitration agreement], we
distinguish between ‘validity’ or ‘enforceability’ challenges and ‘formation’ or
‘existence’ challenges.”)). “This formation versus validity distinction is critical to the

must possess the capacity to contract; (2) the parties’ mutual consent must be freely

have a lawful purpose.” Provenza v. Cent. & Sw. Servs., Inc. , 775 So. 2d 84, 89 (La.

3. The Direct Benefits Estoppel Doctrine Applies.
This result—granting D.R. Horton’s motion to compel arbitration—applies
equally to Ms. Royal’s minor child, as well as Trumaine Lee and Tyron Brown.
Though they are not signatories to the Contract, invoking the direct benefits
estoppel doctrine, D.R. Horton contends that they, too, must be compelled to
arbitrate their claims against D.R. Hort on. For the reasons set forth in D.R.
Horton’s motion and reply,21 the Court agrees.
The Royal Defendants resist this conclusion. But these federal Defendants do
not dispute that the Fifth Circuit has adopte d the direct benefits estoppel doctrine
and that the doctrine may be applied to non-signatories. 22 To be sure, where “a non-
signatory ‘knowingly exploits the agreement containing the arbitration clause,’” he
is bound to arbitrate the claims that seek to benefit from the agreement. See, e.g.,
Hellenic Inv. Fund, Inc. v. Det Norske Veritas , 464 F.3d 514, 518 (5th Cir. 2004).
The Fifth Circuit has described the doctrine thusly:
Direct-benefits estoppel applies to non-signatories who, during the life
of the contract, have embraced the contract despite their non-signatory
status but then, during litigation, attempt to repudiate the arbitration
clause in the contract. A nonsignatory can embrace a contract
containing an arbitration contract  in two ways: (1) by knowingly
seeking and obtaining direct benefi ts from the contract, or (2) by
seeking to enforce the terms of the contract or asserting claims that
must be determined by reference to that contract.
