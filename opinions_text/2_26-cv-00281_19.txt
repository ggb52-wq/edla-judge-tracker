II. Legal Standard
20 C.F.R. § 404.1520 requires a five-step sequential analysis in determining a
claimant’s assertion of disability.
The five steps consider: (1) Is the claimant “doing substantial gainful
activity”? (2) If not, does the claimant “have a severe medically
determinable physical or mental impairment” of sufficient duration?
(3) If so, does her impairment(s) meet or equal a listing in Appendix
1 of the applicable regulations? (4) If not, considering the claimant’s
“residual functional capacity,” can the claimant still do her “past
relevant work”? (5) If not, can the claimant adjust to other work given
her residual functional capacity, age, education, and work
experience?
Vasquez v. O’Malley, 2024 WL 4381269, at *1 n.1 (5th Cir. Oct. 3, 2024) (quoting 20
C.F.R. § 416.920). At Steps One through Four, the burden of proof is on the claimant
to show she is disabled. Shave v. Apfel , 238 F.3d 592, 594 (5th Cir. 2001)  (citation
omitted). If the claimant can carry this burden through Steps Three or Four then she
has established disability. Id. The burden then shifts to the Commissioner at Step
Five to prove the claimant nonetheless retains sufficient functional capacity to adjust
to other employment that exists in significant numbers in the national economy. Id.;
see also Perez v. Heckler, 777 F.2d 298, 300-301 (5th Cir. 1985). If the Commissioner
meets that burden, then the burden shifts back to the claimant to prove that she cannot
perform the other work. Carey v. Apfel, 230 F.3d 131, 135 (5th Cir. 2000).
This Court’s review of a denial of social security benefits “is exceedingly
deferential and limited to two inquiries: whether substantial evidence supports the
ALJ’s decision, and whether the ALJ applied the proper legal standards when

evaluating the evidence.” Taylor v. Astrue, 706 F.3d 600, 602 (5th Cir. 2012)  (citing
Greenspan v. Shalala, 38 F.3d 232, 236 (5th Cir. 1994)). When the ALJ’s findings are
supported by substantial evidence, such findings are deemed conclusive and the Court
must affirm them. Richardson v. Perales, 402 U.S. 389, 390 (1971) (citing Section 205(g),
42 U.S.C. § 405(g)). The Court “may not reweigh the evidence in the record, nor try
the issues de novo, nor substitute [its] judgment. . . even if [the Court concludes that]
the evidence preponderates against” the ALJ’s findings. Johnson v. Bowen, 864 F.2d
340, 343 (5th Cir. 1988) (citation omitted).
The substantial -evidence standard requires the Court to determine whether
sufficient evidence supports t he Administration’s factual determinations. Biestek v.
Berryhill, 139 S.Ct. 1148, 1154 (2019) (citation omitted). “Substantial” here is not a high
evidentiary threshold, but rather the Court may properly find that substantial evidence
does not support the ALJ’s decision only absent credible evidentiary choices  or a lack of
contrary medical evidence. Id.; see also Johnson, 864 F.2d at 343–344 (citing Hames v.
Heckler, 707 F.2d 162, 164 (5th Cir. 1986)) . “Substantial evidence is ‘such relevant
evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.’”
Greenspan, 38 F.3d at 236 (citations omitted). This must constitute “more than a mere
scintilla” of evidence but may be “less than a preponderance.” Spellman v. Shalala, 1
F.3d 357, 360 (5th Cir. 1993)  (citing Moore v. Sullivan, 919 F.2d 901, 904 (5th Cir.
1990)).

§ 404.1520 and determined that Claimant met Steps One through Four but not Step

Five.10 The ALJ considered the vocational expert’s testimony that Claimant is able to
perform the work of unskilled sedentary occupations , such as order clerk and
interviewer, even with a limitation of standing or walking no more than two hours
each workday .11 Although Dr. Genovese opined that Claimant is subject to more

The ALJ, not the treating physician, has “sole responsibility for determining a
claimant’s disability status.” Greenspan, 38 F.3d at  237. The ALJ accordingly “is
entitled to determine the credibility of medical experts . . . and weigh their opinions
accordingly.” Scott v. Heckler, 770 F.2d 482, 485 (5th Cir. 1985). This includes an
ALJ’s ability to assign little or no weight to a treating physician’s opinions when good
cause is shown. Greenspan, 38 F.3d at 237.
When a treating source’s opinion is not well -supported or is inconsistent with
other substantial evidence, the ALJ considers a number of factors in determining
what weight to afford the opinion . 20 C.F.R. §§ 404.1529(c)(2)–(6), 416.927(c)(2)–(6),

2017 as: (1) whether the medical source performed an examination; (2) the nature of
the treatment relationship (considering the length of the treating relationship  and
the frequency of examination , and  the nature and extent of the treatment
relationship); ( 3) whether the physician provides medical evidence to support his

IV.  Conclusion
Substantial evidence supports the ALJ’s conclusion that Claimant can adjust
to other work as required by Step Five in the sequential analysis. The ALJ was within
his discretion to afford Dr. Genovese’s assessments little to no weight considering the
significant contrary evidence from multiple other sources.
Accordingly,
IT IS ORDERED that the decision of the Administrative Law Judge at Step
Five that Claimant could perform other work given her residual functional capacity,
age, education, and work experience, and thereby is not disabled, is AFFIRMED.
