A. Administrative Exhaustion
A plaintiff must exhaust their administrative remedies before filing an ADA claim in
federal court.  Patton v. Jacobs Eng’g Grp., Inc. , 874 F.3d 437, 443 (5th Cir. 2017). A plaintiff
exhausts those remedies by filing a timely charge with the EEOC. Id. (citing 42 U.S.C. § 2000e–
5(e)(1)). The charge should include “[a] clear and concise statement of the facts, including
pertinent dates, constituting the alleged unlawful employment practices.” 29 C.F.R. §
1601.12(a)(3).
To satisfy the exhaustion requirement, a plaintiff’s present claims must have been asserted
in their EEOC charge or “fall within the scope of the EEOC investigation which can reasonably
be expected to grow out of the charge.” Chhim v. Univ. of Tex. at Austin, 836 F.3d 467, 472 (5th
Cir. 2016)  (internal quotations omitted).  Determining w hether a claim might grow out of an
administrative charge requires a “fact-intensive analysis” that looks “slightly beyond [the] four
corners” of the charge to “substance rather than [] label.” Pacheco v. Mineta, 448 F.3d 783, 789
(5th Cir. 2006). Courts construe the charge liberally, mindful that most administrative complaints
are filed pro se. Id. at 788. A plaintiff need not allege every element of a claim, id. at 792 (“Nor
do we require, for purposes of exhaustion, that a plaintiff allege a prima face case before the
EEOC.”), but rather enough facts to prompt a reasonably diligent investigator to inquire into the
claim. See id.
B. Failure to State a Claim
Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(b)(6) provides that an action may be dismissed “for failure to state a
claim upon which relief can be granted.” “To survive a motion to dismiss, a complaint must contain
sufficient factual matter, accepted as true, to ‘state a claim for relief that is plausible on its face.’”
Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009) (quoting  Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly , 550 U.S. 544,
Case 2:26-cv-00726-EEF-DPC     Document 16     Filed 09/08/26     Page 4 of 16
5

570 (2008)). “Factual allegations must be enough to raise a right to relief above the speculative
level.” Twombly, 550 U.S. at 556. A claim is plausible on its face when the plaintiff has pled facts
that allow the court to “draw a reasonable inference that the defendant is liable for the misconduct
alleged.” Id. at 570. Although a court must liberally construe the complaint in light most favorable
to the plaintiff, accept the plaintiff’s allegations as true, and draw all reasonable inferences in favor
of the plaintiff, Baker v. Putnal , 75 F.3d 190, 196 (5th Cir. 1996), courts “do not accept as true
conclusory allegations, unwarranted factual inferences, or legal conclusions.” Arias-Benn v. State
Farm Fire & Cas. Co. , 495 F.3d 228, 230 (5th Cir. 2007) (quoting Plotkin v. IP Axess Inc. , 407
F.3d 690, 696 (5th Cir. 2005)).
If a complaint fails to state a plausible claim, a district court should generally grant leave
to amend under Fed. R. Civ. P. 15 before dismissing the action. Carroll v. Fort James Corp., 470
F.3d 1171, 1175 (5th Cir. 2006 ) (“[Rule 15] evinces a bias in favor of granting leave to amend.
The policy of the Federal Rules is to permit liberal amendment . . .”). A court may deny leave,
however, when amendment would be futile. Villarreal v. Wells Fargo Bank , N.A., 814 F.3d 763,
766 (5th Cir. 2016) (explaining that amendment is futile when “the amended complaint would fail
to state a claim upon which relief could be granted”).

C. ADA Discrimination: Failure to Reasonably Accommodate
Walker avers that AmeriGas discriminated against  him in violation of the ADA when it
failed to reasonably accommodate his disability. R. Doc. 10 at 7. The ADA requires employers to
make  “reasonable accommodations to the known physical or mental limitations of an
otherwise qualified individual  with a  disability who is an applicant or  employee, unless
such covered entity can demonstrate that the accommodation would impose an undue hardship on
the operation of the business . . .” 42 U.S. C. § 12112(b)(5)(A). Generally, each denial of a
reasonable accommodation request gives rise to a separate cause of action subject to its own
exhaustion requirement and limitations period. Henson v. Bell Helicopter Textron, Inc. , 128 F.
App’x 387, 391 (5th Cir. 2005) (per curium) (characterizing requests for different accommodations
as “discrete acts” with their own limitations period); Drake v. Spring Indep . Sch. Dist., No. 20 -
20376, 2021 WL 3176081, at *4 (S.D. Tex. July 27, 2021)  (exhaustion of one failure to
accommodate incident did not exhaust a request for a different form of accommodation);
Henderson v. City of Dallas, No. 3:16-cv-3317-S, 2018 WL 4326936, at *10 (N.D. Tex. Sept. 10,
2018) (same); Frankola v. La. State Univ. Sch. of Med., No. CV 15-5933, 2017 WL 372520, at *3
(E.D. La. Jan. 26, 2017) (same) ; Windhauser, 360 F. App’x at 566 (two requests for the same
accommodation occurring roughly six years apart must be independently exhausted ). As an

D. Discrimination Claim: Disparate Treatment
Walker asserts another discrimination claim under a disparate treatment theory of liability,
contending that AmeriGas constructively terminated him because of his disability.  R. Doc. 10 at
13.3 “Disparate-treatment discrimination addresses employment actions that treat an employee
worse than others based on the employee ’s [protected characteristic] .” Cicalese v. Univ. of Tex .
Med. Branch, 924 F.3d 762, 766 (5th Cir. 2019) . “In such disparate -treatment cases, proof and
finding of discriminatory motive is required.” Id.

Walker’s Charge lays out the circumstances surrounding his constructive termination and
accuses AmeriGas of discriminating against him based on his disability. R. Doc. 7 -5 at 1 –2.  It

2. Failure to State a Claim
To state a plausible disparate treatment claim, a plaintiff must plead: (1) an adverse
employment action, and (2) taken against them because of a protected characteristic. Cicalese, 924
F.3d at 767. A plaintiff need not plead direct evidence of discrimination. Id. at 767–86. Nor is a
plaintiff required to make out a prima facie case of discrimination under the McDonnell Douglas
burden-shifting framework or identify a similarly situated comparator at the pleading stage. Id. at
767. A plaintiff must, however, allege enough facts, whether through comparator allegations,
direct statements of discriminatory animus, statistical patterns, or other circumstantial indicators,
to “nudge the discrimination claim across the line from conceivable to plausible.” Id. at 768; see
also Raj v. La. State Univ., 714 F.3d 322, 331 (5th Cir. 2013)
Walker’s Complaint does not allege any facts, either direct or circumstantial, that would
suggest his termination was based on his identity as an individual with a disability, rather than his
need for an accommodation. As such, Walker has failed to state a  claim for disparate treatment .
There is no indication, however, that amendment would be futile. Accordingly, the Court will grant
Walker leave to amend to rectify this deficiency, to the extent he is able.

Walker claims that AmeriGas retaliated against him for his accommodation requests when
it gave him an ultimatum to resume full duties or resign. R. Doc. 10 at 8. Retaliation under the
ADA occurs when an employer takes a materially adverse action against an employee because of
the employee’s engagement in a protected activity. See Strife, 138 F.4th at 249. Walker’s Charge
states that Walker made an accommodation request, AmeriGas denied this request, and that
sometime thereafter AmeriGas required Walker “to return to work full duty or resign.” R. Doc. 7-
3 at 1.  An accommodation request is a protected activity under the ADA. Tabatchnik v. Cont ’l
Airlines, 262 Fed. Appx. 674, 676 (5th Cir. 2008)  (per curiam) (“It is undisputed that making a
request for a reasonable accommodation under the  ADA may constitute engaging in
a protected activity.”). A full -duty-or-resign ultimatum is an adverse employment  action. See
Strife, 138 F.4th at 249. And while the Charge does not expressly link the two, a reasonable
investigator would inquire into the reason for the ultimatum, and in doing so, would uncover its
retaliatory nature, if any. Because a retaliation claim would reasonably fall within the scope of the
EEOC’s investigation, Walker has exhausted this claim.4
2. Failure to State a Claim

A plaintiff is required to plead three elements to state a retaliation claim: “(1) engagement
in an activity protected by the ADA, (2) an adverse employment action, and (3) a causal connection
between the protected activity and the adverse action.” Strife, 138 F.4th at 249 . Walker’s
Complaint identifies his termination as the adverse employment action, and his “continuing request
for accommodations” as his protected activity. R. Doc. 1 at 7. Causation may be inferred from a
“close temporal proximity” between the adverse action and the protected activity . Gorman v.
Verizon Wireless Tex., L.L.C., 753 F.3d 165, 171 (5th Cir.2014) (citation omitted). Walker’s final
accommodation request occurred in the month prior to his discharge. This temporal proximity is
enough, at the motion to dismiss stage, to allow Walker’s retaliation claim to proceed. See Lyons
v. Katy Indep. Sch. Dist ., 964 F.3d 298, 306 (5th Cir. 2020) (“The one -week temporal proximity
between filing the level one grievance and [the adverse employment action] is sufficient evidence
of causality.”), with Besser v. Tex. Gen. Land Off., 834 F. App’x 876, 885 (5th Cir. 2020) (“[T]wo
and one-half months between the protected activity and the adverse employment decision, standing
alone, is not within the “very close” proximity that is necessary to establish causation”). Additional
details, such as the identity of the AmeriGas employees who initiated the full -duty-or-resign
ultimatum are unnecessary.  Twombly, 550 U.S. at 545 (“ detailed factual allegations ” are not
required).
F. ADA Interference
1. Failure to Exhaust Administrative Remedies
An employer violates the ADA’s anti -interference provision when it “coerce[s],
intimidate[s], threaten[s], or interfere[s] with any individual in the exercise or enjoyment of” their
ADA rights or “coerce[s], intimidate[s], threaten[s], or interfere[s] with any individual on account
of his or her having exercised” or “having aided or encouraged” another to exercise their ADA

14

rights. 42 U.S.C. § 12203(b); 29 C .F.R § 1630.12. The Fifth Circuit has identified at least three
elements required to sustain an inference claim: “(1) coercion, intimidation, threats, or interference
toward an individual, (2) on the basis of that individual’s exercise or enjoyment, or having
exercised or  enjoyed, or aiding or encouraging others in exercising or enjoying, (3) any right
protected under the ADA.” Strife, 138 F.4th at 251.
Walker’s Charge raises two forms of interference. First, the Charge states that AmeriGas
told Walker to resume working without an accommodation or resign. R. Doc. 7-5 at 2. The EEOC
has recognized conduct of this kind as a form of unlawful interference. See Equal Emp.
Opportunity Cmm’n, Enforcement Guidance on Relation and Related Issues  (August 25, 2016),
https://www.eeoc.gov/laws/guidance/enforcement-guidance-retaliation-and-related-
issues#_ftnref177  (identifying as an example of inference a “manager threaten[ing] employee with
adverse action if she does not forgo accommodation previously granted ”). A reasonable
investigator would flag the full-duty-or-resign ultimatum as a potential threat to the Walker’s right
to a reasonable accommodation, and a form of interference warranting further investigation.
Second, the Charge alleges that AmeriGas constantly asked for updates on Walker’s
condition and required repeated doctor’s visits. R. Doc. 7 -5 at 1. While an employer is generally
permitted to request medical documentation sufficient to establish that an employee has a disability

2. Failure to State a Claim
Walker’s Complaint plausibly alleges interference  for largely the same reasons . The
Complaint avers that AmeriGas instructed Walker to forgo his accommodation or resign. R. Doc.
1 at 6. It further alleges that AmeriGas management of incessantly “harassed” him for updates on
his condition , R. Doc. 1 at 3 –4, and demanded updated medical information  and a renewed
accommodation request despite previously “verifying his current restrictions”  and receiving the
same accommodation request some months earlier. Id. at 4. Accepted as true, these allegations, at
least at the pleading stage of this litigation, give rise to a plausible interference claim.
G. Motion for a More Definite Statement
As an alternative to dismissal, AmeriGas asks the Court to order Walker to re -plead his
causes of action. AmeriGas notes several deficiencies with the Complaint, including its failure to
identify the legal theories supporting Walker’s discrimination claim and a puzzling statement
about AmeriGas “retaliate[ing] against [Walker] for seeking to permit him to seek leave .” Under
Fed. R. Civ. Pro. 12(e), a party may move for a more definite statement when a pleading to which
a responsive pleading is allowed “is so vague and ambiguous” that it “fails to specify the
allegations in a manner that provides sufficient notice .” Swierkiewicz v. Sorema N. A ., 534 U.S.
506, 514 (2002).
In the Court’s view, the Complaint provides AmeriGas with adequate notice of the claims
against it. The Complaint expressly asserts discrimination, retaliation and interference claims, and
sets forth a coherent account of the facts. The shortcomings AmeriGas identifies are more
appropriately rectified by amendment rather than a more definite statement. Accordingly, Walker
