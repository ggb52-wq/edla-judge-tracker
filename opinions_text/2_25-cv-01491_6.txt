II. Standard of Review
“[A] District Court may dismiss without prejudice a pro se litigant's complaint if it is
frivolous or malicious.” Harris v. United States Dept. of Justice, 680 F.2d 1109, 1111 (5th Cir.
1982) (citing Mitchell v. Beaubouef , 581 F.2d 412, 416 (5th Cir. 1978)). As the Fifth Circuit in
Harris explained:
The two stage procedure that has been adopted in this Circuit for processing
prisoner pro se complaints filed in forma pauperis has full application in the present
context for it gives adequate protection to those not represented by attorneys and
comports with the explicit provisions of 28 U.S.C. [§] 1915. The District Court first
decides whether the litigant meets the economic requirements to proceed in forma

4

pauperis. Then, pursuant to [§] 1915[(e)(2)], the Court may dismiss the complaint
if, upon giving it the liberal reading traditionally granted pro se complaints, it
determines that it is unmeritorious, frivolous or malicious.

680 F.2d at 1111 (internal citations omitted); see also 28 U.S.C. § 1915(e)(2); see also Phillips v.
City of Dallas, 2015 WL 233336, at *4 (N.D. Tex. Jan. 14, 2015) (noting that “a district court may
summarily dismiss a complaint filed in forma pauperis if it concludes that the action” is frivolous,
malicious or fails to state a claim).
 The Court has broad discretion in determining the frivolous nature of the complaint.  See
Cay v. Estelle, 789 F.2d 318 (5th Cir. 1986), modified on other grounds, Booker v. Koonce, 2 F.3d
114 (5th Cir. 1993).  However, the Court may not sua sponte dismiss an action merely because of
questionable legal theories or unlikely factual allegations in the complaint.
Under this statute, a claim is frivolous only when it lacks an arguable basis either in law or
in fact.  Neitzke v. Williams , 490 U.S. 319 (1989); Talib v. Gilley , 138 F.3d 211, 213 (5th Cir.
1998).  A claim lacks an arguable basis in law if it is based on an indisputably meritless legal
theory, such as if the complaint alleges the violation of a legal interest which clearly does not exist.
Harper v. Showers, 174 F.3d 716, 718 (5th Cir. 1999).  It lacks an arguable factual basis only if
the facts alleged are “clearly baseless,” a category encompassing fanciful, fantastic, and delusional
allegations.  Denton v. Hernandez , 504 U.S. 25, 32- 33 (1992); Neitzke, 490 U.S. at 327 -28.
Therefore, the Court must determine whether the plaintiff’s claims are based on an indisputably
meritless legal theory or clearly baseless factual allegations.  Reeves v. Collins, 27 F.3d 174, 176
(5th Cir. 1994); see Jackson v. Vannoy, 49 F.3d 175, 176-77 (5th Cir. 1995); Moore v. Mabus, 976
F.2d 268, 269 (5th Cir. 1992).

III. Analysis
A. 42 U.S.C. § 1983
i. New Orleans Police Department – Fifth District
Amacker first alleges that the  New Orleans Police Department -Fifth District (“NOPD”)
violated 42 U.S.C. § 1983 for  refusing to accept her complaint of a crime by Dannye Jones, her
landlord, who allegedly entered her apartment without her permission to plant stolen evidence. R.
Doc. 1 at 5. Amacker complains the NOPD instead aided her neighbors and Dannye Jones, her
landlord, to “stalk and target” her. Id. at 6. The complaint further alleges the NOPD conspired with
her landlord and the manager of Brooks Grocery & De li to obtain a mutual agenda of retaliation
and an abuse of power. Id.
To recover under § 1983, a plaintiff must identify both the constitutional violation and the
responsible person acting under color of state law.  See Flagg Bros., Inc. v. Brooks, 436 U.S. 149,
156 (1978); Polk County v. Dodson, 454 U.S. 312 (1981).
However, the New Orleans Police Department is not a person for purposes of suit under §
1983.  See 42 U.S.C. § 1983 (2006); Will v. Mich. Dep’t of St. Police , 491 U.S. 58 (1989). There
is no law that attributes personality to the NOPD, and Courts in this District have consistently held
that the NOPD lacks juridical capacity.  See, e.g., Winn v. New Orleans City, 919 F. Supp. 2d 743,
750 (E.D. La. 2013); Cook v. New Orleans Police Dep’t , No. 13-6324, 2014 U.S. Dist. LEXIS
60649, at *3 (E.D. La. May 1, 2014).  Therefore, Amacker’s claim against the NOPD- Fifth District
should be dismissed as frivolous.
ii. Dannye Jones and Brooks Grocery & Deli
Amacker also asserts that Dannye Jones and Brooks Grocery & Deli acted under the color
of the law when they conspired with the New Orleans Police Department to unlawfully enter her

unit to plant stolen evidence, which the complaint defines as store keys from Brooks Grocery,
inside her home. R. Doc. 1 at 7. Amacker complains that Defendants’ conduct violates her civil
rights and caused her and her child emotional distress as a result of this alleged criminal
conspiracy. Id.
In order for a private person’s conduct to make him a state actor under Section 1983, that
conduct must be fairly attributed to the state.  Lugar v. Edmonson Oil Co. 457 U.S. 922, 102 S. Ct.
2744, 73 L. Ed. 2d 482 (1982); Richard v. Hoechst Celanese Chemical Group, Inc., 355 F.3d 345,
352 (5th Cir. 2003).  One example is if the private person is performing a public function that is
exclusively reserved to the state. See Flagg Bros., Inc., 436 U.S. at 158.
Another example is when the state exerts coercive power over the private person or
provides significant encouragement.  Cornish v. Correctional Services Corp., 402 F.3d 545, 549-
50 (5th Cir. 2005); Cornish v. Correctional Services Corp., 402 F.3d 545, 549-50(5th Cir. 2005);
Webb v. Police Juror of Claiborne Parish, No. 19- 1252, 2019 U.S. Dist. LEXIS 227181, at *6
(W.D. La. Oct. 4, 2019). Lastly , state action by the private person can be found if the state has
inserted itself into a position of interdependence with the private person such that it wa s a joint
participant in the action. See id.
In this case, Dannye Jones and Brooks Grocery & Deli are private actors, so their conduct
would have to attributable to the State based on the three bases listed above. However, the Court
needs not to delve into any basis in this context because Amacker has failed to identity a proper
state actor. As noted previously, the Court established that the NOPD -Fifth District is not the
proper party here, so Amacker cannot establish any State interdependence with Jones and Brooks
Grocery & Deli. For these reasons, Amacker’s § 1983 claims against Jones and Brooks Grocery

B. Violence Against Women Act (VAWA)
Amacker also asserts a claim under the Violence Against Women Act pursuant to 34 U.S.C.
§ 12471. It is unclear which Defendant she purports to assert the claim against based on the
complaint and the basis for her claim. Nevertheless, the Violence Against Women Act confers no
private right of action. See United States v. Morrison, 529 U.S. 598, 627, 120 S. Ct. 1740, 146 L.
Ed. 2d 658 (2000). Therefore , it is recommended that Amacker’s VAWA claim be dismissed as
frivolous and for failure to state a claim upon which relief could be granted.
C. Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act and Americans with Disabilities Act
Next, Amacker asserts that Dannye Jones, her landlord, violated Section 504 of the
Rehabilitation Act (“RA”) and the Americans with Disabilities Act (“ADA”). R. Doc. 1 at 6.
Amacker generally claims that Dannye Jones ignored her disability accommodations. Id.  The
complaint does not identity any disability Amacker suffers from as well as explain what reasonable
accommodations she was seeking due to this ability.
The Rehabilitation Act, in pertinent part, states that “no otherwise qualified individual with
a disability in the United States … shall, solely by reason of her or his disability… be denied the
benefits of…any program or activity receiving Federal financial assistance….” 29 U.S.C. § 794(a).
On the other hand, the ADA provides that “[n]o qualified individual with a disability shall by
reason of such disability, be excluded from participation in or be denied the benefits of the services,
programs, or activities of a public entity, or be subjected to discrimination by any such entity.” 42
U.S.C. § 12132.

 The ADA and the RA are judged and evaluated under the same legal standards, and the
same remedies are available under both. Kemp v. Holder , 610 F.3d 231, 234 (5th Cir. 2010). The
ADA and the RA permits claims only against departments, agencies, and/or instrumentalities of a
State and does not confer liability on private individuals. See Lollar v. Baker , 196 F.3d 603, 609
(5th Cir. 1999); Smith v. Hood, 900 F.3d 180, 184 n.6 (5th Cir. 2018).
To prevail on a failure to accommodate claim, the plaintiff must prove (1) she is a qualified
individual with a disability, (2) the disability is known by the defendant; and (3) the defendant
failed to make reasonable accommodations. Providence Behavioral Health v. Grant Rd. Pub. Util.
Dist., 902 F.3d 448, 459 (5th Cir. 2018).
 First, the ADA and RA require that a person seeking relief under such provisions provide
that they are a “qualified individual with a disability.” 42 U.S.C. § 12132; 29 U.S.C. §  794(a).
Here, Amacker fails to assert a disability at all and generally alleges that her landlord ignored her
accommodations. Amacker also fails to specify what accommodations she was seeking and how
such requests were denied. Further, Amacker seeks to assert the claims against her landlord, whom
is a private individual, which is not permitted by the ADA or R A. Therefore, Amacker’s claims
under the Rehabilitation Act and Americans with Disabilities Act are also frivolous and should be
dismissed as frivolous and for failure to state a claim upon which relief can be granted.
D. Title VI of the Civil Rights Act
 Amacker lastly identifies a claim under Title VI of the Civil Rights Act against Brooks
Grocery & Deli. 42 U.S.C. § 2000(d) et seq; R. Doc. 1 at 8.  Amacker claims Brooks Grocery &
Deli violated her civil rights when they denied services to her while benefiting from federal funds
like SNAP/EBT. Id. It is unclear the nature of the relief Amacker seeks against Brooks. In the
broadest sense, Amacker is seeking a declaratory judgment.

 Title 42 U.S.C. § 2000(d ) et seq. forbids any person or institution which receives federal
funds to discriminate on the basis of race, color or national origin.  Cannon v. University of
Chicago, 441 U.S. 677, 704, 99 S. Ct. 1946, 1961, 60 L. Ed. 2d. 560, 579 (1979). “The purpose of
Title VI is twofold: 1) to avoid the use of federal funds to support discriminatory practices and 2)
to provide individual citizens effective protection against these practices.” Drayden v. Needville
Indep. Sch. Dist., 642 F.2d 129, 132 (5th Cir. 1981).
 In reviewing the allegations set forth by Amacker , she generally alleges that she and her
toddler were “often” treated unfairly compared to other customers when she tried to purchase
items. She provides no further explanation regarding the unfair treatment that she labelled
discriminatory. Without more, these allegations fail to state a claim for which relief may be granted
and is therefore frivolous. It is therefore recommended that the Title 42 U.S.C. § 2000(d) et seq
claim against Brooks Grocery & Deli, LLC should be dismissed as frivolous and for failure to state
a claim upon which relief can be granted.
