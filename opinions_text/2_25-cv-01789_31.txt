ORDER AND REASONS
 Before the Court is the  Second Motion to Dismiss for Lack of Subject Matter
Jurisdiction, and in the Alternative, Failure to State a Claim  by Defendant the
United States Department of Housing and Urban Development .1 Plaintiff Jennifer
Lynne Carver filed an opposition to the motion,2 and HUD filed a reply. 3 The Court
ordered supplemental briefing to address whether Carver has Article III standing, 4
which the parties provided.5 Having considered the parties’ memoranda, the record,
and the applicable law, the Court issues this Order and Reasons granting HUD’s
motion to dismiss because Carver lacks Article III standing.

II. Article III Standing
 Federal courts are courts of limited jurisdiction and possess only the authority
conferred upon them by the United States Constitution and Congress.18 Therefore,
the Court has an “independent obligation to determine whether subject -matter
jurisdiction exists, even in the absence of a challenge from any party. ”19 Federal
courts’ subject-matter jurisdiction is restricted to “Cases” and “Controversies.”20 This
constitutional limitation on judicial power ensures that courts do not encroach on the
powers of the political branches in our system of government.21
“[A] lawsuit is not a ’ Case[ ]’ or ‘Controvers[y]’ unless the plaintiff can prove
that [she] has standing to bring suit.22 To have standing, a plaintiff must show that
she has suffered an injury in fact “fairly traceable” to the defendant’s conduct which

A. Traceability of the Alleged Injuries to HUD’s Conduct
 To satisfy the second element of standing, Carver must show that her injuries
are fairly traceable to HUD ’s conduct, and not “ the result of the independent action
of some third party not before the court. ”26 “[T]he presence ... of third -party links in
[a] causal chain [can] independently corroborate that [a party’s] claim of causation is
‘entirely speculative’ and insufficient for standing.”27 Thus, the operative question is
whether HUD’s conduct was the cause of the injuries Carver suffered.

B. Redressability of the Requested Relief
 E ven if Carver could establish traceability, she still fails to establish standing
because a favorable judgment would not redress her alleged injury.
 Redressability asks, for each form of relief a plaintiff seeks, whether it is likely,
as opposed to merely speculative, that the plaintiff's injury will be remedied by the
relief she requests.39 Here, Carver vaguely seeks declaratory and injunctive relief
concerning HUD ’s administrative review procedures . In Count I (Administrative
Procedures Act), she alleges the agency arbitrarily failed to review her evidence of
unsafe housing conditions.40 In Count II (Procedural Due Process), she alleges HUD
violated her protected property interest in the voucher program by refusing to
intervene or grant her a meaningful administrative review before her housing
assistance was terminated.41
 Even setting aside the prospective nature of the relief sought for a past injury,
an order running to HUD could not redress Carver’ s injury, because HUD lacks the
authority to provide that remedy, i.e. , restoration of her voucher, a hearing, or a
change in the determinations that ended her participation. Those matters are
committed to the local public housing authority.42 As the court explained in Robbins,
“[i]t is substantially more difficult for a [plaintiff] to establish redressability where
