LEGAL STANDARD
A. Rule 12(b)(1)
The Court must consider Defendant’s challenge to subject matter jurisdiction
under Rule 12(b)(1) before deciding the other Rule 12 motions to dismiss because
jurisdiction is a threshold determination. See In re Great Lakes Dredge & Dock Co.,
LLC, 624 F.3d 201, 209 (5th Cir. 2010). When deciding a motion to dismiss for lack of
subject matter jurisdiction under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(1), “the
district court is ‘free to weigh the evidence and resolve factual disputes in order to
satisfy itself that it has the power to hear the case.’” Krim v. pcOrder.com, Inc., 402
F.3d 489, 494 (5th Cir. 2005). The party asserting jurisdiction must carry the burden
of proof to defeat a Rule 12(b)(1) motion to dismiss. Randall D. Walcott, M.D., P.A.
v. Sebelius, 635 F.3d 757, 762 (5th Cir. 2011) (citation omitted). The standard of

review for a Rule 12(b)(1) motion to dismiss is the same as that for a motion to dismiss
pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6).
B. Rule 12(b)(6)
To survive a Rule 12(b)(6) motion to dismiss, the plaintiff must plead sufficient
facts to “state a claim to relief that is plausible on its face.” Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S.
662, 678 (2009) (quoting Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly, 550 U.S. 544, 570 (2007)). A claim
is facially plausible when the plaintiff pleads facts that allow the court to “draw the
reasonable inference that the defendant is liable for the misconduct alleged.” Id. The
factual allegations in the complaint “must be enough to raise a right to relief above
the speculative level.” Twombly, 550 U.S. at 555. “[D]etailed factual allegations” are
not required, but the pleading must p resent “more than an unadorned, the -
defendant-unlawfully-harmed-me accusation.” Iqbal, 556 U.S. at 678. The court must
accept all well-pleaded facts as true and must draw all reasonable inferences in favor
of the plaintiff. Lormand v. US Unwired, Inc. , 565 F.3d 228, 232 (5th Cir. 2009).
However, “conclusory allegations or legal conclusions masquerading as factual
conclusions will not suffice to prevent a motion to dismiss.” Beavers v. Metro. Life Ins.
Co., 566 F.3d 436, 439 (5th Cir. 2009) (citation omitted).

DISCUSSION
I. Motion to Dismiss for Lack of Subject Matter Jurisdiction Under 12(b)(1)
First, Gallagher claims that the Court lacks subject matter jurisdiction
because his complaint to the OIPM alleging Cockerham’s misconduct is not
sufficiently connected to Cockerham’s First and Fourteenth Amendment claims to

6

support supplemental juris diction. Id. at 14. Gallagher asserts that Cockerham’s
allegations of malicious misconduct on behalf of Kirkpatrick and the NOPD do not
involve him and relate  to a totally separate investigation. Id. Because the claims
against Gallagher, Kirkpatrick, and the NOPD do not arise from a common nucleus
of operative facts, Gallagher contends the claims against him should be dismissed
under Rule 12(b)(1). Id.
Conversely, Cockerham maintains that supplemental  jurisdiction exists
because Gallagher’s payroll fraud accusations prompted Cockerham’s work history,
payroll records, paid- detail activity, and professional integrity to be investigated ,
beginning a “continuing course of retaliatory and disciplinary conduct that adversely
affected his employment, reputation, earnings, and career.” (Rec. Doc. 26, at 5, 20).
In essence, Cockerham’s reasoning relies heavily on the chronology of events leading
up to his termination.
Subject matter jurisdiction is defined as “the courts’ statutory or constitutional
power to adjudicate [a] case.” Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env. , 523 U.S. 83, 89
(1998) (citations omitted). Under 28 U.S.C.  § 1331, “[t]he district courts shall have
original jurisdiction of all civil actions arising under the Constitution, laws, or
treaties of the United States.” Cockerham’s First and Fourteenth Amendment claims
fall squarely within this standard, successfully establishing subject matter
jurisdiction. However, Cockerham asserts state law claims of infliction of emotional
distress and negligence against Gallagher. To hear state law claims in federal court,
the Court must exercise supplemental jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1367 and attach
Case 2:26-cv-00647-CJB-DPC     Document 31     Filed 11/12/26     Page 6 of 11
7

Cockerham’s state law claims to his federal First and Fourteenth Amendment claims.
See 28 U.S.C. § 1367(a). “The question under section 1367(a) is whether the
supplemental claims are so related to the original claims that they form part of the
same case or controversy.” Mendoza v . Murphy, 532 F.3d 342, 346 (5th Cir. 2008)
(citation omitted).
To exercise supplemental jurisdiction, Cockerham’s state and federal claims
must derive from a common nucleus of operative fact s. United Mine Workers of Am.
v. Gibbs, 383 U.S. 715, 725 (1966). “[I]f, considered without regard to their federal or
state character, a plaintiff’s claims are such that he would be expected to try them all
in one judicial proceeding, then . . . there is power in federal courts to hear the whole.”
Id. Historically, the Fifth Circuit has affirmed this standard. See S J Associated
Pathologists, P.L.L.C. v. Cigna Healthcare of Tex., Inc., 964 F.3d 369, 373–74 (5th Cir.
2020) (holding that a connection had not been established between a federal security
claim against a defendant and state law claims against a separate insurance company
where the claims arose from two separate, unrelated controversies and, “even if tried
together, would be decided on evidence that would have no bearing on the claims
asserted against the other”). Therefore, to determine whether Cockerham’s claims
against Gallagher and the other defendants form part of the same case or controversy,
the Court must consider whether the claims  derive from a common nucleus of
operative facts such that it would be expected to try them all in the same proceeding.
 The Court finds Gallagher’s involvement in Cockerham’s demotion and
eventual termination to be insufficient. Gallagher’s role in this controversy is, at best,
Case 2:26-cv-00647-CJB-DPC     Document 31     Filed 11/12/26     Page 7 of 11
8

remote. Cockerham asks the Court to agree that Gallagher’s complaint is sufficiently
connected to the investigations into Cockerham’s conduct relating  to the NOPD
cheating controversy.  However, Gallagher’s allegations related to  only one topic:
payroll fraud. It is true that Gallagher’s complaint prompted an investigation. But
the harm Cockerham suffered was the result of an entirely separate investigation,
focusing solely on his  potential involvement with the NOPD cheating controversy .
(Rec. Doc. 1, at 9, 14 –15). Effectively connecting Gallagher’s complaint to any
subsequent discipline, demotion, or termination would require evidence of a direct
link, which Cockerham fails to establish . Cockerham provides no evidence that he
was disciplined  because of Gallagher’s reporting of payroll fraud.  The evidence
presented concerning Gallagher’s complaint has no bearing on his claims against the
other defendants. See S J Associated Pathologists, P.L.L.C., 964 F.3d at 373–74.
Cockerham relies on the mere assumption that Gallagher’s OIPM complaint
began a chain reaction that led to his termination to establish him as a defendant.
However, a baseless assumption alone does not establish supplemental jurisdiction .
See Ill. Cent. Gulf R.R. Co. v. Pargas, Inc. , 706 F.2d 633, 636 (5th Cir. 1983) (“[I]t is
generally agreed that the basis upon which jurisdiction depends . . . cannot be
established argumentatively or by mere inference.”) (citations omitted) (citation
modified). Therefore, the Court finds that there is a lack of subject matter jurisdiction
over Cockerham’s claims against Gallagher and declines to exercise supplemental
jurisdiction.

II. Motion to Dismiss for Failure to State a Claim Under 12(b)(6)
Next, Gallagher urges the Court to dismiss Cockerham’s claims pursuant to
Rule 12(b)(6) for failure to state a claim on which relief can be granted. Even if
Cockerham’s claims against Gallagher were to survive dismissal under Rule 12(b)(1),
Cockerham fails to state a valid claim against Gallagher under Rule 12(b)(6).
Gallagher argues Cockerham fails to allege sufficient facts supporting the
elements necessary to properly state a negligence claim against him. (Rec. Doc. 17-1,
at 7). Specifically, Gallagher argues that Cockerham fails to plead which defendants
breached their duty of care or which specific duty was allegedly breached. Id.  at 9.
Further, Gallagher asserts that the chain of causation was broken when third-party
investigators intervened to conduct an independent investigation. Id. at 10–11.
In response, Cockerham asserts his complaint contains specific allegations and
that Gallagher was given fair notice of the claims against him . (Rec. Doc. 26, at 12).
Cockerham alleges Gallagher made “negligent and/or grossly negligent false
statements about [him] which spurred baseless investigations and caused emotional
injury . . . .” (Rec. Doc. 1, at 19).1 Cockerham fails to elaborate further or present facts
explaining why or how Gallagher’s negligent action caused him emotional injury.
Cockerham’s reasoning relies on inference instead of fact. Cockerham contends
Gallagher’s OIPM complaint led to Cockerham’s demotion and termination and

The allegations Cockerham makes against Gallagher in his  complaint
resemble precisely the kind of  “unadorned, the -defendant-unlawfully-harmed-me”

Cockerham’s negligence and infliction  of emotional distress claims a re merely a

in his opposition to support his assertions. (Rec. Doc. 26). Therefore, the Court cannot
reasonably infer that Gallagher is liable for the misconduct Cockerham alleges. See
Iqbal, 556 U.S. at 678 (quoting Twombly, 550 U.S. at 570).
Gallagher correctly asserts that Cockerham’s complaint alleg es no viable
claims against him.  The Court agrees with  Gallagher’s contention that he  is not a
proper defendant here and finds that Cockerham does not state any plausible claim
for relief against him.

III. Motion to Dismiss for Insufficient Service of Process Under 12(b)(5)
Lastly, Gallagher asserts Cockerham’s claims against him should be dismissed
because Cockerham does not claim to have served Gallagher within ninety days, and
that he in fact did not serve Gallagher at all. (Rec. Doc. 17 -1, at 14). Because
Cockerham’s claims against Gallagher do not survive a Rule 12(b)(1) or 12(b)(6)
analysis, Gallagher’s motion to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(5) of the Federal Rules of
Civil Procedure is effectively moot. The Court declines to address Cockerham’s service
of process because, as the pleadings presently stand, Gallagher is not a proper
defendant and the claims against him are invalid.
CONCLUSION
Accordingly,
 IT IS ORDERED  that Gallagher’s Motion to Dismiss for Lack of Subject
Matter Jurisdiction, Failure to State a Claim, and Lack of Proper Service (Rec. Doc.
17) is GRANTED, and Plaintiff Cockerham’s claims against Gallagher are
DISMISSED without prejudice.
IT IS FURTHER ORDERED that Plaintiff’s Motion to Confirm Validity of
Service of Process Upon Defendant Charles “Skip” Gallagher and to Establish
Responsive-Pleading Deadline (Rec. Doc. 16) is DENIED as moot.
