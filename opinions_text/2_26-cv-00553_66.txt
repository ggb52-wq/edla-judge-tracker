ORDER AND REASONS
 Before the Court  is a motion 1 by Plaintiff Edward Delaune to  remand his
lawsuit to Orleans Parish Civil District Court. No opposition has been filed. Because
Plaintiff voluntarily dismissed his claims against Huntington Ingalls Incorporated
f/k/a Avondale Shipyards, Inc., effectively amending his lawsuit to remove the
singular jurisdictional anchor, the Court lacks jurisdiction, so the motion to remand
is GRANTED.
I.  BACKGROUND
Edward Delaune alleges that he was diagnosed with lung cancer  due to
exposure to asbestos. He  filed suit in state court against 12 defendants, including
Huntington Ingalls Incorporated f/k/a Avondale Shipyards, Inc. (“Avondale”). In his
state-court petition, Plaintiff alleged that he was exposed to asbestos, in part, because
his father brought home asbestos fibers on his clothes when he worked as a welder at
Avondale from 1959 to 1969. Avondale removed the case to this Court under 28 U.S.C.
§ 1442(a)(1), the federal officer removal statute.

II.  LAW AND ANALYSIS
The Federal Officer Removal Statute authorizes removal of a suit by the
“United States or any agency thereof (or any person acting under that officer) of the
United States or any agency thereof, in an official or individual capacity, for or
relating to any  color of such office [.]” 28 U.S.C. § 1442(a)(1). Suits against federal
officers may be removed if a defense depends on federal law, even if plaintiff’s
complaint does not assert a federal claim. Jefferson Cnty., Ala. v. Acker, 527 U.S. 423,
431 (1999), superseded in part by statutory amendment as indicated in Chevron USA
Incorporated v. Plaquemine s Parish, 608 U.S. 1,  11 n.3 (2026). To establish federal
jurisdiction under the federal-officer removal statute, the defendant must show: “(1)
it has asserted a colorable federal defense, (2) it is a ‘person’ within the meaning of
the statute, (3) that has acted pursuant to a federal officer ’s directions, and (4) the
charged conduct is connected or associated with an act pursuant to a federal officer’s
directions.” Latiolais v. Huntington Ingalls, Inc., 951 F.3d 286, 296 (5th Cir. 2020) (en
banc).

Although federal courts are courts of limited jurisdiction, “federal officer
removal under 28 U.S.C. § 1442 is unlike other removal doctrines: it is not narrow or
limited.” State v. Kleinert, 855 F.3d 305, 311 (5th Cir. 2017); Howery v. Allstate Ins.
Co., 243 F.3d 912, 916 (5th Cir. 2001). “[U]nlike the general removal statute, the
federal officer removal statute is to be broadly construed in favor of a federal forum.”
See Williams v. Lockheed Martin Corp. , 990 F.3d 852, 859 (5th Cir. 2021) ; see also
Martin v. LCMC Health Holdings, Inc. , 101 F.4th 410, 414 (5th Cir. 2024)  (unlike
other removal doctrines, the Fifth Circuit “broadly construe[s] the federal officer
removal statute in favor of a federal forum”).
It is undisputed that Avondale properly removed the case under this federal -
officer removal framework and that this Court had subject matter jurisdiction at the
time of removal given the federal officer defense  advanced. However, Plaintiff has
since discovered that his father was never employed at Avondale such that he has no
basis for  pursuing asbestos-exposure allegations against Avondale  as originally
alleged in his state court petition.  He concedes he has no basis to recover against
Avondale. With Avondale’s blessing, h e accordingly dismissed his claims against
Avondale, eliminating the jurisdictional anchor —Avondale’s alleged “acting under”
status. Having done so, invoking  Royal Canin U.S.A., Inc. v. Wullschleger , 604 U.S.
22 (2025), Plaintiff contends that remand is mandatory  because his state -court
petition alleges only state -law claims and the jurisdictional anchor “has been
eliminated entirely.”3 Because Plaintiff’s claims against Avondale are “gone for good,”

Plaintiff has eliminated the claims that anchored this Court’s removal jurisdiction,

In Royal Canin, the plaintiff originally filed a complaint containing federal and
state law claims. Invoking federal-question jurisdiction, the defendant removed  the
lawsuit to federal court. Post-removal, the plaintiff deleted the federal claims by filing
an amended complaint and moved to remand.  The district court ultimately denied
the motion to remand , but the Eighth Circuit vacated , ordering that the case be
remanded. The Supreme Court affirmed, holding that when a plaintiff amends her
complaint to eliminate the federal claims that predicated removal to federal court,
leaving only state -law claims behind, the federal court loses supplemental
jurisdiction over the state claims, and the case must be remanded to state court. 604
U.S. at 25, 30.
To be sure, the Supreme Court was presented with the plaintiff’s post-removal
amendment to a complaint to eliminate federal claims  and whether such an
amendment could divest the federal court of its once -assumed federal and
supplemental jurisdiction . Id. at 25 (“This case presents [this] question: What
happens if, after removal, the plaintiff amends her complaint to delete all the federal-
law claims, leaving nothing but state -law claims behind? May the federal court still
adjudicate the now purely-state-law suit? We hold that it may not.”). In analyzing an
amendment’s impact on jurisdiction, the Supreme Court necessarily consulted the
supplemental jurisdiction statute , observing that:  “Section 1367 contemplates that

when an amended complaint is filed, the jurisdictional basis for the suit is reviewed
anew. If nothing in the amended complaint now falls ‘within [the federal court’s]
original jurisdiction,’ then neither does anything fall within the court’s ‘supplemental
jurisdiction.’” Id. at 30. (citing  § 1367(a)). In other words, “ there is no discretion to
decline supplemental jurisdiction . . . because there is no supplemental jurisdiction
at all. Once the plaintiff has ditched all claims involving federal questions, the
leftover state claims are supplemental to nothing —and § 1367(a) does not authorize
a federal court to resolve them. ” Id. at 33-34. Thus, where the plaintiff “amends her
complaint to withdraw the federal claims” that conferred federal-question jurisdiction
over a case removed pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1441, the lawsuit must be remanded to
state court. Id. at 33 , 39 (“By adding or subtracting claims or parties, and thus
reframing the suit, that pleading can alter a federal court's authority.”).
Notably, Royal Canin distinguishes between claims “dismissed by the district
court, as in § 1367(c)(3),” which “may yet [be] revive[d]” by a court of appeals, and
those that may not be revived—those that are “gone for good.” Id. at 33. In the former
dismissed-but-may-be-revived category , where a plaintiff’s federal claims are
dismissed on the merits or otherwise dismissed against the plaintiff’s wishes (e.g.,
when the court grants a defendant’s motion to dismiss or for summary judgment), the
district court may retain supplemental jurisdiction over the remaining claims,
considering that the jurisdiction -conferring dismissed claims may be revived.  See,
e.g., Allen v. City of New York, No. 24-2589, 2025 WL 3152723, at *2 (2d Cir. Nov. 12,
2025) (summary order) (concluding that the district court did not abuse its discretion

Where, as here,  the claims serving as the federal jurisdictional anchor  are
“gone for good” from the case, and may not be re vived on appeal, the Court lacks
discretion or ability to exercise supplemental jurisdiction over the remaining state
law claims. Royal Canin , 604 U.S. at 33; see also Hain Celestial Grp., Inc. v.
Palmquist, 607 U.S. 421, 431 (2026) (“[The defendant], however, was not dismissed
correctly, and the interlocutory nature of that dismissal meant that it was reversible
on appeal from the final judgment in [the plaintiff's] favor. [The defendant] thus was
only temporarily and erroneously removed from the case; it was not ‘gone for good.’
When the Fifth Circuit reversed the [d]istrict [c]ourt's error, it restored [the

Here, the dismissed, jurisdiction -conferring claim (Plaintiff’s claim against
ostensible federal-officer Avondale) may not be revived on appeal. Thus, even though
Plaintiff did not formally amend his complaint to eliminate his claims against
Avondale from his lawsuit, he has effectively done so , and this case no longer has a
federal anchor for jurisdiction.
III . CONCLUSION
 Though supplemental jurisdiction may remain discretionary where a plaintiff's
federal claims have been involuntarily dismissed (with the potential to be revived),
such as when a defendant’s motion to dismiss or for summary judgment has been
granted, that is not the case here. Here, the dismissed, jurisdiction-conferring claim
(Plaintiff’s claim against ostensible federal-officer Avondale) is gone for good and may
not be revived on appeal. Thus, even though Plaintiff did not formally amend his
complaint to remove his claims against Avondale from his lawsuit, he has effectively
done so by affirmatively securing dismissal of his claims against Avondale, once he
discovered that he had no valid claim against the ostensible federal officer. Where as
here, the dismissed, jurisdiction-conferring claim may not be revived on appeal, the
Court lacks jurisdiction and may not exercise supplemental jurisdiction over the
remaining state law claims (absent some other basis for jurisdiction). Accordingly,
