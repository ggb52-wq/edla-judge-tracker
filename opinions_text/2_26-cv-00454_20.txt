II. LEGAL STANDARD
The Federal Arbitration Act (“FAA”), 9 U.S.C. §§ 1–16, expresses a
strong federal policy in favor of enforcing arbitration agreements.  See, e.g.,
Dean Witter Reynolds, Inc. v. Byrd, 470 U.S. 213, 217–18 (1985); Southland
Corp. v. Keating , 465 U.S. 1, 10 (1984); Moses H. Cone Mem’l Hosp. v.
Mercury Constr. Corp. , 460 U.S. 1, 24 (1983); Safer v. Nelson Fin. Group,
Inc., 422 F.3d 289, 294 (5th Cir. 2005).  Under the FAA, a written agreement
to arbitrate “shall be valid, irrevocabl e, and enforceable, save upon such
grounds as exist at law or in equity for the revocation of any contract.”

9 U.S.C. § 2.  This provision reflects  “both a liberal fede ral policy favoring
arbitration, and the fundamental princi ple that arbitration is a matter of
contract.”  AT&T Mobility LLC v. Concepcion, 563 U.S. 333, 339 (2011).
Section 3 of the FAA further provides that if any suit is brought in
federal court “upon any issue referable to arbitration,” the court, “upon being
satisfied that the issue involved in such suit or proceeding is referable to
arbitration . . . , shall on application of one of the parties stay the trial of the
action until such arbitration has been had in accordance with the terms of
the agreement.”  9 U.S.C. § 3.  The Supreme Court has directed courts to
“rigorously enforce” the terms of arbitration agreements.  Am. Express Co.
v. Italian Colors Rest., 570 U.S. 228, 233 (2013) (quoting Dean Witter, 470
U.S. at 221).  The Act “leaves no plac e for the exercise of discretion by a
district court” and requires district courts to order the parties to proceed to
arbitration on issues covered by the arbitration agreement.  Dean Witter ,
470 U.S. at 218.  Further, any doubts about the scope of an arbitration
agreement are to be resolved in favor of arbitration.  Safer, 422 F.3d at 294.
To determine whether to compel th e parties to arbitrate, the Court
must conduct a two-step inquiry.  Id. at 293.  First, the Court must determine
whether the parties agreed to arbi trate the disputes in question.  Id.  This
determination ordinarily involves two considerations: (1) whether there is a

valid agreement to arbitrate; and (2 ) whether the dispute falls within the
scope of that arbitration agreement.  Id.  But when, as here, the parties’
agreement contains a delegation clause, the Court simply analyzes whether
there is a valid agreement, and if so, whether there is “clear and unmistakable
evidence” that the parties intended to arbitrate the arbitrability issue.  See
Lloyd’s Syndicate 457 v. FloaTEC, L.L.C., 921 F.3d 508, 514 (5th Cir. 2019).
Second, if the Court determines that the parties have agreed to arbitrate the
dispute in question, it then must ask whether “any federal statute or policy
renders the claim nonarbitrable.”  Sherer v. Green Tree Servicing LLC, 548
F.3d 379, 381 (5th Cir. 20 08).  But if there is no  argument that a federal
statute or policy bars arbitration, the Court need not analyze the second step.
Id.
If the Court finds that the making of the agreement for arbitration or
the failure to comply with it are not in  issue, it must order the parties to
proceed to arbitration.  9 U.S.C. § 4.  If the Court finds that the dispute is
subject to arbitration and a party has requested a stay pending arbitration,
the Court must issue the stay.  Id. § 3; see also Smith v. Spizzirri , 601 U.S.
472, 475–76 (2024) (holding that courts  lack “discretion to dismiss the suit
on the basis that all the claims are subject to arbitration”).

The movant seeking to compel arbitr ation bears the initial burden of
proving that an arbitration agreemen t exists by a preponderance of the
evidence.  Chester v. DirecTV, L.L.C., 607 F. App’x 362, 364 (5th Cir. 2015)
(citing Banks v. Mitsubishi Moto rs Credit of Am., Inc. , 435 F.3d 538, 540
(5th Cir. 2005) (per curiam)).  If a valid arbitration ag reement exists, the
party resisting arbitration has the burden  to prove that the claims at issue
are not amenable to arbitration.  Green Tree Fin. Corp.-Ala. v. Randolph ,
531 U.S. 79, 91 (2000).

III. DISCUSSION
In determining whether there is a valid agreement to arbitrate, the
Court must apply ordinary state- law principles of contract.  Will-Drill Res.
Inc. v. Samson Res. Co. , 352 F.3d 211, 218 (5th Cir. 2003); see also Klein v.
Nabors Drilling USA L.P. , 710 F.3d 234, 236 (5th Cir. 2013) (“Because
arbitration is simply a matter of cont ract between the parties, the strong
federal policy favoring arbitration does not apply to the initial determination
of whether there is a valid agreement to  arbitrate.”).  The parties agree that
Louisiana contract law applies here.17

Under Louisiana law, formation of a valid contract requires four
elements: “(1) the parties must posse ss the capacity to contract; (2) the
parties’ mutual consent must be free ly given; (3) there must be a certain
object for the contract; and (4) the con tract must have a lawful purpose.”
Provenza v. Cent. & Sw. Servs., Inc. , 775 So. 2d 84, 89 (L a. Ct. App. 2 Cir.
2000) (citing La. Civ. Code Ann. arts. 1918, 1927, 1966, 1977).
Here, the record shows that plaintiff electronically agreed to the terms
of the DRA, including the arbitration provision, on November 22, 2024.18  In
registering to be a Spark driver, plaintiff was presented with the terms of the
DRA and then had to click “I agree” and provide her signature and contact
information.19  The following notice was displayed directly above the
signature box:

Courts in this circuit have held that  this form of notice and consent,
known as a “clickwrap agreement,” 21 suffices to bind individuals to the
displayed terms and conditions under Louisiana law.  See, e.g., Hughes v.
Uber Techs., Inc., 718 F.Supp.3d 571, 579–80 (E.D. La. 2024); Express Lien,
Inc. v. Handle, Inc. , 2021 WL 2476853, at *4 (E.D. La. June 17, 2021)
(collecting cases) (noting that this fi nding “is consistent with Louisiana law
holding that ‘signatures are not mere ornaments’ and that ‘a person who
signs a written instrument is presumed  to know its contents and cannot
avoid its obligations by contending that he did not read it, or that it was not
explained or that he did not understand it’”); Deshotel v. PayPal, Inc., 2020
WL 5813322, at *7 (W.D. La. Aug. 24, 2020) (collecting cases upholding
clickwrap agreements).  This conclusi on comports with Louisiana statutory
law, which gives legal effect to both electronic contracts and electronic
signatures.  See La. Rev. Stat. Ann. § 9:2607 .  Thus, a valid arbitration
agreement exists between Walmart and plaintiff.
