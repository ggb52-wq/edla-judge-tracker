I.  Background
Claimant is a 58 year old female. 6 She has a college degree and work
experience as a sales director  and bartender. 7 Claimant applied for disability
insurance benefits on March 21, 2023. 8 In her application documents, Claimant
represented that she suffered from fine motor impairment of her right hand and leg,
aphasia, and impaired cognitive ability . 9 Claimant maintains that a stroke on
February 16, 2023, rendered her disabled by these conditions. 10 After the
Administration denied her application and request for reconsideration,11 a hearing to
review the claim was held at Claimant ’s request. 12 The ALJ issued a decision  on
September 8, 2025, holding that Claimant  was not disabled within the definition of
the SSA13 between February 16, 2023, through the date of the decision.14
The ALJ conducted the five -step sequential analysis required by 20 C.F.R.
§ 404.1520 to consider a claimant’s assertion of disability. As the Fifth Circuit has
explained:

The five steps consider: (1) Is the claimant “doing substantial gainful
activity”? (2) If not, does the claimant “have a severe medically
determinable physical or mental impairment” of sufficient duration? (3)
If so, does her impairment(s) meet or equal a listing in Appendix 1 of the
applicable regulations? (4) If not, considering the claimant’s “residual
functional capacity,” can the claimant still do her “past relevant work”?
(5) If not, can the claimant adjust to other work given her residual
functional capacity, age, education, and work experience?

Vasquez v. O’Malley, 2024 WL 4381269, at *1 (5th Cir. Oct. 3, 2024) (quoting 20 C.F.R.
§ 416.920). At steps one through four, the burden of proof is on the claimant to show
she is disabled. Shave v. Apfel, 238 F.3d 592, 594 (5th Cir. 2001). If the claimant can
carry this burden through step three or four, s he has established disability and the
inquiry is over. Id. Otherwise, the burden shifts to the Commissioner at step five to
prove the claimant nonetheless retains sufficient functional capacity to adjust to
other employment. Id.
The ALJ found that Claimant satisfied steps one and two. First, Claimant had
not engaged in substantial gainful activity since the alleged onset date of her
disability.15 Second, the ALJ found that Claimant suffered from severe impairments,
including “vascular insult to the brain, hypertensive cardiovascular disease, essential
hypertension, osteoarthritis, obstructive sleep apnea, depressive disorder, anxiety
disorder, and post -traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) (20 CFR 404.1520(c)) .”16 T he
ALJ determined, however, that Claimant did not satisfy step three because none of
these impairments or any combination thereof met or equaled an impairment listed

ALJ’s decision, and whether the ALJ applied the proper legal standards when
evaluating the evidence.” Taylor v. Astrue, 706 F.3d 600, 602 (5th Cir. 2012).
“Substantial evidence is ‘such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept
as adequate to support a conclusion.’” Greenspan v. Shalala, 38 F.3d 232, 236 (5th
Cir. 1994) (quoting Richardson v. Perales, 402 U.S. 389, 401 (1971)). This must
constitute “more than a mere scintilla” of evidence but may be “less than a
preponderance.” Spellman v. Shalala, 1 F.3d 357, 360 (5th Cir. 1993). The Court “may
not reweigh the evidence in the record, nor try the issues de novo, nor substitute [its]
judgment . . . even if [the Court concludes that] the evidence preponderates against”
the ALJ’s findings. Johnson v. Bowen, 864 F.2d 340, 343 (5th Cir. 1988). “A finding
of no substantial evidence is appropriate only if no credible evidentiary choices or
medical findings to support” the denial of benefits. Harris v. Apfel, 209 F.3d 413, 417
(5th Cir. 2000) (citing Johnso n, 864 F.2d at 343– 44). If supported by substantial
evidence, the ALJ’s determination is conclusive and must be affirmed. Richardson ,
402 U.S. at 390 (citing 42 U.S.C. § 405(g)).

Claimant relies primarily on Prudhomme v. Colvin, 605 F. App’x 250, 252 (5th
Cir. 2015).26 In that case, the Fifth Circuit found that substantial evidence did not
support the ALJ’s conclusion that an unskilled claimant , Teisha Prudhomme, could
perform semiskilled jobs. At the hearing, the ALJ asked the VE to identify
occupations a person similarly situated to the claimant could perform.  Prudhomme
v. Colvin, 605 F. App ’x 250, 25 2 (5th Cir. 2015) . The VE testified that Prudhomme
would be able to perform three “cashier positions that would be from the unskilled up
to the semi-skilled occupational level at the sedentary level.”  Id. at 254. Yet the VE
never explained how or why Prudhomme would be able to perform the suggested
semi-skilled jobs. Id. “Without such information in the record, there is not substantial
evidence upon which to base the denial of benefits.” Id. at 252 (citation omitted).
In this case, t he ALJ similarly concluded that Claimant was not disabled
because she was capable of making a successful adjustment to other work that exists
in significant numbers in the national economy .27 The Administration emphasizes
that the ALJ stated that the transferability of job skills was “ not material to the
determination of disability .”28 In Prudhomme, the Fifth Circuit determined that

The cited positions, however, are designated as semiskilled by the DOT, with specific

According to the Administration, the VE did not rely on the DOT classification
of the jobs and instead relied on Occupational Requirements Survey (ORS) data. The
“VE may cite to multiple acceptable sources of occupational data that do not precisely
correspond to each other ,” including the ORS.  See Titles II and XVI: Use of
Occupational Information and Vocational Specialist and Vocational Expert Evidence
in Disability Determination, SSR 24-3p (S.S.A. 2025). Yet, the policy is clear that the
“categorization of skills [] corresponds with the DOT. ” Id. In addition, “ VEs should
identify and explain if they cite an occupation that is performed in a different way
than identified in the source of data they used.” Id. “If the VE uses a data source that
defines exertion, education, or skill levels differently than our regulations, we expect
the VS or VE to explain the difference.” Id. To the extent that the VE contemplated
that the cited semiskilled occupations could be performed by an unskilled worker
without transferable skills, he did not provide any such explanation at the hearing.
