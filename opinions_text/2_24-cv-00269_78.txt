LAW AND ANALYSIS
I. The Defendants have failed to comply with Local Rule 5.6
 Local Rule 5.6(A) recognizes “the right of  the public to access material filed with
the court” and accordingly imposes certain re quirements on parties seeking to file a
document under seal. The rule provides:
All reasonable alternatives to filing under seal must be explored, including
a line-by-line analysis of the document s or other tangible item (including
documents marked confidential under a protective order) to redact only the
truly sensitive information rather than  simply seeking to file the entire
document or other item under seal. The redacted document or other item
may then be publicly filed, with the unredacted version filed under seal.

Local Rule 5.6(D)(1) enumerates the requirements a moving party seeking to designate a
document as confidential must meet. They ar e: “(a) [a] non-confidential description of
the portions of the document sought to be se aled (e.g., personal identifiers in medical
records); (b) [a] statement as to why sealing is necessary; (c) [r]eference to governing case

II. The Fifth Circuit counsels a strong  presumption in favor of public
access.

seal. “Judicial records are public records. And public records, by definition, presume
public access.” 14 This presumption “gives the federal judiciary ‘a measure of

In determining whether to seal a document, then, the Court “must balance the public’s
common law right of access against the interests favoring nondisclosure.” 16 There is a
strong presumption in favor of public access, 17  and the Fifth Circuit has counseled that
courts “should be ungenerous with their discretion to seal judicial records.” 18 “In order
for a document to be sealed, the movant mu st not only point to specific confidential
information contained in the document, but must also show the specific harm that would
be suffered if the public were granted access to [the] document.”19 The Fifth Circuit has
not identified the limits of what “specific harm” sufficient to outweigh the public’s interest
entails, but examples include the exposure  of “trade secrets or the identities of
confidential informants.”20 Confidentiality interests of the parties, lacking more, do not
suffice. “Courts within [the Fifth  Circuit] have repeatedly held  that a decision to seal is
not properly predicated on a movant’s mere allegations that documents contain
confidential material.”21

surmount the presumption of public access.

 The Fifth Circuit has provided guidance as to what reasons might outweigh the
