Before the Court is plaintiff Bernard Singleton’s motion to continue the
submission date for defendant’s motion for summary judgment.1  Defendant
Lowe’s Home Centers, LLC (“Lowe’s”) opposes the motion.2
Defendant’s motion for summary judgment was submitted on October
28, 2026. 3  Singleton requests a continuance under Federal Rule of Civil
Procedure 56(d) because he seeks to depose the claims adjuster who handled
his demand to preserve surveillance footage of a slip-and-fall incident inside
a Lowe’s store in New Orleans, Louisiana.4
Rule 56(d) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure provides:
If a nonmovant shows by affidavit or declaration
that, for specified reasons, it cannot present facts
essential to justify its opposition, the court may:

(1) defer considering the motion or deny it;

 The Fifth Circuit has held that Ru le 56(d) motions are “broadly favored
and should be liberally granted because the rule is designed to safeguard
non-moving parties from summary jud gment motions that they cannot
adequately oppose.”  Raby v. Livingston, 600 F.3d 552, 561 (5th Cir. 2010).
But the non-moving party still must “set forth a plausible basis for believing
that specified facts, susceptible of collection within a reasonable time frame,
probably exist” and demonstrate how those facts “will influence the outcome
of the pending summary judgment motion.”  Id.  Plaintiff must also show
that he “exercised due diligence in discovery.”  Baker v. Am. Airlines, Inc. ,
430 F.3d 750, 756 (5th Cir. 2005).
 Here, plaintiff argues that Rule 56 (d) relief is warranted because the
deposition of the claims adjuster w ho handled his request for surveillance
footage is necessary to oppose Lo we’s motion for summary judgment.5  The
claims adjuster, Kendra McCray, is employed by Sedgwick Claims
Management Services, Inc. (“Sedgwick”), a third party that handles claims
on behalf of Lowe’s and received plaintiff’s written demand to preserve
surveillance footage.6  Plaintiff contends that Lowe’s failure to preserve

Rule 37(e)(2)(A) provides that “only upon finding that [a] party acted
with intent to deprive another party” of electronically stored information, the
Court “may presume that the lost information was unfavorable to the party.”
Fed. R. Civ. P. 37(e)(2)(A).  Lowe’s  has stated that the footage was not

its representatives have testified that the preservation process “would have
been directed by Sedgwick.” 9  Thus, plaintiff argues that the deposition of
McCray is necessary to determine whet her Lowe’s acted with the intent to
deprive him of the footage.
 Lowe’s responds that plaintiff is no t entitled to relief under Rule 56(d)
because he has failed to diligently pursue  discovery.  It avers that plaintiff
was aware of Sedgwick’s involvement in the claims process for several

The Court finds that plaintiff is entitled to relief under Rule 56(d).
Plaintiff has demonstrated that McCr ay’s deposition is necessary to
determine how plaintiff’s preservation demand was processed.  Defendant’s
own representative has testified that the preservation process “would have
been directed by Sedgwick.” 12  And this evidence about the preservation
process is necessary to determine whether plaintiff is entitled to a
presumption that the surveillance foot age was unfavorable to Lowe’s under
Rule 37.  Thus, plaintiff has demonstr ated how the contents of McCray’s
deposition “will influence the outcome of the pending summary judgment
motion.”  Raby, 600 F.3d at 561.
Plaintiff has also diligently pursued discovery.  Plaintiff has deposed
several of Lowe’s employees and representatives to date.13  The Court notes
that plaintiff has been aware of McCray ’s identity since December 18, 2024,
