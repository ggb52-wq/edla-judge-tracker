II. LEGAL STANDARD
A. Federal Rule 12(b)(1)
Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(1) requires dismissal of an action
without prejudice if a court lacks jurisdi ction over the subject matter of the
plaintiff’s claim.  See Mitchell v. Bailey , 982 F.3d 937, 944 (5th Cir. 2020),
as revised (Dec. 30, 2020).  Motions subm itted under Rule 12(b)(1) allow a
party to challenge the court’s subjec t matter jurisdiction based upon the
allegations on the face of the complaint.  Barrera-Montenegro v. United
States, 74 F.3d 657, 659 (5th Cir. 1996).  In ruling on a 12(b)(1) motion to
dismiss, the court may re ly on “(1) the complaint alone; (2) the complaint
supplemented by undisputed facts ev idenced in the record; or (3) the
complaint supplemented by the undisputed facts plus the court’s resolution
of disputed facts.”  Moore v. Bryant , 853 F.3d 245, 248 (5th Cir. 2017)
(quoting Barrera-Montenegro, 74 F.3d at 659).  The plaintiff bears the
burden of demonstrating that subject matter jurisdiction exists.  See Spokeo
v. Robins, 578 U.S. 330, 338 (2016).
“[R]ipeness is a constitutional prerequisite to the exercise of
jurisdiction.”  Inst. for Free Speech v. Johnson , 148 F.4th 318, 331 (5th Cir.
2025).  A case is ripe only when it “would not benefit from any further factual
development and when the court would be in no better position to adjudicate

the issues in the future than it is now.”  Pearson v. Holder , 624 F.3d 682,
684 (5th Cir. 2010).
The Declaratory Judgment Act (“DJA” ) is intended “to settle actual
controversies before they ripen into vi olations of law or breach of some
contractual duty.”  United Transp. Union v. Foster , 205 F.3d 851, 857 n.8
(5th Cir. 2000).  Although the DJA “enlarged the range of remedies available
in the federal courts,” it did not “create a new right to seek those remedies.”
Harris County v. MERSCORP Inc., 791 F.3d 545, 552 (5th Cir. 2015).  Thus,
in order to obtain relief under the DJ A, the plaintiff still must demonstrate
that the dispute is ripe for adjudication.  Lower Colorado River Auth. v.
Papalote Creek II, L.L.C., 858 F.3d 916, 924 (5th Cir. 2003).

B. Federal Rule 12(c)
In deciding a Rule 12(c) motion, the Court must determine whether the
complaint, viewed in the light most favo rable to the plaintiff, states a valid
claim for relief.  Doe v. MySpace, Inc. , 528 F.3d 413, 418 (5th Cir.
2008).  Motions under Rule 12(c) may be filed only “after the pleadings are
closed.”  Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(c).  At  this stage, the Court “accepts all well-
pleaded facts as true.”  In re Katrina Canal  Breaches Litig. , 495 F.3d 191,
205 (5th Cir. 2007).  “Judgment on the pleadings is appropriate only if
material facts are not in dispute and questions of law are all that

A. EMSCO’s Rule 12(b)(1) Motion
EMSCO moves to dismiss Scottsda le’s claim for a declaratory
judgment, arguing that Scottsdale’s duty to indemnify claim is not ripe.18
An insurer’s duty to defend and in demnify its insureds are “distinct
and separate duties.”  Liberty Mut. Fire Ins. Co. v. Copart of Connecticut,
Inc., 75 F.4th 522, 535 (5th Cir. 2023).   For ripeness purposes, the duty to
indemnify is typically not justiciable be fore the underlying issue of liability
is resolved.  Hartford Cas. Ins. Co. v. DP Eng’g, L.L.C., 827 F.3d 423, 430
(5th Cir. 2016).  This is because “the duty to indemnify is triggered by the
actual facts that establish liability in the underlying lawsuit.”  Columbia Cas.
Co. v. Georgia & Florida Railnet, Inc., 542 F.3d 106, 110–11 (5th Cir. 2008).
But if “the same reasons that negate th e duty to defend likewise negate any
possibility the insurer will ever have  a duty to indemnify,” a court may
address the duty to indemnify before  the resolution of the underlying
litigation.  Northfield Ins. Co. v. Loving Home Care, Inc., 363 F.3d 523, 536–
37 (5th Cir. 2004).  Thus, “where the duty to defend is not at issue and where
it is apparent before liability is resolved in the underlying case that the Policy

The Court finds that Colorado River  is the applicable abstention
doctrine.  The Fifth Circuit has held that “a declaratory action that also seeks

held that this coercive relief in an insurance dispute triggers Colorado River,

Insulations, Inc. , 408 F.3d 248, 251 (5th Cir. 2005).  Indeed, “it is

Federal courts have a “virtually unfl agging obligation . . . to exercise
the jurisdiction given them.”  Colorado River, 424 U.S. at 817.  But under
Colorado River , “in ‘extraordinary and narro w’ circumstances, a district
court may abstain from exercising jurisdic tion over a case when there is a
concurrent state proceeding.”  Murphy v. Uncl e Ben’s, Inc., 168 F.3d 734,
737–38 (5th Cir. 1999) (quoting Colorado River , 424 U.S. at 813).  As a
threshold matter, federal courts may abstain from hearing a case under
Colorado River  only when the state and federal proceedings are “parallel,
having the same parties and the same issues.”  Stewart v. Western Heritage
Ins. Co., 438 F.3d 488, 491 (5th Cir. 2006).
Although Scottsdale was recent ly added as a defendant in the
underlying litigation, the two proceedings do not cover the same issues.
Indeed, Scottsdale’s claim for the expenses it has incurred in defending
EMSCO is not at issue in the underlying li tigation, nor is its duty to defend.

The Fifth Circuit has held that a distri ct court lacked discretion to abstain
under Colorado River  in analogous circumstances.  See Anco , 408 F.3d
at 251–52 (finding that because “the state action [did] not encompass” the
insurer’s claim for amounts paid to defend, the district court “lacked
discretion under Colorado River  to stay in favor of the state suit.”).
Moreover, several defendants and claims in the underlying litigation are not
at issue here.  The Fifth Circuit has held that this difference in issues and
parties also weighs against a finding that the federal an d state proceedings
are parallel.  Id. at 252.  Thus, because the federal proceedings and state
proceedings are not parallel, abstention is not proper here.
