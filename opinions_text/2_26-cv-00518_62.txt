or Amend Judgment and Rule 15(a)(2) Motion for Leave to File First Amended

LDCFS, UPS, and Connick for the alleged violation of his Fourth and Fourteenth
Amendment rights.4 The allegations date back to a 2004 default judgment entered
against Kindell. He alleged that his funds were seized unlawfully when his wages
were garnished to pay child support pursuant to this  allegedly fraudulent judgment

II. Legal Standard
 Rule 59(e) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure provides that a party may
move to alter or amend a judgment within 28 days after entry of the judgment. A
Rule 59(e) motion “calls into question the correctness of a judgment.”7 This rule serves
the “narrow purpose of allowing a party to correct manifest errors of law or fact or to
present newly discovered evidence.” 8 Thus, a Rule 59(e) motion is “not the proper
vehicle for rehashing evidence, legal theories, or arguments that could have been
offered or raised before the entry of judgment.”9 The Fifth Circuit has cautioned that
“[r]econsideration of a judgment after its entry is an extraordinary remedy that
should be used sparingly.” 10 In short, Rule 59(e) “should not be used to raise
arguments that were presented or could have been presented in the past.”11

III. Analysis
In its Order and Reasons granting defendants’ motions to dismiss, the Court
held that Kindell had failed to state a claim for relief because the Louisiana
Department of Children and Family Services is entitled to sovereign immunity;
Kindell had not sufficiently alleged a claim for Monell liability against Paul
Connick, Jr., and UPS was not subject to liability under 42 U.S.C. § 1983. None of
the perceived errors identified by Kindell constitutes a manifest error of law or fact
or presents newly discovered evidence.
First, Kindell fails to present any new evidence or manifest error in the
Court’s application of absolute prosecutorial immunity with respect to claims
against Connick to the extent they were alleged against him in his individual
capacity.
Second, with respect to Monell liability, Kindell reasserts his argument
rejected by the Court that the complaint established a “persistent practice and
official failure to train” regarding executing wage withholdings, returning
undeliverable mail notices, falsely logging non-appearances, and denying American
with Disability Act accommodations.
Third, with respect to Kindell’s alleged manifest errors of fact, the Court’s
decision did not rely on the items identified by Kindell and therefore they could not
impact the correctness of the judgment.

Once the Court entered final judgment, the appropriate course for Kindell to
seek leave to amend his complaint was by “either appealing the judgment[] or
seeking to alter or reopen the judgment under Rule 59 or 60.”12 Because the Court
denies Kindell’s Rule 59(e) motion, the judgment remains in place and Kindell’s
motion for leave to amend his complaint must also be denied.
Notably, Kindell did not request leave to file an amended complaint before
the Court entered judgment. All of the legal theories and claims set forth in his
proposed amended complaint could have been raised prior to the Court’s entry of
judgment. But even if the Court did have discretion to consider Kindell’s motion on
the merits, it would be denied because amendment would be futile.
With respect to the claims against Connick, Kindell again makes only
conclusory, unsupported claims regarding an official policy, custom, or practice that
is the “moving force” behind a constitutional violation as required for Monell
liability.13 Kindell also seeks to add a claim under Title II of the Americans with
Disabilities Act and Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act against Connick in his
official capacity, but he fails to allege the required elements.14
The allegations that he seeks to add against UPS likewise do not cure the
deficiencies in his original complaint. He again fails to allege in any non-conclusory
