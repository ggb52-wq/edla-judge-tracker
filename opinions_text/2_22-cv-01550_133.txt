ORDER AND REASONS
 In this litigation, Plaintiff Kaliegh Smith , Jr. (“Plaintiff”) brings a Monell claim1 against
Jason Williams, in his official capacity as the District Attorney for Orleans Parish (“Defendant”),
on behalf of his deceased father, 2 Kaliegh Smith, Sr. (“Mr. Smith”) . Plaintiff alleges th at the
Orleans Parish District Attorney’s Office (“OPDA”) secured Mr. Smith’s wrongful conviction in
violation of his constitutional rights by withholding material exculpatory evidence in violation of
its obligations under Brady v. Maryland.3
Before the Court is Defendan t’s Motion for Summary Judgment. 4 Defendant argues that
Plaintiff’s claims fail because Plaintiff cannot present competent evidence to show  that: (1) the
alleged undisclosed evidence was material or favorable; (2) the OPDA’s policymaker, then District
Attorney Leon Cannizzaro (“Cannizzaro”), adopted or implemented an unconstitutional policy
concerning Brady disclosures; (3) Cannizzaro acted with deliberate indifference to the rights of

B. Monell Claim

that a policy maker can be charged with actual or constructive knowledge, and (3) a constitutional
violation whose moving force is that policy (or custom).”158 “Official policy is ordinarily contained

evidenced by a “widespread” practice of officials or employees that, while not officially adopted
or promulgated, “is so common and well -settled as to constitute a custom that fairly represents
municipal policy.” 160 “[E]ven a facially innocuous policy will support liability if it was
promulgated with deliberate indifference to the known or obvious consequences that constitutional
violations would result.” 161 To prove deliberate indifference, a “showing of simple or even
heightened negligence will not suffice.” 162 Instead, “Courts of Appeals have routinely equated
deliberate indifference with recklessness.”163

evidence at issue was neither favorable nor material. In Brady v. Maryland , the Supreme Court
held that “suppression by the prosecution of evidence favorable to an accused [] violates due
process where the evidence is material either to guilt or to punishment, irrespective of the good
faith or bad faith of the prosecution.” 166 The prosecutor’s duty to provide favorable evidence
includes impeachment evidence and exculpatory evidence. 167 The prosecutor’s duty to disclose

actual or constructive knowledge of an official policy, practice, or custom of similar constitutional
violations. Plaintiff argues that he has put forth sufficient evidence to support Monell liability
under three distinct theories:
(1) OPDA had a widespread custom and practice of suppressing exculpatory
evidence; (2) OPDA maintained unconstitutional policies by using an improperly
narrow definition of constitutional disclosure obligations  as expressed by
Cannizzaro and as written in trial manuals adopted by OPDA; and (3) the direct
actions of Cannizzaro, the policymaker, and of Freeman, the Chief of Trials, to
whom policymaking authority had been delegated.

 Establishing an official policy, practice, or custom can be done in one of three ways. First,
there may be a policy that is “officially adopted and promulgated” by the municipality or an official
within the municipality with policymaking authority. 219 Second, there may be a “persistent,
widespread practice of city officials or employees, which, although not authorized by officially
adopted and promulgated policy, is so common and well settled as to constitute a custom that fairly
represents municipal policy.”220 A persistent, widespread practice may include allegations that a
policymaker failed to act affirmatively, “if the need to take some action to control the agents of
the local governmental entity ‘is so obvious, and the inadequacy [of existing practice] so likely to
result in the violation of constitutional rights, that the policymake[r] … can reasonably be said to
have been deliberately indifferent to the need.’” 221 A policymaker with policymaking authority

C. Whether Plaintiff Has Presented Evidence that the Policy or Custom is the “Moving
Force” Behind the Alleged Constitutional Violation

To establish “moving force” causation, a “plaintiff must show that the municipal action
was taken with the requisite degree of culpability and must demonstrate a direct causal link
between the municipal action and the deprivation of federal rights.”262 Critically, “[t]he causal link
‘moving force’ requirement and the degree of culpability ‘deliberate indifference’ requirement
must not be diluted, for ‘where a court fails to adhere to rigorous requirements of culpability and
causation, municipal liability collapses into respondeat superior liability.’” 263 Still, “‘[w]here a
plaintiff claims that a particular municipal action itself violates federal law, or directs an employee
to do so,’ the causation determination ‘is straightforward.’”264
Viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to Plaintiff, the Court concludes that
Plaintiff has presented sufficient evidence to create a dispute of material fact on this element.
Plaintiff has presented evidence that OPDA allegedly maintained a long standing practice of
suppressing similar categories of exculpatory evidence that Plaintiff contends was withheld during
Mr. Smith’s prosecution. The similarity between the alleged constitutional violations permits a
reasonable inference that Mr. Smith’s injury resulted from the customs Plaintiff contends existed
within the OPDA. Therefore, Plaintiff has presented evidence upon which a reasonable juror could
find that OPDA’s custom of suppressing exculpatory evidence in violation of Brady was the
moving force behind the alleged constitutional violations Mr. Smith suffered.

V. Conclusion
For the reasons stated herein, there are facts in dispute precluding summary judgment on
the Monell claim brought against Williams in his official capacity as District Attorney . Plaintiff
has presented sufficient evidence  to create genuine disputes of material fact as to whether  the
undisclosed evidence was favorable and material, whether  OPDA maintained a persistent and
widespread practice of suppressing material evidence in violation of Brady, whether OPDA acted
with deliberate indifference, and whether the alleged practice was the moving force behind the
alleged Brady violations. Because these issues present factual disputes that must be resolved by
the trier of fact, Defendant is not entitled to summary judgment.
 Accordingly,
