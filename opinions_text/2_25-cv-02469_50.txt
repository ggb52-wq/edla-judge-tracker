II. APPLICABLE LAW
A. Scope of Discovery
Courts have broad discretion in discovery matters.4  Rule 26(b)(1) of the Federal Rules of
Civil Procedure permits parties to obtain in discovery “any nonprivileged matter that is relevant to
any party’s claim or defense and proportional to the needs of the case.”  Information need not be
admissible into evidence to be discoverable.5  Rather, information merely needs to be proportional
and relevant to any claim or defense. 6  “Mutual knowledge of all the relevant facts gathered by
both parties is essential to proper litigation.  To that end, either party may compel the other to
disgorge whatever facts he has in his possession.”7
B. Interrogatories and Requests for Production

service, absent court order or stipulation.8  “Each interrogatory must, to the extent it is not objected
to, be answered separately and fully in writing under oath.” 9  And all objections must be stated

Rule 36 requests for admissions must move the court to amend or withdraw the default admissions
in accordance with the standard outlined in Rule 36(b), which provides:  “A matter admitted under
this rule is conclusively established unless the court, on motion, permits the admission to be
withdrawn or amended. Subject to Rule 16(e), the court may permit withdrawal or amendment if
it would promote the presentation of the merits of the action and if the court is not persuaded that
it would prejudice the requesting party in maintaining or defending the action on the merits.”28
The Court may allow a party to withdraw a deemed admission if same (1) would serve the
presentation of the case on its merits, and (2) not prejudice opposing party who obtained the
admissions.  Under the first prong, the court considers “whether refusing to permit withdrawal or
amendment would have the practical effect of eliminating any presentation of the merits  of the
case, whether the admission is contrary to the record of the case, whether the admission is no
longer true because of changed circumstances, and whether a party has made an honest error.29
Prejudice under the second prong does not arise simply because the party who obtained the
admission will now have to convince the fact finder of its truth or incur costs of discovery to
replace the deemed admissions.30  Rather, the necessary prejudice relates to the difficulty a party
may face in proving its case, such as the unavailability of key witnesses as a result of the sudden
need to obtain evidence with respect to the questions previously answered by the
admission.31  Courts also consider the timing of the motion, the time remaining before expiration

D. Protective Orders
Under Rule 26, a “court may, for good cause, issue an order to protect a party or person
from annoyance, embarrassment, oppression, or undue burden or expense.”34  “Good cause” exists
when disclosure will result in a clearly defined and serious injury to the party seeking the protective
order.35  “ In determining good cause, the court must balance the risk of injury without the
protective order and the requesting party’s need for information.”36
Protective orders applicable during the discovery stage, when parties are exchanging
information, honor a party’s legitimate privacy interests while facilitating the efficient exchange
of information.37  The court enjoys wide discretion in setting the parameters of a protective order.38
Rule 26 offers a variety of potential options that the court may use to protect the moving party,
including forbidding or limiting the scope of discovery into certain matters.39  A district court may
exercise its sound discretion in determining how far to restrict discovery, and in particular, the
decision whether to grant or deny a request for a protective order is entrusted to the district court’s

E. Rule 37 Sanctions
Rule 37 requires the imposition of reasonable expenses, including attorneys ’ fees,
when: (1) a motion to compel is granted or (2) the requested disclosure or discovery is produced
after the motion was filed.46  There are only three exceptions to the imposition of expenses. First,
“the movant filed the motion before attempting in good faith to obtain the disclosure or discovery
without court action.”47  Second, “the opposing party’s nondisclosure, response, or objection was
substantially justified.”48  Or, third, “other circumstances make an award of expenses unjust.”49

III.   ANALYSIS
Defendant Freedom Mortgage failed to deliver timely written responses to discovery.
Although that failure would ordinarily result in a waiver of objections other than privilege, neither
party addresses the relevant factors of good cause and prejudice.  Nor did Plaintiff file a reply
addressing whether Defendant’s delinquent discovery responses, which have not been provided by
either party, sufficiently respond to the requests.  Considering the ongoing discussions between
the parties despite the lapsed deadline, Defendant’s assertion that the delay was attributable to the
need for a protective order, the absence of any allegations of dilatory or bad faith conduct, the
absence of any prejudice given that discovery does not close until March 2027, the assertion that
much of the discovery is directed to dismissed claims, and the excessively harsh result of a finding
of waiver, the Court declines to find that Defendant’s delay has resulted in a wholesale waiver of
any objections.  Of course, if Plaintiff believes that any particular discovery response is insufficient
or any particular objection is improper, he may file a motion to compel that identifies the particular
matters at issue.
