II.  LEGAL STANDARD
Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 8(a)(2) requires “a short and plain statement
of the claim showing that the pleader is entitled to relief.” F ED . R.  CIV . P.  8(a)(2).
A complaint that does not meet Rule 8(a)(2)’s pleading standard should be dismissed
for failing to state a claim upon which relief can be granted. FED . R.  CIV . P.  12(b)(6).
“[T]he pleading standard . . . does not require ‘detailed factual allegations,’ but it
demands more than an unadorned, the-defendant-unlawfully-harmed-me
accusation.” Ashcroft v. Iqbal , 556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009) (quoting Bell Atl. Corp. v.

Twombly, 550 U.S. 544, 555 (2007)). “A pleading that offers ‘labels and conclusions’
or ‘a formulaic recitation of the elements of a cause of action will not do.’” Id. (quoting
Twombly, 550 U.S. at 555). “Nor does a complaint suffice if it tenders ‘naked
assertion[s]’ devoid of ‘further factual enhancement.’” Id. (quoting Twombly, 550 U.S.
at 557).
To survive a motion to dismiss, “a complaint must contain sufficient factual
matter, accepted as true, to ‘state a claim to relief that is plausible on its face.’” I d.
(quoting Twombly, 550 U.S. at 570). “A claim has facial plausibility when the plaintiff
pleads factual content that allows the court to draw the reasonable inference that the
defendant is liable for the misconduct alleged.” Id. (citing Twombly, 550 U.S. at 556).
A motion under Rule 12(b)(6) “ tests the sufficiency of the pleadings, not the
merits of the case. ” George v. SI Grp. , Inc., 36 F.4th 611, 619 (5th Cir. 2022).  The
Court’s task is “to determine whether the plaintiff has stated a legally cognizable
claim that is plausible, not to evaluate the plaintiff’s likelihood of success.” George v.
Hobby Lobby Stores, Inc., 769 F. Supp. 3d 537, 544 (E.D. La. 2025) (quoting Body by
Cook, Inc. v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins., 869 F.3d 381, 385 (5th Cir. 2017)). Therefore,
the Court accepts “all well -pleaded facts as true, viewing them in the light most
favorable to the plaintiff.” In re Katrina Canal Breaches Litig., 495 F.3d 191, 205 (5th
Cir. 2007) (quoting Martin K. Eby Constr. Co. v. Dall. Area Rapid Transit, 369 F.3d
464, 467 (5th Cir. 2004)). “ [C]onclusory allegations, unwarranted factual inferences,
or legal conclusions are not accepted as true.” Hodge v. Engleman, 90 F.4th 840, 843
(5th Cir. 2024) (quoting Allen v. Hays, 65 F.4th 736, 743 (5th Cir. 2023)).

III . ANALYSIS
A. “Shotgun Pleading”

Schneider National first moves to dismiss Glencore’s complaint as a n
impermissible “shotgun pleading” that fails to give fair notice of the basis  for its
claims.22 It further claims that the complaint does not state a cause of action or
explain which facts support which claims.23 Upon review of the complaint, the Court
does not agree.
 A “shotgun pleading” is one that fails “to give the defendants adequate notice
of the claims against them and the grounds upon which [each] claim rests.” Sistrunk
v. Haddox, No. 18-CV-516, 2020 WL 2549699, at *11 (W.D. La. May 19, 2020) (quoting
Weiland v. Palm Beach Cty. Sheriff ’s Off ., 792 F.3d 1313, 1323 (11th Cir.  2015)).
Shotgun pleadings violate Rule 8(a)(2) and thus should be dismissed for failing to
state a claim upon which relief can be granted. FED. R. CIV. P. 12(b)(6);  see McZeal
v. Higher Educ. Loan Auth. of State of Mo., No. 4:25-CV-02343, 2025 WL 3167760, at
*2 (S.D. Tex. Nov. 13, 2025) (citing Weiland, 792 F.3d at 1320).
There are “four rough types or categories” of shotgun pleadings:
1. “[A] complaint containing multiple counts where each count
adopts the allegations of all preceding counts, causing each
successive count to carry all that came before and the last count
to be a combination of the entire complaint.”

2. “[A] complaint . . . replete with conclusory, vague, and immaterial
facts not obviously connected to any particular cause of action.”

3. A complaint “that commits the sin of not separating into a
different count each cause of action or claim for relief.”

defendants without specifying which of the defendants are

May 20, 2022) (quoting Weiland, 792 F.3d at 1321–23).
However, a complaint is not a shotgun pleading simply because it contains
group allegations or lumps together defendants. Body by Cook, Inc. v. State Farm
Mut. Auto. Ins., No. 15-CV-2177, 2016 WL 4479507, at *3 (E.D. La. Aug. 25, 2016),
aff’d in part, rev’d in part, and remanded on other grounds, 869 F.3d 381 (5th Cir.
2017). The purpose of Rule 8 is “to [e]liminate prolixity in pleading and to achieve
brevity, simplicity, and clarity.” Gordon v. Green , 602 F.2d 743, 746 (5th Cir. 1979)
(quoting Knox v. First Sec. Bank of Utah , 196 F.2d 112, 117 (10th Cir. 1952)). What
matters is whether the complaint lays out the conduct underlying each allegation and
gives each defendant adequate notice of the plaintiff’s claims and the grounds upon
which they rest. Body by Cook, Inc. , 2016 WL 4479507, at *3. Dismissal is only
appropriate when “ it is virtually impossible  to know which allegations of fact are
intended to support which claim(s) for relief.” Valadez , 2022 WL 1608016, at *7
(quoting Weiland, 792 F.3d at 1325).
Schneider National presents several arguments against Glencore’s complaint,
none of which are persuasive. First, Schneider National alleges that the complaint is
a shotgun pleading because it does not separate each cause of action or claim for relief

B. The Carmack Amendment
Next, Schneider National moves  to dismiss Glencore’s complaint because ,
under the Carmack Amendment, a broker  cannot be held liable for loss of cargo.28
Schneider National claims that it was acting as a broker. However, Glencore alleges
facts that, taken as true, present a plausible claim  that Schneider National was
acting as a motor carrier, not a broker.
The Carmack Amendment governs the liability of interstate carriers for loss,
damage, or delay of cargo during transport. 49 U.S.C. § 1470 6. Congress passed the
Carmack Amendment “to provide the exclusive cause of action for loss or damages to
goods arising from the interstate transportation of those goods by a common carrier.”
Hoskins v. Bekins Van Lines, 343 F.3d 769, 778 (5th Cir. 2003).
The statute distinguishes between  motor carriers and brokers. A “motor

carrier” is defined as “a person providing motor vehicle transportation for
compensation.” 49 U.S.C. § 13102(14). In contrast, a “broker” is “a person, other than
a motor carrier, or an employee or agent of a motor carrier, that . . . sells, offers for
sale, negotiates for, or holds itself out . . . as providing, or arranging for,
transportation by motor carrier for compensation.” Id.  § 13102(2).  Put simply,
“[b]rokers are the transportation industry’s matchmakers, connecting sellers of goods
to the carriers who move them.” Montgomery v. Caribe Transp. II, LLC, 608 U.S. 219,
222–23 (2026).
Many courts, including this one, have held that the Carmack Amendment does
not apply to claims brought against brokers. See, e.g., Centennial Ins. Co. v. W.C. Fore
Trucking, Inc. , No. 05 -CV-166, 2005 WL 8173985, at *3 (E.D. La. Dec. 21, 2005)
(“[O]nly carriers can be liable to the shipper of goods for damages during transit.”).
But because they provide similar services,  “the distinction between a broker and a
carrier ‘is often blurry.’” Id. at *4 (quoting CGU Int’l Ins., PLC v. Keystone Lines Corp.,
No. C -02-3751, 2004 WL 1047982, at *2 (N.D. Cal. May 5, 2004)). For example,
brokers may be classified as motor carriers “when they arrange or offer to arrange
the transportation of shipments which they are authorized to transport and which
they have accepted and legally bound themselves to transport.” 49 C.F.R. § 371. 2
(2025). Thus, courts are reticent to decide whether a party acted as a broker or motor
carrier on a pre-trial motion. See, e.g. , Centennial Ins. Co., 2005 WL 8173985, at *4
(“[A] genuine issue of fact persists as to whether [defendant] was a broker or a carrier.
As such, the issue is not ripe for summary judgment . . . .”); Consol. Freightways Corp.

10

of Del. v. Travelers Ins. Co. , No. 00 -CV-20726, 2003 WL 22159468, at *6 (N.D. Cal.
Mar. 28, 2003) (“The question of whether [defendant] is a ‘broker’ or a ‘motor carrier’
cannot be resolved as a matter of law.”); Just Take Action, Inc. v. GST (Americas) Inc.,
No. 04 -3024, 2005 WL 1080597, at *5 (D. Minn. May 6, 2005) (finding summary
judgment inappropriate due to  “a factual dispute over whether [defendant] played
the role of broker or motor carrier”).
 Admittedly, the complaint  suggests that Schneider National may have acted
as a broker: “Schneider National . . . brokered the transportation of the Cargo.”29 But
this is not conclusive. The complaint also presents evidence that Schneider National
acted as a motor carrier. First, Glencore claims it gave Schneider National the
shipping instructions and security code, raising at least a plausible inference that
Schneider National was “authorized” to transport the cargo. See  49 C.F.R. § 371.2
(2025). Glencore further alleges that Schneider National then “sub -contracted out”
this obligation .30 If so , Schneider National was not just acting as a broker or
matchmaker but was “legally bound [] to transport” the cargo as a motor carrier. See
id. Taking these facts as true and viewing them in the light most favorable to the
Plaintiff, the Court cannot say that Schneider National was acting as a broker as a
matter of law.
For these reasons, Defendant’s motion to dismiss on grounds of the  Carmack
Amendment is DENIED .

Schneider National further moves to dismiss Glencore’s complaint because the
Federal Aviation Administration Authorization Act (“FAAAA”) preempts Glencore’s
state law claims. 31 The Court agrees with Schneider National that  Glencore’s state
law tort claims are preempted. However, the FAAAA does not preempt Glencore’s
state law claim arising from breach of contract.
 The FAAAA preempts state laws related to the prices, routes, and services of
motor carriers or brokers “with respect to the transportation of property.”  49 U.S.C.
§ 14501 (c)(1). Still, the statute carved out an important exception: States retain
“safety regulatory authority . . . with respect to motor vehicles.”  Id. § 14501(c)(2)(A).
The statute explicitly applies this “safety exception” to motor carriers. Id.  But for
many years, the Circuits were divided as to whether the exception permits state law
tort claims against brokers. Compare Ye v. GlobalTranz Enters., Inc., 74 F.4th 453,
464 (7th Cir. 2023) (holding that a negligent hiring claim against a broker did not fall
within the safety exception ), and Aspen Am. Ins. Co. v. Landstar Ranger, 65 F.4th
1261, 1272 (11th Cir. 2023) (same), with Cox v. Total Quality Logistics, Inc., 142 F.4th
847, 857−58 (6th Cir. 2025)  (holding that a negligent hiring claim against a broker
did fall within the safety exception), and Miller v. C.H. Robinson Worldwide, Inc., 976
F.3d 1016, 1020 (9th Cir. 2020) (same).
In May 2026, the Supreme Court made headway towards resolving this Circuit
split, holding  that the safety exception permits negligent -hiring claims against
brokers. Montgomery, 608 U.S. at 226 (“All agree that common -law duties and

standards of care form part of a State’s authority to regulate safety.”). In Montgomery,
the plaintiff suffered severe injuries and a leg amputation after being hit by a truck
driver who veered off the road. Id.  at 224. The plaintiff sued the broker that
coordinated the transport , claiming it  was negligent in hiring a trucking company
with a deficient safety rating. Id.  at 224–25. The plaintiff alleged that, based on the
safety rating,  the broker knew or should have known that choosing a  subpar
transporter was reasonably likely to result in an accident.  Id. at 225. The Supreme
Court agreed, holding that the safety exception applies to a broker’s negligent hiring
of an unsafe motor carrier that results injury. Id. at 228.
As a result of this decision, both motor carriers and brokers are subject to the
safety exception, and the exception can apply to Schneider National in either
capacity. See id. at 228– 29. However, not all state  law tort cases fall within the
exception; only “those involving regulations concerning motor vehicle safety.” Id.  at
227. The Supreme Court concluded that a negligent-hiring claim arising from a two-
vehicle collision clearly concerns motor vehicle safety. Id.  But “[o] ne can imagine
many state laws that are related to motor carrier prices, routes, and services . . . that
have no relationship to safety.” Id.
The question, therefore, is whether a negligence  claim arising from a loss of
cargo has “a relationship to safety.” Other courts, including in this Circuit, have held
that it does not . See, e.g. , Grupo Gusi S. de P.R. de R.L. de C.V. v. Select Transp.
Partners, LLC, No. H-26-120, 2026 WL 1804233, at *2 (S.D. Tex. June 23, 2026) (“An
allegation that a broker negligently hired a fraudulent carrier is different from an

v. Star Logistics, Inc., No. 2:26-CV-01254, 2026 WL 2262180, at *5 (C.D. Cal. Aug. 5,
2026) (holding that theft -based negligent -hiring claims remain preempted by the

 T his Court agree s. Unlike in Montgomery , Glencore does not allege that
Schneider National negligently hired a  driver or carrier that was likely to cause an
accident or otherwise pose a threat to public safety. See Montgomery, 608 U.S. at 225.
And Glencore does not claim that the carrier’s operation of a motor vehicle was
unsafe. Instead, Glencore  alleges that Schneider National fail ed to exercise due
diligence in allowing someone to fraudulently obtain the shipping instructions and
abscond with the cargo .32 Based on the se facts, Glencore has not shown that its
negligence claims against Schneider National relate to  motor vehicle safety.
Therefore, these claims do not fall within the safety exception and are preempted by
the FAAAA.
However, the FAAA does not preempt claims for breach of contract. See Wise
Recycling, LLC v. M2 Logistics, 943 F. Supp. 2d 700, 705 (N.D. Tex. 2013) (“49 U.S.C.
§ 14501 preempts all state law claims except for ordinary breach of contract claims.”);

D. Breach of Contract
The Court now turns to Glencore’s  only remaining claim: breach of contract.
Schneider National moves to dismiss Glencore’s breach of contract claim for failing
to put the Defendant on notice of the basic elements of its claim.33 The Court does not
agree.
Schneider National first notes that Glencore fail s to attach the contract or
recite the terms of the contract in the complaint.34 However, Schneider National cites
no authority supporting why failing to attach the contract would entitle Defendant to
dismissal. Neither the Federal Rules n or this Court’s Local Rules mandate that a
complaint for breach of contract must include the contract at issue. See Int’l Pecans,
LLC v. Ultra Trading Int’l Corp. , 764 F. Supp. 3d 527,  534, 537 (W.D. Tex. 2025)
(holding that a plaintiff plausibly alleged a breach of contract claim despite failing to
attach the contract to the complaint). And although the complaint does not quote the
