III. LEGAL STANDARD
Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(b)(6) provides that an action may be dismissed “for failure to state a
claim upon which relief can be granted.” “To survive a motion to dismiss, a complaint must contain
sufficient factual matter, accepted as true, to ‘state a claim for relief that is plausible on its face.’”
Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009) (quoting  Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly , 550 U.S. 544,
570 (2008)). “Factual allegations must be enough to raise a right to relief above the speculative
level.” Twombly, 550 U.S. at 556. A claim is plausible on its face when the plaintiff has pled facts
that allow the court to “draw a reasonable inference that the defendant is liable for the misconduct
alleged.” Id. at 570. Although a court must liberally construe the complaint in light most favorable
to the plaintiff, accept the plaintiff’s allegations as true, and draw all reasonable inferences in favor
of the plaintiff, Baker v. Putnal , 75 F.3d 190, 196 (5th Cir. 1996), courts “do not accept as true
conclusory allegations, unwarranted factual inferences, or legal conclusions.” Arias-Benn v. State
Farm Fire & Cas. Co. , 495 F.3d 228, 230 (5th Cir. 2007) (quoting Plotkin v. IP Axess Inc. , 407
F.3d 690, 696 (5th Cir. 2005)).

If a complaint fails to state a plausible claim, a district court should generally grant leave
to amend under Fed. R. Civ. P. 15 before dismissing the action. Carroll v. Fort James Corp., 470
F.3d 1171, 1175 (5th Cir. 2006) (“[Rule 15] evinces a bias in favor of granting leave to amend.
The policy of the Federal Rules is to permit liberal amendment . . .”). A court may deny leave,
however, when amendment would be futile. Villarreal v. Wells Fargo Bank , N.A., 814 F.3d 763,
766 (5th Cir. 2016) (explaining that amendment is futile when “the amended complaint would fail
to state a claim upon which relief could be granted”).
IV. DISCUSSION
The sole question before the Court is whether Rogers has stated a plausible claim for IIED
under Louisiana law. The elements of this claim are “(1) that the conduct of the defendant was
extreme and outrageous; (2) that the emotional distress suffered by the plaintiff was severe; and
(3) that the defendant desired to inflict severe emotional dist ress or knew that severe emotional
distress would be certain or substantially certain to result from his conduct.” White, 585 So. 2d at
1209.
A. Element One: Extreme and Outrageous Conduct
To satisfy the first element of an IIED claim, the conduct alleged “must be so outrageous
in character, and so extreme in degree, as to go beyond all possible bounds of decency. . . .” Id. at
1209. “Liability does not extend to mere insults, indignities, threats, annoyances, petty
oppressions, and trivialities.” Id. Nor does a claim for intentional infliction of emotional distress
lie in an ordinary employment dispute.  Wilson, 939 F.2d at 1144.  Most workplace conduct,
including “demeaning comments, unfair criticism, demotion, termination or other harassment, is
not sufficiently ‘extreme and outrageous,’ howev er deplorable it may be  . . .  even where the
evidence suggests that those acts are based upon an illegitimate factor such as race, age or disability

and are therefore  illegal.” Bird v. Simpson Inv. , 121 F.3d 705, at *7 (5th Cir. July 15,
1997) (unpublished). IIED claims are instead “limited to situations where the distress is more than
a reasonable person could be expected to endure and the offending conduct is intended or
calculated to cause severe emotional distress.” Washington v. Mother Works, Inc., 197 F. Supp. 2d
569, 572 (E.D. La. 2002).
Two decisions from the Fifth Circuit help illustrate the allegations necessary to state an
IIED claim. In the aforementioned Wilson, the president of a paper company engaged in a year -
long campaign to humiliate, demoralize, and demote his vice president, with the ultimate goal of
replacing him with a younger candidate. 939 F.2d at 1139–41. At trial, the jury returned a verdict
for the vice president on an IIED claim, which the Fifth Circuit reviewed for sufficiency of the
evidence. Id. at 1139. The Circuit began by identifying conduct that was insufficient to support an
IIED finding: (1) the company assigned the vice president’s duties to a younger employee; (2) the
president refused to speak to the vice president; (3) the company executed a plan to move younger
employees into management positions; (4) other managers refused to work with the vice president;
(5) the company reassigned the vice president from his corporate office to a position as a middle
manager at company warehouse; and (6) the vice president’s colleagues referred to him as old and
made signs mocking his age. Id. at 1144–45. In the Circuit’s view, this conduct, although illegal
and discriminatory, fell within the “realm of an ordinary employment dispute,” and did not reach
the level of outrageousness necessary to find IIED. Id. at 1145.
One event, however, stood out. Toward the end of his employ with the company, the vice
president was demoted to an entry level warehouse supervisor  where his primary duty became
janitorial work. Id. The vice president was given no employees to supervise  and spent his days
cleaning up after other employees in the warehouse cafeteria . Id. The Circuit found that the

B. Element Two: Severe Emotional Distress
The second element requires the distress suffered to be such “ that no reasonable person
could be expected to endure it. ” Id. “[T]he mental suffering or anguish [must be] extreme.” Id.
“[S]ymptoms of severe emotional distress include neuroses, psychoses, chronic depression,
phobia, and shock ,” while allegations of “ humiliation, anxiety, confusion, upset, worry and the
like are typically insufficient .” Gressett v. Sw. Airlines Co ., 216 F. Supp. 3d 743, 749 (E.D. La.
2016) (internal quotation marks omitted). “The intensity and the duration of the distress are factors
to be considered in determining [the] severity” of the alleged injuries.  Restatement (Second) of
Torts § 46 (1965).3
Rogers alleges that she suffered  “emotional distress, psychiatric injury, depression , . . .
anxiety, stress, . . . humiliation, [and] embarrassment.” R. Doc. 5 at 21. Rogers provides no details
about the intensity or duration of these alleged injuries or other indicators that her suffering
exceeded “what a reasonable person could be expected to endure .” McKinney v. Sharp, No. 25 -

C. Element Three: Intent to Inflict Severe Emotional Distress
The final element requires that the defendant either  desired to inflict severe emotional
distress or knew that severe emotional distress was certain or substantially certain to result from
their actions. White, 585 So. 2d at 1208. The defendant’s conduct “must be intended or calculated
to cause severe emotional distress and not just some lesser degree of fright, humiliation,
embarrassment, worry, or the like.” Id. at 1210. “Subjective intent can rarely be proven directly.”
Taylor v. State, 617 So. 2d 1198, 1205 (La. Ct. App. 1993), writ denied, 620 So. 2d 875 (La. 1993).
So, where appropriate, the defendant’s intent may be presumed from their engagement in extreme
and outrageous conduct. Id.
The Complaint alleges that “Ochsner . . . knew or w [as] substantially certain that severe
emotional distress would result . . . from [its] conduct.” R. Doc. 1 at 21. If Ochsner did engage in
extreme or outrageous conduct, a reasonable factfinder could infer that it intended to cause severe
emotional distress, or that such distress was substantially certain. Taylor, 617 So. 2d at 1205. To
the extent that Rogers amends her Complaint to show severe or outrageous conduct, the Court will
find this final element satisfied.

Rogers’s Complaint fails to adequately plead that Ochsner engaged in extreme and
outrageous conduct or that she suffered severe emotional distress as a result. There is no present
