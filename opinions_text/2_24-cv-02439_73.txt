BACKGROUND
Plaintiff Donald Turner brings claims for personal injuries he allegedly
sustained while working as a production operator for Crosby Energy Services,
Inc. (“Crosby”). Turner was assigned to an oil and gas platform in Grand Isle
Block 43, located off of the coast of Louisiana, which was owned and operated
by Defendant Cox Operating, LLC and Cox Oil Offshore, LLC (collectively
“Cox”). Turner alleges that on November 16, 2023, he sustained injuries while
being transported by way of crane from Cox’s platform to the deck of the M/V
GOL LIGHTNING (“GOL LIGHTNING”), a vessel owned and operated by
Defendant REC Marine Logistics, LLC (“REC”). During the personnel basket
transfer, Turner alleges that the GOL LIGHTNING rose on a swell at the same

LEGAL STANDARD
Summary judgment is appropriate “if the pleadings, depositions,
answers to interrogatories, and admissions on file, together with affidavits, if
any, show that there is no genuine issue as to any material fact and that the
moving party is entitled to a judgment as a matter of law.”6
In determining whether the movant is entitled to summary judgment,
the Court views facts in the light most favorable to the non-movant and draws

As a threshold issue, the Court must address jurisdiction. Jurisdiction
in this case arises pursuant to § 1349(b)(1) of the Outer Continental Shelf Act,
or OCSLA.13 Once OCSLA jurisdiction is established,  a court must ordinarily
“turn to the OCSLA choice of law provision to ascertain whether state, federal,
or maritime law applies to a particular case.”14 The Court, however, finds that
for purposes of resolving the instant Motion, it need not decide the issue
because “the elements to be established under either legal regime are so
similar.”15 Both Louisiana law (the law of the adjacent state) and general
maritime law require Turner to establish that the alleged incident occurred
and that it was a result of REC’s breach of a duty owed to Turner.16

The courts in this jurisdiction have consistently held that  a vessel does not
breach any duty to a plaintiff merely because it moves vertically in response to
wave action. 35 Accordingly, REC did not breach a duty by reacting to sea
conditions.
Further, despite arguing otherwise, Turner has not presented any
evidence that the GOL LIGHTNING was improperly positioned when the
basket landed, that it was drifting, that the sea conditions were unsafe for
transfer, or that it was understaffed. Moreover, Turner does not explain how
any of these alleged failures by the vessel caused his injury.
Specifically, Turner argues that there is no affirmative evidence that the
vessel was not drifting. He points out that Captain Gordon testified that it was
his (and therefore REC’s) duty during a basket transfer to “pretty much get the
boat in a comfortable position” before telling the crane operator that
“everything looks good” and to “lower the basket.” 36 Turner argues therefore
that “whether the GOL LIGHTNING was in a comfortable position when the
signal was given for Turner’s basket transfer is a disputed fact.” 37 The Court
disagrees. Turner identifies no evidence that the GOL LIGHTNING was
improperly positioned when the basket landed. Further, Turner expressly
disclaimed that the vessel was drifting when he was injured, attributing the
hard landing instead to the vessel’s vertical movement with the swell.
Next, although Turner points to evidence that seas were between six and
eight feet and that winds were between twenty and twenty-five miles per hour,
