A. Summary Judgment Standard
Summary judgment is proper if the pleadings, depositions, answers to interrogatories, and
admissions on file, together  with the affidav its, if any, show that there is no genuine issue as to
any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to a judgment as a matter of law.  Celotex
Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317, 322 (1986); Fed. R. Civ. P. 56.  “Rule 56(c) mandates the entry of
summary judgment, after adequate time for discovery and upon motion, against a party who fails
to make a showing sufficient to establish the existence of an element essential to that party’s case,
and on which that party will bear the burden of proof at trial.”  Celotex, 477 U.S. at 322.  A party
moving for summary judgment bears the initial bur den of demonstrating the basis for summary
judgment and identifying those portions of the record, discovery, and any affidavits supporting the
conclusion that there is no genuine issue of material fact.  Id. at 323.  If the moving party meets
that burden, then the nonmoving party must use evidence cognizable under Rule 56 to demonstrate
the existence of a genuine issue of material fact.  Id. at 324.
A genuine issue of material fact exists if a reasonable jury could return a verdict for the
nonmoving party.  See Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc ., 477 U.S. 242, 248 (1986).  The
substantive law identifies which facts are material.  Id.  Material facts are not genuinely disputed
when a rational trier of fact c ould not find for the nonmoving part y upon a review of the record
taken as a whole.  See Matsushita Elec. Indus. C o. v. Zenith Radio Corp ., 475 U.S. 574, 587
(1986); EEOC v. Simbaki, Ltd ., 767 F.3d 475, 481 (5th Cir. 2014).  Unsubstantiated assertions,

conclusory allegations, and merely colorable factua l bases are insufficient to defeat a motion for
summary judgment.  See Anderson, 477 U.S. at 249-50; Little v. Liquid Air Corp., 37 F.3d 1069,
1075 (5th Cir. 1994); Hopper v. Frank, 16 F.3d 92, 97 (5th Cir. 1994).  In ruling on a summary-
judgment motion, a court may not resolve credibility issues or weigh evidence.  See Delta & Pine
Land Co. v. Nationwide Agribusiness Ins. Co ., 530 F.3d 395, 398-99 (5th Cir. 2008).
Furthermore, a court must assess the evidence , review the facts, and draw any appropriate
inferences based on the evidence in the light most favorable to the party opposing summary
judgment.  See Tolan v. Cotton, 572 U.S. 650, 656-57 (2014); Daniels v. City of Arlington , 246
F.3d 500, 502 (5th Cir. 2001).  Yet, a court only draws reasonable inferenc es in favor of the
nonmovant “when there is an actual controversy, that is, when both parties have submitted
evidence of contradictory facts.”  Little, 37 F.3d at 1075 (citing Lujan v. Nat’l Wildlife Fed’n, 497
U.S. 871, 888 (1990)).
After the movant demonstrates the absence of a genuine issue of  material fact, the
nonmovant must articulat e specific facts showing a genuine  issue and point  to supporting,
competent evidence that may be presented in a form admissible at trial.  See Lynch Props., Inc. v.
Potomac Ins. Co., 140 F.3d 622, 625 (5th Cir. 1998); Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(c)(1)(A), (c)(2).  Such
facts must create more than “some metaphysical doubt as to the material facts.”  Matsushita, 475
U.S. at 586.  When the nonmovant will bear the bur den of proof at trial on the dispositive issue,
the moving party may simply point to insufficient admissible evidence to establish an essential
element of the nonmovant’s claim in order to satisfy its summary-judgment burden.  See Celotex,
477 U.S. at 322-25; Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(c)(1)(B).  Unless there is a genuine issue for trial that could
support a judgment in favor of  the nonmovant, summary judgm ent must be granted.  See Little,
37 F.3d at 1075-76.

B. Exhaustion of Administrative Remedies
Pursuant to the Prison Litigation Reform Act (“PLRA”), prisoner-plaintiffs must exhaust
their administrative remedies be fore bringing an action under § 1983, or any federal law, for
injuries related to prison conditions or events.  42 U.S.C. § 1997e(a) (“No action shall be brought
with respect to prison conditions under section 1983 of th is title, or any other Federal law, by a
prisoner confined in any jail, prison, or other correctional facility un til such administrative
remedies as are available are exhausted.”); Porter v. Nussle, 534 U.S. 516, 520 (2002).  “[T]he
PLRA’s exhaustion requirement applies to all inmate suits about prison life, whether they involve
general circumstances or particul ar episodes, and whether they al lege excessive force or some
other wrong.”  Porter, 534 U.S. at 532.  Because exhaustion is an affirmative defense, DPSC
bears the burden to demonstrat e that Isaac failed to exhaust his administrative remedies.  See
Cowart v. Erwin, 837 F.3d 444, 451 (5th Cir. 2016).
Courts look to the prison’s grievance pro cedures “[t]o determine what remedies are
‘available’ and thus must be exhausted.” Huskey v. Jones , 45 F.4th 827, 831 (5th Cir. 2022)
(quotation omitted); Wilson v. Epps, 776 F.3d 296, 299 (5th Cir. 2015); Cowart, 837 F.3d at 451.
Louisiana’s ARP grievance process, which applie s to all facilities operated by the Louisiana
Department of Public Safety and Corrections, La. Admin. Code tit. 22, pt. I, § 325(D)(1), is a two-
step process, and “a prisoner has not exhausted his remedies until he has completed both.”
Bargher v. White, 928 F.3d 439, 447 (5th Cir. 2019).
Relevant to the instant case is whether Isaac submitted the November 2, 2025 grievance at
all.  An inmate commences the “process by completing a request for administrative remedy … or
writing a letter to the warden.”  La. Admin. Code tit. 22, pt. I, § 325(G)(1 )(a)(i).  If the inmate
opts to write a letter, it must “contain[] the phrase: ‘This is a request for administrative remedy’

or ‘ARP’” and “briefly set[] out the basis for his claim, and the relief sought.”  La. Admin. Code
tit. 22, pt. I, § 325(G)(1)(a)(i), (G)(1)(a)(i)((b)).  The Administrative Code does not specify to
whom the inmate is to give his request, although it does provide that the request is to be made to
the warden.
An ARP screening officer then screens the request before it is assigned to the first step and
notifies the prisoner that his request has been accepted or rejected.  Id. § 325(I)(1).  The prisoner
“has not properly exhauste d administrative remedies if his re quest is rejected for any of the
reasons” enumerated in the Administrative Code.  Id. § 325(I)(1)(c)(iv).  The prisoner must
correct the noted deficiencies and resubmit his request for re medy to the scre ening officer,
otherwise it “will not be processed,” see id. § 325(I)(1)(a)(ii), (c)(iii), but the request may still
appear within the prison’s database.32  If a prisoner submits a request  while a previous request is
pending, the subsequent request “w ill be logged and set aside fo r handling” afte r the pending
request is exhausted.  Id. § 325(F)(3)(a)(ix).  If this happe ns, “[t]he warden may determine
whether a letter of instruction to the offender is in order,” but such a letter is not required.  Id.
The Administrative Code also does not specify the inmate’s options should his request be ignored
entirely or if it is not handed to the screening officer or logged into the system.
 The Supreme Court has held that a prisoner only needs to exhaust “available” remedies.
Ross v. Blake , 578 U.S. 632, 642 (2016).  A remedy is “av ailable” if its “procedures … are
‘capable of use’ to obtain ‘some re lief for the action complained of.’”  Id. (quoting Booth v.
Churner, 532 U.S. 731, 738 (2001)).  But a prisoner ha s no obligation to exhaust “unavailable”
remedies.  Id.  A remedy is “unavailable” when “(1) prison officials are una ble or consistently
unwilling to provide any relief to aggrieved inmates, (2) the administrative scheme is so opaque
