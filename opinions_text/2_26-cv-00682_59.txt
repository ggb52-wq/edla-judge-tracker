III. LEGAL STANDARD
“Federal courts are courts of limited jurisdiction.” Kokkonen v. Guardian Life Ins. Co. of
Am., 511 U.S. 375, 377 (1994). Thus,  this Court “may not exercise that jurisdiction absent a
statutory basis.” Exxon Mobil Corp. v. Allapattah Servs., Inc. , 545 U.S. 546, 552 (2005). The
removing party bears the burden of proving that a district court has jurisdiction over a matter. See
Jernigan v. Ashland Oil Inc. , 989 F.2d 812, 815 (5th Cir. 1993). Because removal jurisdiction
“raises significant federalism concerns,” Willy v. Coastal Corp. , 855 F.2d 1160, 1164 (5th Cir.
1988), it is strictly construed and doubts regarding removal jurisdiction should be resolved in favor
of remand, Acuna v. Brown & Root, Inc., 200 F.3d 335, 339 (5th Cir. 2000). Accordingly, the Court
“must presume that a suit lies outside [its] limited jurisdiction,” Howery v. Allstate Ins. Co., 243
F.3d 912, 916 (5th Cir. 2001), and “[a]ny ambiguities are construed against removal and in favor

of remand to state court.” Mumfrey v. CVS Pharm., Inc., 719 F.3d 392, 397 (5th Cir. 2013) (citing
Manguno v. Prudential Prop. & Cas. Ins. Co., 276 F.3d 720, 723 (5th Cir. 2002)).
 A defendant may remove a civil action filed in state court if a federal court would have had
original jurisdiction over the issue. 28 U.S.C. § 1441(a). A federal court has original jurisdiction
over cases involving complete diversity of citizenship among the parties where the amount  in
controversy exceeds $75,000. 28 U.S.C. § 1332(a). Complete diversity exists when no
plaintiff shares a state of citizenship with any defendant. McLaughlin v. Miss. Power Co. , 376
F.3d 344, 353 (5th Cir. 2004). When any party is a limited liability company, courts
determine its citizenship by looking to “the citizenship of all of its members,” Harvey v. Grey
Wolf Drilling Co., 542 F.3d 1077, 1080 (5th Cir. 2008), which can be natural persons and other
entities, including other limited liability companies.
For the purposes of diversity jurisdiction, citizenship of a natural person is determined by
an individual’s domicile. Preston v. Tenent Healthsystem Mem’l Med. Ctr., Inc., 485 F.3d 793, 797
(5th Cir.  2007). “A person’s state of domicile  presumptively continues unless rebutted  with
sufficient evidence of change.” Id. at 797–98. A change in domicile requires physical presence in
the new location coupled with an intent to remain there indefinitel y. Id. at 798. The burden rests
with the party seeking to show a change in domicile to come forward with sufficient evidence of
that change. Coury v. Prot, 85 F.3d 244, 250 (5th Cir. 1996). A number of factors drive the “intent
to remain” analysis, including “where the litigant exercises civil and political rights, pays taxes,
owns real and personal property, has driver’s and other licenses, maintains bank accounts, belongs
to clubs and churches, has places of business or employment, and maintains a home for his family.”
Id. at 251.

IV. DISCUSSION
            Two issues are before the Court. First, whether this court may exercise diversity jurisdiction
when a plaintiff’s post-filing change of citizenship creates complete diversity prior removal.
Second, Francis’s citizenship on the filing date, February 11, 2026.
1. Complete diversity must exist on the date of filing.
To remove a case to federal court on diversity grounds, complete diversity must exist at
moment of filing. That is the general rule. Grupo Dataflux v. Atlas Glob. Grp., L.P., 541 U.S. 567,
570 (2004) (“It has long been the case that the jurisdiction of the court depends upon the state of
things at the time of the action brought .” (internal quotation marks omitted)); see also Coury, 85
F.3d at 249; Ashford v. Aeroframe Servs., L.L.C., 907 F.3d 385, 386 (5th Cir. 2018). Defendants
invoke an exception to this rule known as the “voluntary -involuntary exception,” which permits
removal when a plaintiff’s voluntary action in state court creates complete diversity. See Hoyt v.
Lane Constr. Corp., 927 F.3d 287, 295 (5th Cir. 2019).2 Courts have recognized this exception in
certain, limited circumstances; most notably, when a plaintiff voluntarily dismisses a diversity -
destroying defendant.  See Powers v. Chesapeake & O. Ry. , 169 U.S. 92 (1898) ; Am. Car &
Foundry Co. v. Kettelhake, 236 U.S. 311, 316 (1915). No higher court has extended this exception
to situations where a plaintiff’s post-filing change of citizenship creates diversity. To the contrary,
the Supreme Court has expressly indicated that this exception does not apply in such
circumstances. In Grupo Dataflux v. Atlas Glob. Group Limited, the diversity-destroying members
of the plaintiff-limited partnership left the partnership after the case was filed in federal court. 541

U.S. at 568–69. The question presented was “whether a post -filing change in citizenship c[ould]
cure a lack of subject matter jurisdiction that existed at the time of filing.” Id. at 568. Answering
that question in the negative, the Court reaffirmed the longstanding principle that “‘[w]here there
is no change of party, a  jurisdiction depending on the  condition of the  party is governed by that
condition, as it was at the commencement of the suit.” Id. at 574. Because the “[t]he purported
cure” to the jurisdictional defect “arose not for a change in the parties to the action, but from a
change in the citizenship of a continuing party,” federal subject matter jurisdiction did not exist.
Id. at 575. The import of Grupo is that the voluntary -involuntary exception is not triggered by a
change in the citizenship of a party to the action. In such circumstances, the general rule holds that
diversity of citizenship must exist at the time of filing. While Grupo concerned a case originating
in federal court, the language of the opinion does not limit its application, and Defendants have
offered no reason why it would not apply in the removal context. Accordingly, the Court follows
Grupo’ s guidance and assesses citizenship solely at the time of filing.

After evaluating the record evidence, the Court’s view is that Francis remained a California
domiciliary on February 1, 2026, the date of filing in state court. Plaintiffs explained that Francis’s
February trip to Louisiana was simply a vacation to attend Mardi Gras. Objective evidence
supports this characterization: Francis traveled on a return ticket , intended on staying for only a
few weeks, continued to own and reside in her California home with her belongings, retained a
California driver’s license and vehicle registration, and had not purchased property in Louisiana
or arranged long -term housing. Regardless of her future plans, Francis’s February 11 visit to
Louisiana was intended to be short trip, not an indefinite relocation. Because Francis lacked
requisite intent to remain, she did not become a Louisiana domiciliary on that date.
