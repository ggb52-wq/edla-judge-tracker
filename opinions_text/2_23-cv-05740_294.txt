III. LAW & ANALYSIS
A. Summary Judgment Standard
Summary judgment is proper if the pleadings, depositions, answers to interrogatories, and
admissions on file, together  with the affidav its, if any, show that there is no genuine issue as to
any material fact and that the moving party is  entitled to a judgment as a matter of law.  Celotex
Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317, 322 (1986); Fed. R. Civ. P. 56.  “Rule 56(c) mandates the entry of
summary judgment, after adequate time for discovery and upon motion, against a party who fails
to make a showing sufficient to establish the existence of an element essential to that party’s case,
and on which that party will bear the burden of proof at trial.”  Celotex, 477 U.S. at 322.  A party
moving for summary judgment bears the initial bur den of demonstrating the basis for summary
judgment and identifying those portions of the record, discovery, and any affidavits supporting the
conclusion that there is no genui ne issue of material fact.  Id. at 323.  If the moving party meets
that burden, then the nonmoving party must use evidence cognizable under Rule 56 to demonstrate
the existence of a genuine issue of material fact.  Id. at 324.
A genuine issue of material fact exists if a reasonable jury could return a verdict for the
nonmoving party.  See Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 248 (1986).  The substantive

B. Analysis
The pending cross-motions ask the Court to de termine whether Plaintiffs can recover as
damages those portions of the rent al amounts attributable to the common areas that were paid by
government assistance.  More simp ly stated (and as framed by th e parties), the question is: who
gets the windfall, Plaintiffs or Defendants?  The answer must be Plaintiffs.
“The collateral source rule is a rule of evidence and damages that is of common law origin,
yet embraced and applied by Louisiana courts.”  Bozeman, 879 So. 2d at 697.  “Under the collateral
source rule, a tortfeasor may not benefit, and an  injured plaintiff’s tort recovery may not be
reduced, because of monies received by the plaintiff from sources independent of the tortfeasor’s
procuration or contribution.”  Kansas City S. Ry., 846 So. 2d at 739.  Thus, the rule provides that
“the payments received from the independent source are not  deducted from the award the
aggrieved party would otherwis e receive from the wrongdoer.”  Id.  The collateral source rule
exists for “several reasons,” but “[t]he reason mo st often stated is that the defendant should not
gain an advantage from outside benefits provided to the plaintiff independently of any act of the
defendant.”  Id.  It also has the goal of deterring wrongdoing.  Id.
Although the collateral source ru le “typically applies to tort  cases involving insurance
payments or other benefits,” it has “been applied to a variety of factual circumstances.”  Bozeman,
879 So. 2d at 698.  For example, in Kansas City Southern Railway, the Louisiana supreme court
held that the rule applies to cases arising under the Louisiana Environmental Quality Act where a
damaged party seeks reimbursement for remedia tion expenses, although the federal government

had already covered much of the cost.  846 So. 2d at 740.  In so holding, the court noted that it
“believe[d] it is mere happenstance that the collat eral source rule has been  applied chiefly in the
context of conventional La. Civ. Code art. 2315 tort.”  Id. at 741.  The court thus recognized that
Louisiana “courts have applied the doctrine to a range of situations where the collateral source is
provided to the plaintiff by a government agency or even a gratuitous source,” including medical
expenses paid by Medicare and other government benefits.  Id. at 740, 742-43 (quote at 740).  The
Louisiana supreme court in Kansas City Southern Railway emphasized that, when considering the
application of the collateral source rule, these Louisiana “courts focused their analysis on the fact
that a wrongdoer may not benefit, and an injured party’s recovery may not be diminished, because
of benefits received by the plaintiff from sources independent of the wrongdoer’s procuration or
contribution.”  Id. at 741.  In other words, “[a] wrongdoer’s liability should not be reduced by the
amount of collateral source payments to an injured plaintiff, even where the nature of the collateral
source is a public relief provided to the plaintiff by application of federal or state law.”  Id. at 743.
Thus, if there is to be a windfa ll, the collateral source rule counsels that it should inure to the
benefit of the injured party because, although there may be a double recovery, the wrongdoer does
not face “a double burden” and should not reap th e benefit of the victim’s other benefits.  Id. at
743-44; see also Bozeman, 879 So. 2d at 702.
Applying these principles to the case at bar, any potential windfall should go to Plaintiffs,
not Defendants, regardless of th e theory of recovery.  At l east one court in  Louisiana has
recognized that “there cannot be a blanket prohibition on the applica tion of the collateral source
rule to contract claims.”  Metoyer v. Auto Club Family Ins. Co. , 536 F. Supp. 2d 664, 670 (E.D.
La. 2008).  This is particularly true where the case has an “element of tort.”  Id.  Here, Plaintiffs’
breach-of-contract and breach-of-warranty claims both have an element of tort because, to prevail,

Plaintiffs must prove that the common areas were maintained in a substandard condition.  See id.;
see also Ganheart v. Exec. House Apartments, 671 So. 2d 525, 529 (La. App. 1996) (recognizing
that a tenant may recover tort-like damages for uninhabitable premises, including mental anguish,
anxiety, and worry).
Moreover, Defendants are mista ken that a diminution in th e Plaintiffs’ patrimony or
payment for the benefit is required for the collateral source rule to apply.  Metoyer, 536 F. Supp.
2d a 670.  Contrary to Defendants’  assertion, the Louisiana supr eme court did not make such a
blanket statement in Bozeman.  See generally 879 So. 2d at 697-706.  Instead, the court allowed
the plaintiff to recover from the defendant amounts Medicaid actually paid for medical bills, just
not the written-off amounts.  Id. at 705-06.  Medicaid, like Section 8 housing assistance, is a public
benefit, and receiving the benefit does not diminish a person’s patrimony.   Although diminution
of patrimony has been explained as preventing double recovery, the Louisiana supreme court has
recognized that the collateral source rule may per mit a windfall to a plaintiff where the alterative
would be for the defendant to receive the windfall.  Kansas City S. Ry. at 743-44; see also Bozeman,
879 So. 2d at 702.  That is precisely the case here.
Persuasive authority also demonstrates that th is is the correct result.  For example, in
Anderson v. District of Columbia Housing Authority, the tenant, a portion of whose rent was paid
by government assistance, claimed that she was entitled to full rental abatement because the
landlord’s breach of the implied warranty of habitability deprived her of the benefit of her bargain.
923 A.2d 853, 855-58 (D.C. 2007).  The court recognized that the portion of the rent paid by public
assistance belonged to the public housing authority, but “absent a claim asserted by HUD or [the
local housing authority], as between the landlord and the tenant, … th e tenant was entitled to 100%
of the … abatements.”  Id. at 859 (emphasis omitted).  This re sult comports with  the collateral
