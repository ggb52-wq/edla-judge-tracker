Ziegler claimed would be opening in the Veterans Affairs Hospital (the “VA Hospital”) in
New Orleans.   ( Id. at ¶ 5).  The parties allegedly executed a letter of intent.   ( Id. at ¶ 6).

  II. Law and Analysis
 Rule 37(b)(2)(A) allows a district court to impose a variety of sanctions for not
obeying discovery orders, including striking the pleadings in whole or in part and/or
entering a default judgment against the offending party.  Fed. R. Civ. P. 37(b)(2)(A)(iii), (vi).
The purpose of these sanctions is to ensure the effectiveness of the discovery process.
Tubby v. Allen , Civ. A. No. 6:16cv972, 2019 WL 2482733, at *2 (E.D. Tex. Apr. 25, 2019)
(citing Diaz v. S. Drilling Corp. , 427 F.2d 1118, 1126 ( 5th Cir. 1970)); see also 8B Charles
Alan Wright & Arthur R. Miller, Federal Practice and Procedure § 2281 (3d ed. 2019).  “A
district court has ‘broad discretion in all discovery matters,’ and ‘such discretion will not be
disturbed ordinarily unless there are unusual circumstances showing a clear abuse.’”  Kelly
v. Syria Shell Petroleum Dev. B.V. , 213 F.3d 841, 855 (5th Cir. 2000) (quoting Wyatt v.
Kaplan, 686 F.2d 276, 283 (5th Cir. 1982)).  This broad discretion extends to the imposition
of discovery sanctions.  See Moore v. CITGO Ref. & Chemicals Co., L.P. , 735 F.3d 309, 315 (5th
Cir. 2013).
 While Rule 37 allows for the entry of a default judgment as a sanction, “[l]itigation-
ending sanctions are, by their very nature, the last resort.”  Fuqua v. Horizon/CMS
Healthcare Corp., 199 F.R.D. 200, 204 (N.D.  Tex. 2000) (citing FDIC v. Conner, 20 F.3d  1376,
1380 (5th Cir.  1994)).  “Nevertheless, in certain circumstances, they are justified ‘not
merely to penalize those whose conduct may be deemed to warrant such a sanction, but to
deter those who might be tempted to such conduct in the absence of such a deterrent.’”  Id.
(citing National Hockey League v. Metropolitan Hockey Club, 427 U.S. 639, 643 (1976)).

 Under Fifth Circuit precedent, sanction rulings that result in the entry of default
judgment and/or the striking of affirmative defenses must meet t wo criteria: (1) the
sanctioned party's discovery violation must be willful and (2) a lesser sanction would not
substantially achieve the desired deterrent effect.  United States v. $ 49,000 Currency , 330
F.3d 371, 376 (5th Cir. 2003) ; see also Smith v. Sm ith, 145 F.3d 335, 344 (5th Cir. 1998)
(noting that w hen a district court awards default judgment as a discovery sanction, two
criteria must be met: the penalized party's discovery violation must be willful  and the
drastic measure is only to be employed wh en a lesser sanction would not substantially
achieve the desired deterrent effect).  The reviewing court may also consider whether the
discovery violation prejudiced the opposing party's preparation for trial, and whether the
client was blameless in the violation.  Batson v. Neal Spelce Assocs.,  765 F.2d 511, 514 (5th
Cir. 1985).
 Entry of default is warranted when a “party against whom a judgment for
affirmative relief is sought has failed to plead or otherwise defend  . . . .”  Fed. R. Civ. P. 55(a)
(emphasis added)  (emphasis added) .  “A district court has ‘inherent power’ necessarily
vested in courts to manage their own affairs so as to achieve the orderly and expeditious
disposition of cases.”  Luis C. Forteza e Hijos, Inc. v. Mills , 534 F.2d 415, 419 (1st Cir. 1976)
(quoting Link v. Wabash, 370 U.S. 626, 631 (1962)).  The Fifth Circuit is in accord.  See Callip
v. Harris Co. Child Welfare Dept. , 757 F.2d 1513, 1518- 19 (5th Cir. 1985) (upholding
dismissal for want of prosecution when there is a clear  record of delay or contumacious
conduct and lesser sanctions would not serve the ends of justice) ; McGrady v. D'Andrea
Elec., Inc. , 434 F.2d 1000, 1001 (5th Cir. 1970) (“[t]here is ample authority to uphold a
district court's power to order entry of defau lt for failure to comply with court orders or

 Defendants contend that the striking of their affirmative defenses and/or entering a
default judgment against them is unwarranted in this lawsuit because Zeigler has not been
willfully indifferent or acting in bad faith when participating in the discovery process.
Zeigler maintains that the events here occurred over six years ago, and she cannot be
faulted for not retaining all relevant discovery given that she has lost numerous Subway
restaurants, her husband – who managed the businesses – passed away, and she has been
forced to relocate.  She contends that she has not completed the forensic review because
she does not possess the financial means to pay for such a review even though she
attempted to engage a company to complete such a review.  Counsel for Zeigler also notes
that his email server  malfunctioned, resulting in the late notice of some of this Court’s
orders.  Zeigler argues that lesser sanctions are appro priate given that her participation
has only resulted in delays and misunderstandings due to lack of notice and her inability to
pay.
 Plaintiff’s pleading outlines Defendants’ numerous violations of this Court’s orders
and contends that the violations demonstrate bad faith and willful misconduct.  He
maintains that there is no evidence in the record to support Zeigler’s contention that she is
unable to pay for a forensic review of her email system.  Plaintiff contends that he has been
prejudiced by having to ask for a continuance of his trial.  He also argues that the evidence

 A review of case law in this Circuit reveals that the striking of a defendant’s answer
and/or the entry of default judgment are reserv ed for the most willful and bad faith
violations of the discovery process.  See, e.g., Plasticsource Workers Committee v. Coburn ,
283 F. App’x 181 (5th Cir. 2008) (affirming district court’s striking of answer and entering
default judgment when defendant refused to comply with written discovery demands and
district court orders regarding his deposition and leaving deposition early and failing to
return); Tubby v. Allen , Civ. A. No. 6:16cv972, 2019 WL 2482733 (E.D. Tex. Apr. 25, 2019)
(striking answer and entering default judgment when defendant (1) failed to appear for his
deposition and participate in scheduling of new depositions dates; (2) refused to engage in
other litigation processes such as filing responses to a motion for sanctions and default; (3)
failed to appear for a scheduled hearing; and (4) refused to communicate with his lawyer );
McLane Foodservice, Inc. v. Hawkins, No. Civ. A.3:03-CV-1412-G, 2004 WL 350667 (N.D. Tex.
Feb. 25, 2004) (striking answer and entering default judgment when defendant failed to
provide initial disclosures and written discovery responses, failed to appear at a
deposition, and failed to appear at a court hearing to explain why he had failed to attend
deposition).
 The Court cannot find that Defendants’ actions here rise to the level of willful and
bad faith behavior required by  the case law  to impose case -dispositive sanctions .  While
Defendants’ conduct  in responding to Plaintiff’s discovery requests  has been far from
acceptable, there is evidence in the record that Def endants at least attempted to comply
with this Court’s orders to respond to Plaintiff’s discovery requests.  Indeed, Defendants

have produced some documents to Plaintiff, even though Plaintiff maintains that the
production has been arguably “incomplete, untrue[,] and evasive.”  (Rec. doc. 65 at 2).  The
Court recognizes that the events that gave rise to this lawsuit occurred approximately 6 -10
years ago, and individuals such as Zeigler may not have been as diligent a keeper of records
as a more sophisticate d defendant, such as a corporation.  It is also possible  that Zeigler’s
husband – who is now deceased – may have run the Subway businesses while Zeigler
played merely a supporting role,  as she has alleged.  The Court must also take counsel for
Zeigler at his word that she attempted to engage a company to perform the forensic review,
but that her finances preclude the retention of said company.  In short, Defendants here
have not displayed a n outright  refusal to engage in the discovery process such as the
defendants in the case law cited above.
 Notwithstanding these findings, the Court finds that Defendants’ inability to
thoroughly and fully participate in the discovery process warrants a lesser sanction,  given
that their inability to fully engage in the dis covery process has impeded Plaintiff from fully
preparing his suit for prosecution and has even forced a continuance of the trial , through
no fault of Plaintiff .  Specifically, the Court will recommend that Defendants be precluded
from entering into evidence at the trial of this matter any document and/or testimony that
was not produced and/or disclosed  during discovery.  This Court will also recommend an
adverse inference against Defendants regarding the agreement between the parties.  It will
be re commended that any such agreement that Plaintiff proves  at trial – through
documentary evidence or sworn testimony – shall be deemed the proven agreement
between the parties.  The Court finds that these lesser sanctions will achieve the required
deterrent effect and address any potential prejudice to Plaintiff.

 Accordingly, and for the foregoing reasons,
 IT IS RECOMMENDED that Defendants be precluded from entering into evidence at
