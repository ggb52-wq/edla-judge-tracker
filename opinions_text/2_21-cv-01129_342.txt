II. LEGAL STANDARD
Summary judgment is appropriate if “there is no genuine dispute as to any material fa ct
and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(a). “Material facts”
are facts that might affect the outcome of the suit under the governing law. Anderson v. Liberty
Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 248 (1986).  A dispute about a material fact is “genuine” if the evidence
is such that a reasonable jury could return a verdict for the non-moving party. Id. “The non-movant
cannot satisfy the summary judgment burden with conclusory allegations, unsubstantiated
assertions, or only a scintilla of evidence.” King v. Goebel, No. CIV .A. 10-2708, 2012 WL 6482783
(E.D. La. Dec. 13, 2012)  (citing Little v. Liquid Air Corp. , 37 F.3d 1069, 1075 (5th Cir. 1994)).
“The party moving for summary judgment must demonstrate the absence of a genuine issue of
material fact, but need not negate the elements of the non-movant's case.” Celotex Corp. v. Catrett,
477 U.S. 317, 323 (1986). The Court must resolve any factual controversies in favor of the
nonmoving party, “but only when there is an actual controversy, that is, when both parties have
submitted evidence of contradictory facts.” Little, 37 F.3d at 1075 (5th Cir. 1994).

III. DISCUSSION
“Qualified immunity shields government officials from civil damages liability unless the
official violated a statutory or constitutional right that was clearly established at the time of the
challenged conduct.” Heaney v. Roberts , 846 F.3d 795, 801 (5th Cir. 2017) (quoting Reichle v.
Howards, 566 U.S. 658, 664 (2012)). To determine whether an official is entitled to qualified
immunity, the court must assess: “(1) whether the plaintiff has alleged a violation of a clearly
established constitutional right; and (2) if so, whether the defendant’s conduct was objectively
reasonable in light of clearly established law at the time of the incident.” Jacobs v. W. Feliciana
Sheriff’ s Dep’t, 228 F.3d 388, 393 (5th Cir. 2000). For this reason, courts will not deny immunity
unless “existing precedent . . . placed the statutory or constitutional question beyond debate.” Id.
at 741. Here, the Fifth Circuit previous reversal of this Court’s grant of summary judgment based
on qualified immunity to Officers Wheeler and Pierre indicates that Hankins has sufficiently
alleged a violation of a clearly established right. As such, the first element is no longer in dispute.
Rather, the Court’s decision will turn on whether Defendants’ conduct regarding the supervision,
training, and discipline of their subordinate, Officer Wheeler or Pierre, was objectively reasonable
given the law that existed in June of 2020 when the alleged incident occurred.
A. The SLFPA-E Officers
1. Failure to Supervise
The SLFPA-E Officers assert that they are entitled to qualified immunity as to Hankins’s
claim relative to their alleged failure to supervise Officer Wheeler. When a defendant invokes the
defense of qualified immunity on summary judgment, the burden is on the plaintiff to demonstrate
the inapplicability of the defense. See Kitchen v. Dallas Cty., 759 F.3d 468, 476 (5th Cir. 2014).
“Under section 1983, supervisory officials are not liable for the actions of subordinates on any

5

theory of vicarious liability.” Thompkins v. Belt , 828 F.2d 298, 303 (5th Cir. 1987). Rather, a
supervisory official may be held liable only if “he affirmatively participates in the acts that cause
the constitutional deprivation, or [] he implements unconstitutional policies that causally result in
the constitutional injury.” Gates v. Tex. Dep't of Protective & Regul. Servs., 537 F.3d 404, 435 (5th
Cir. 2008). For a supervisor to be found liable for a failure to supervise, the plaintiff must show
(1) that the supervisor failed to supervise the subordinate official; (2) a causal link exists between
the failure to supervise and the violation of the plaintiff's rights; and (3) the failure to supervise
amounts to deliberate indifference. Porter v. Epps, 659 F.3d 440, 446 (5th Cir. 2011).
 Here, as the Court previously held with reference to the HANO supervisors, the SLFPA-E
Officers cannot be held liable for a failure to supervise Officer Wheeler within the context of his
secondary employment with Hurstville because they had no duty to do so. See R. Doc. 218 at 8–
10. At the time of the alleged incident in June of 2020, Louisiana jurisprudence consistently held
that for purposes of liability, an off -duty officer working a private detail is the employee of the
company paying the detail, not of the city or the police depa rtment. See Wright v. Skate Country,
Inc., 98-0217 (La. App. 4. Cir. 5/12/99); 734 So. 2d 874 (“A review of the caselaw on this issue
reveals that only two cases . . . have been reported in the last quarter of the 20th century; both of
those held that the governing body cannot be held liable.”); Duryea v. Handy, 96– 1018 (La. App.
4 Cir. 10/3/97), 700 So. 2d 1123; Luccia v. Cummings, 94–416 (La. App. 5 Cir. 11/16/94), 646 So.
2d 1142, writ denied, 94–2983 (La. 2/3/95), 649 So. 2d 406; Kramer v. Continental Casualty Co.,
92–1131 (La. App. 3 Cir. 6/22/94), 641 So. 2d 557. Hankins points to La. Rev. Stat. § 38:330.7(B)
as the source of an alleged duty, which states that the SLFPA-E “may employ a superintendent of
police security to supervise the police security personnel of all levee districts [.]” However, the
permissive “may” used in that statute cannot be read to impose a duty to do so.

Moreover, as Defendants point out, La. Rev Stat. § 38:326(A)(1)(a) makes clear that the
authority of levee district police is to be “responsible for maintaining order and exercising general
police power on and off the levees, within the area of the levee district or levee and drainage
district, and upon its surrounding waters.” That is, levee district police officers are responsible for
providing law enforcement functions within the district and while employed by that district.
Accordingly, La. Rev. Stat. § 38:330.7 must be read to contemplate supervision by a superintendent
of the levee police officers only while on duty and patrolling in the district on levee police business;
it does not impose on the SLFPA -E Officers a duty to supervise their officers while engaged in
secondary employment. Accordingly, the SLFPA-E Officers are entitled to the entry of summary
judgment in their favor as to Hankins’s claim for failure to supervise.
 2. Failure to Train
In order to succeed on a § 1983 claim for a failure to train, a plaintiff must show (1) that
the supervisor failed to train the subordinate official; (2) a causal link exists between the failure to
train and the violation of the plaintiff's rights; and (3)  the failure to train amounts to deliberate
indifference. Porter, 659 F.3d at 446. Therefore, Hankins cannot overcome the SLFPA-E Officers’
qualified immunity without showing that their failure to train was objectively unreasonable in light
of the requirement not to be deliberately indifferent to his rights. Smith v. Carruth, No. CV 15-
4570, 2017 WL 785345, at *5 (E.D. La. Mar. 1, 2017); see also Hare v. City of Conrinth, Miss.,
135 F.3d 320, 327-28 (5th Cir. 1998) (explaining the “minimum standard not to be deliberately
indifferent” informs whether a defendant acted objectively unreasonable); Thompson v. Upshur
Cty., TX, 245 F.3d 447, 460 (5th Cir. 2001) (noting that when a defendant moves for summary
judgment based on qualified immunity on deliberate indifference claims, plaintiff must show that
“all reasonable officials similarly situated would have then known that the [failure to train]”

7

constituted deliberate indifference in violation of the Constitution). Additionally, “for liability to
attach based on an ‘inadequate training’ claim, a plaintiff must allege with specificity how a
particular training program is defective.” Zarnow v. City of Wichita Falls, 614 F.3d 161, 170 (5th
Cir. 2010) (quoting Roberts v. City of Shreveport, 397 F.3d 287, 293 (5th Cir. 2005)). Indeed, “the
focus must be on the adequacy of the training program in relation to the tasks the particular officers
must perform.” Roberts, 397 F.3d at 293. Moreover, to defeat qualified immunity, a plaintiff must
show the supervisors knew of “a pattern of similar violations arising from training . . . that is so
clearly inadequate as to be obviously likely to result in a constitutional violation.” Brumfield v.
Hollins, 551 F.3d 322, 329 (5th Cir. 2008).
Here, Hankins cannot meet his burden to show that any failure to train Officer Wheeler
constituted deliberate indifference. Deliberate indifference is “a stringent standard of fault”
requiring “more than negligence or even gross negligence.” Estate of Davis ex rel. McCully v. City
of N. Richland Hills, 406 F.3d 375, 379, 381 (5th Cir. 2005). Deliberate indifference requires the
plaintiff to show the official actually knew of and consciously disregarded a substantial risk of
serious harm. Damond v. City of Rayville, 127 F.4th 935, 940 (5th Cir. 2025). Hankins has not
identified a pattern of unconstitutional stops, complaints of racial profiling, or similar misconduct
by Officer Wheeler that could have put the SLFPA-E Officers on notice that Officer Wheeler and
any deficit in his training constituted a substantial risk of serious harm. Hankins relies heavily on
Officer Wheeler’s previous termination from the New Orleans Police Department (“NOPD”) to
have provided this notice. However, prior conduct provides notice only when it is “fairly similar
to what ultimately transpired.” Sanders-Burns v. City of Plano, 594 F.3d 366, 381 (5th Cir. 2010).
The incident occasioning Officer Wheeler’s termination from the NOPD was not an investigatory
stop, involved a taser rather than a gun, and did not contain any allegation of racial profiling. It

was thus not similar to the subject incident. Accordingly, nothing about the prior incident made it
predictable that Wheeler would conduct an allegedly unlawful stop during a private security detail.
Moreover, a single dissimilar event cannot be construed as a “pattern of similar violations.”
Brumfield, 551 F.3d at 329. While single-incident liability has been recognized, that exception “is
generally reserved for those cases in which the government actor was provided no training
whatsoever.” Hutcheson v. Dallas Cnty., 994 F.3d 477, 483 (5th Cir. 2021). Defendants have
provided evidence that Officer Wheeler completed all training that was required under Louisiana
law, including instruction on use of force, firearms, and racial profiling, among others . See R..
Doc. 321-1 at 17. Hankins can therefore not rely on a theory of single -incident liability to prove
his failure to train claim against the SLFPA-E Officers. The SLFPA-E Officers are entitled to the
entry of summary judgment as to this claim.
 3. Failure to Discipline
Hankins’s failure to discipline claim against the SLFPA-E Officers fails on the same basis
as does his failure to train claim. Claims based upon an alleged failure to discipline require, inter
alia, a showing of deliberate indifference . Porter, 659 F.3d at 446. Officer Wheeler did not have
any record of past similar incidents that could have put the SLFPA -E Officers on notice of any
substantial risk of serious harm. Moreover, Hankins cannot rely on post -incident conduct to
support this claim. See Deville v. Marcantel, 567 F.3d 156, 171 (5th Cir. 2009)  (explaining that
actions taken after an alleged constitutional violation cannot have caused that violation and cannot
be the moving force behind a plaintiff’s alleged injury). Accordingly, summary judgment in favor
of the SLFPA-E Officers is appropriate as to Hankins’s failure to discipline claim as well.

B. Perilloux
 1. Failure to Supervise
Defendants argue that Hankins cannot establish supervisory liability against Perilloux on
any theory because Perilloux was Hurstville’s “detail coordinator, handled only scheduling, GPS
review, and payroll” and “had no authority to train, investigate, or discipline” officers. R. Doc.
321-1 at 5. But as the employing entity, Hurstville unquestionably had a duty under Louisiana law
to supervise Officers Wheeler and Pierre at the time of the subject incident. Although operating as
Hurstville’s supervising officer, Perilloux disclaimed supervising officers ’ conduct while they
worked for Hurstville, knowing that no other individual or entity was supervising those officers .
See R. Doc. 329 at 15. As Hankins argues, a reasonable jury could find that Perilloux exercised
operational authority over the Hurstville detail and that his failure to provide any supervision
whatsoever constituted deliberate indifference, is causally related to the subject incident, and was
objectively unreasonable.1 Accordingly, the Court will deny summary judgment as to Hankins’s
claim against Perilloux for failure to supervise.
 2. Failure to Train and Failure to Discipline
Unlike Hankins’s failure to supervise claim, Hankins’s failure to train and failure to
discipline claims against Perilloux cannot survive summary judgment for the same reasons that
those claims against the SLFPA-E Officers must be dismissed. Hankins has not shown that there
is any genuine issue of material fact precluding the Court from concluding that no reasonable jury
could find that Perrilloux was deliberately indifferent to the need to train or discipline Officer
