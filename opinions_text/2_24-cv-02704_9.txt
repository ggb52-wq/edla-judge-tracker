II. Standards of Review
A. Statutory Frivolousness Review
Pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1915(e)(2) and § 1915A  and 42 U.S.C. § 1997e(c), the Court is
required to sua sponte  dismiss cases filed by prisoners proceeding in forma pauperis  upon a
determination that they are frivolous.  The Court has broad discretion in determining the frivolous
nature of the complaint.  See Cay v. Estelle, 789 F.2d 318, 325 (5th Cir. 1986), modified on other
grounds, Booker v. Koonce, 2 F.3d 114 (5th Cir. 1993).  However, the Court may not sua sponte
dismiss an action merely because of questionable le gal theories or unlikely factual allegations in
the complaint.
Under this statute, a claim is frivolous when it lacks an arguable basis either in law or fact.
Neitzke v. Williams , 490 U.S. 319, 324-25 (1989); Talib v. Gilley , 138 F.3d 211, 213 (5th Cir.

4

1998).  “A [claim] lacks an arguable basis in law if  it is based on an indis putably meritless legal
theory, such as if the complaint alleges the viol ation of a legal interest which clearly does not
exist.”  Harper v. Showers, 174 F.3d 716, 718 (5th Cir. 1999) (quoting Davis v. Scott, 157 F.3d
1003, 1005 (5th Cir. 1998)).  It lacks an arguable factual basis only if the facts alleged are “clearly
baseless,” a category encompassing fancif ul, fantastic, and delu sional allegations.  Denton v.
Hernandez, 504 U.S. 25, 32-33 (1992) (citing Neitzke, 490 U.S. at 327-28).  Thus, the Court must
determine whether plaintiff’s claims are based on an indisputably meritless legal theory or clearly
baseless factual allegations.  Reeves v. Collins , 27 F.3d 174, 176 (5th Cir. 1994); Jackson v.
Vannoy, 49 F.3d 175, 176-77 (5th Cir. 1995); Moore v. Mabus, 976 F.2d 268, 269 (5th Cir. 1992).
III. Analysis
A. Claims Against TPSOJ and Medical Staff
Wilson named the Tangipahoa Sheriff’s Office Jail (“TPSOJ”) and their medical staff as a
defendant in the caption of his co mplaint.  A plaintiff bringing a § 1983 complaint is required to
identify both a constitutional violation and the responsible person acting under color of state law.
Flagg Bros., Inc. v. Brooks, 436 U.S. 149, 156 (1978).  The TPSOJ, however, is not recognized as
a “person” within the meaning of § 1983.  See Douglas v. Gusman, 567 F. Supp. 2d 877, 892 (E.D.
La. Jun. 9, 2008) (Order adopting attached Report and Recommendation).
In Louisiana, a jail facility is not an entity “legally empowered to do” anything independent
of either the responsible parish officials or the parish sheriff.  Roberts v. Sewerage and Water Bd.
of New Orleans, 634 So. 2d 341, 347 (La. 1994).  Since a ja il is not a juridical entity under state
law, “a prison or jail or its administrative depa rtments are not entities that can be sued under
Section 1983 . . .” and jails “are not persons for purposes of suit under Section 1983 as the statute

and case law define  that term.”  Douglas, 567 F. Supp. 2d at 892; see Fed. R. Civ. P. 17(b).  As
one division of this Court has noted, a parish jail is “not an entity, but a building.”  See Jones v. St.
Tammany Parish Jail, 4 F. Supp.2d 606, 613 (E.D. La. May 8, 1998) (dismissing with prejudice
the St. Tammany Parish Jail as an improper defendant); see also Kerr v. Orleans Parish Sheriff's
Office Prison, No. 15-0746, 2015 WL 4755174, at *1 (E.D. La. Aug. 10, 2015).
Wilson also references the TPSOJ medical staff as a defendant.  This broad group of people
is not a properly named defe ndant. Instead, a § 1983 action must be filed against an actual
identified person who violated a constitutional right, not a department or general staff group at the
jail.  See, e.g., Allen v. Gusman, No. 05-1633, 2006 WL 286007, at *3 n.8 (E.D. La. Feb. 2, 2006)
(“Medical Staff” is not a juridical entity capable of being sued; the specific medical personnel must
be named); Carter v. Strain, No. 09-3401, 2009 WL 2390808, at *3 (E.D. La. July 31, 2009) (citing
August v. Gusman , No. 06-3962, 2008 WL 466202, at *7 (E.D. La. Feb. 13, 2008); Staritz v.
Valdez, No. 06-CV-1926, 2007 WL 1498285, at *2 (N.D. Tex. May 21, 2007); and Banks v. United
States, No. 05-6853, 2007 WL 1030326, at *11 (E.D. La. Mar. 28, 2007)).
Consequently, Wilson’s claims against the TPSOJ and medical staff should be dismissed
under 28 U.S.C. § 1915, § 1915A, and 42 U.S.C. § 1997e as frivolous and for failure to state a
claim for which relief can be granted.
B. Claims Against Nurse Brittany
Wilson claims that Nurse Brittany failed to provide him with adequate medical care when
she denied his requests to have his blood pressure and blood sugar checked when had a headache,
was sweating profusely, and fe lt as though something was wrong with his blood pressure. This
maltreatment, he claims, led to him having a stroke which resulted in Bell’s palsy and loss of vision

in his right eye. He also states that his requests to be provided with the hospital paperwork detailing
his facial exercises for his Bell’s palsy, and to be seen by an eye doctor to address his vision loss
as a result the stroke, have been unanswered.
Inadequate medical care for purposes of a § 1983 claim occurs only when the conduct
amounts to “deliberate indifference to [the prisoner’s] serious medical needs” that “constitutes the
‘unnecessary and wanton infliction of pain.’”  Estelle v. Gamble , 429 U.S. 97, 104 (1978).  As
applied to pretrial detainees, li ke plaintiff, “[t]he Fourteenth  Amendment requires that state
officials not disregard the ‘basic human needs’ of pretrial detainees, including medical care.”  Reed
v. Krajca (Estate of Henson) , 440 F. App’x 341, 343 (5th Cir. 2011).  These constitutional
challenges may be brought as an attack on a “condition of confinement” or as an “episodic act or
omission.”  Shepherd v. Dallas Cnty. , 591 F.3d 445, 452 (5th Cir. 2009); Stevenson-Cotton v.
Galveston Cnty., No. 22-40801, 2024 WL 138631, at *3 (5th Cir. 2024); see also Reed v. Wichita
Cnty. (Estate of Henson), 795 F.3d 456, 464 (5th Cir. 2015) (“[T]here is no rule barring a plaintiff
from pleading both alternativ e theories[.]”).  Wilson, in this case, has asserted an attack on an
episodic act or omission.
For an episodic-act-or-omission claim, “the co mplained-of harm is a particular act or
omission of one or more officials.”  Scott v. Moore, 114 F.3d 51, 53 (5th Cir. 1997).  A plaintiff in
an episodic-act-or-omission case “complains first of a particular act of, or omission by, the actor,”
and may also “point[] derivatively to a policy, custom, or rule (or lack thereof) of the municipality
that permitted or cause d the act or omission.” Scott, 114 F.3d at 53. To impose liability on a
defendant in his individual capacity in an epis odic-act-or-omission case, a pretrial detainee must
establish that the defendant acted with subjective deliberate indifference.  Id. at 54.

A person acts with subjective indifference if (1) “he knows that an inmate faces a
substantial risk of serious bodily  harm,” and (2) “he disregards that risk by failing to take
reasonable measures to abate it.”  Anderson v. Dallas Cnty., Tex., 286 F. App’x 850, 860 (5th Cir.
2008) (citing Gobert v. Caldwell , 463 F.3d 339, 346 (5th Cir. 2006)).  The mental state for
subjective deliberate indifference requires that “(1) ‘the official must both be aware of facts from
which the inference could be drawn that a substantial risk of serious harm exists,’ and (2) ‘he must
also draw the inference.’”  Williams v. Hampton , 797 F.3d 276, 281 (5th Cir. 2015) (quoting
Farmer v. Brennan , 511 U.S. 825, 837 (1970); Adames v. Perez , 331 F.3d 508, 512 (5th Cir.
2003)).  Thus, the official is liable when he ignore s a danger to an inmate after he has been made
fully aware of it.  Jackson v. Cain, 864 F.2d 1235, 1246-47 (5th Cir. 1989).  Subjective deliberate
indifference “is an extremely high standard to meet.”  Domino v. Tex. Dep’t of Crim. Justice, 239
F.3d 752, 756 (5th Cir. 2001).
Under this deliberate indifference standard, a prison official is not liable unless the official
knows of and disregards an excessive ri sk to inmate hea lth or safety.  Farmer, 511 U.S. at 847;
Bradley v. Puckett, 157 F.3d 1022, 1025 (5th Cir. 1998).  Deliberate indifference is manifested in
the provider’s intentional denial of medical care or intentional interference with the treatment once
prescribed.  Farmer, 511 U.S. at 847.  To state a cognizable cl aim, a prisoner must allege acts or
omissions sufficiently harmful to evidence de liberate indifference that can offend “evolving
standards of decency.”  Id. at 833. Thus, mere disagreement with medical treatment is insufficient
to state a claim of deliberate indifference.  See Varnado v. Lynaugh, 920 F.2d 320, 321 (5th Cir.
1991).

As stated, this intentional denial of medical care transcends beyond just mere disagreement
with medical treatment, as no medical treatm ent was rendered by Nurse Brittany upon Wilson’s
request. Wilson alleges that the refusal and failure of Nurse Brittany to recognize and provide him
with treatment for his sweating, headache, and blood pressure symptoms resulted in him suffering
a stroke which has caused long term side effects such as Bell’s palsy and loss of vision. Given the
facts asserted, Wilson has alle ged non-frivolous claims agains t Nurse Brittany for medical
indifference or inadequate medical care necessary to address serious medical conditions which has
led to further physical injury.
Accordingly, his claims against Nurse Brittany for medical indifference should be allowed
to move forward for further proceedings.
C. Claims Against Sgt. Bates
Wilson claims that Sgt. Bates was accompanying Nurse Brittany when she denied him
medical care by telling him she did not do blood pressure checks at night as he was experiencing
his symptoms. According to Wilson, since Sgt. Bates was the shift supervisor, he phoned her later
that night to request he be brought to the front to have his bl ood pressure checked, informing her
that he knew something was wrong. He states Sgt. Bates denied his request and told him he needed
to request a sick call.
The same standards for assessi ng a claim for denial of medi cal care against nurses and
doctors, as outlined above, also apply to claims for denial of medical care against prison personnel.
Deliberate indifference may be “manifested by prison docto rs in their response to the prisoner’s
needs or by prison guards in intentionally  denying or delaying access to medical care or
intentionally interfering with the treatment once prescribed.”  Estelle, 429 U.S. at 104-05.  “Such

a showing requires the inmate to allege that prison officials ‘refu sed to treat him, ignored his
complaints, intentionally treated him incorrectly, or engage d in any similar conduct that would
clearly evince a wanton disregard for any serious medical needs.’”  Brewster v. Dretke, 587 F.3d
764, 770 (5th Cir. 2009) (quoting Domino, 239 F.3d at 756). Ignor ing requests for medical
treatment has been found to satisfy the deliberate indifference standard. See Harris v. Hegmann ,
198 F.3d 153, 159-60 (5th Cir. 1999); see also Hudson v. McHugh, 148 F.3d 859, 863-64 (7th Cir.
1998) (stating that “an inmate with a potentially  serious problem repeat edly requesting medical
aid, receiving none, and then suffering a serious inju ry” is “the prototypical case of deliberate
indifference”).
Under these standards, Wilson has at least stated non-frivolous claims of denial of medical
care against Sgt. Bates. Wilson asserts that Sgt.  Bates was made aware of  his initial request to
Nurse Brittany by accompanying her on “pill check” and then ignored his second request when he
phoned her later that evening requesting a blood pr essure check because he knew something was
wrong, ultimately resulting in hi m having a stroke. The alleged di sregard for a serious medical
need is sufficient to at least state a threshold claim at this time.
For these reasons, the § 1983 medical indifference claims asserted against Sgt. Bates should
be allowed to proceed past frivolousness review.
