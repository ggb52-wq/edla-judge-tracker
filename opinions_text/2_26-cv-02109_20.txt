III. LAW & ANALYSIS
A. Standard for Insufficient Service of Process
“To acquire jurisdiction over the person, a court must serve on the person a document, such
as a summons, notice, writ, or order.”  McGuire v. Sigma Coatings , Inc., 48 F.3d 902, 907 (5th
Cir. 1995)  (quotation omitted) .  Rule 12(b)(5) authorizes a court to dismiss an action for
insufficient service of process.  Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(b)(5).  Service is insufficient if it is not made in
compliance with the requirements of Rule 4.   “[O]nce the validity of service has been contested,
the plaintiff bears the burden of establishing its validity.”  Carimi v. Royal Caribbean Cruise Line,
Inc., 959 F.2d 1344, 1346 (5th Cir. 1992).   “The district court enjoys a broad discretion in
determining whether to dismiss an action for ineffective service of process.”  George v. U.S. Dep’t
of Labor, 788 F.2d 1115, 1116 (5th Cir. 1986).
B. Proof of Service
Rule 4(l) provides that “[e]xcept for service by a United States marshal or deputy marshal,”
proof of service must be made by an affidavit of the server.  Under 28 U.S.C. §  1746, “[a]n
unsworn declaration may be treated as an affidavit if it contains a statement that the contents of
the declaration are ‘true and correct’ under penalty of perjury.”  Way v. City of Missouri City, 133
F.4th 509, 516 (5th Cir. 2025) (citing 28 U.S.C. § 1746).  Once filed, “[t]he general rule is that ‘a
signed return of service constitutes prima facie evidence of valid service,’” People’s United Equip.
Fin. Corp. v. Hartmann , 447 F. App’x 522, 524 (5th Cir. 2011) ( citation modified ) (quoting
O’Brien v. R.J. O’Brien & Assocs., Inc., 998 F.2d 1394, 1398 (7th Cir. 1993)), which a defendant
can overcome by “showing that the return is inaccurate. ”  4B WRIGHT & MILLER’S FEDERAL

C. Method of Service
Rule 4(e) provides that an individual may be properly “served in a judicial district of the
United States by,” inter alia, “following state law for serving a summons … in the state where the
district court is located” or “delivering a copy of the summons and of the complaint to the
individual personally.”  The rule thus gives parties the option to follow the relevant state procedure
for service or to effect service by the various means prescribed by the federal rules.  When serving
an individual personally, Rule 4 does not require that the server know the contents of the envelope
containing a summons and  complaint or that the envelope be marked  before handing it to the
defendant, and Zaghia does not point to a ny federal law or court precedent to that effect.  Zaghia
does urge that Louisiana law requires  the server to know “he is serving judicial process ,”34 but
because Lycée Français opted to use a federal procedure to serve process  (viz., personal delivery
to Zaghia), the state requirements do not apply.  Nor does Rule 4 require the server to ask the
defendant for identification or have “independent knowledge” of the defendant being served.   In
his motion and reply, Zaghia concedes via his declaration that he is “the Defendant in this action,”35

This case is distinguishable from the cases cited by Zaghia, such as Way v. Mueller Brass
Co, 840 F.2d 303 (5th Cir. 1988) .  In that case, the Fifth Circuit found that service was deficient
even though the defendant had actual notice of the pending lawsuit.  Id. at 306.  There the question
was whether the plaintiff properly served a state government organization.  Id. at 305-06.  States
or other governmental bodies  must be served “‘by delivering a copy of the summons and of the
complaint to the chief executive officer ,’” or by serving each document according to state law,
which in Way allowed for process to be served on the  state attorney general.  Fed. R. Civ. P.
4(j)(2)(A)-(B); see Way 840 F.2d at 305 (quoting the predecessor version of the rule).  The plaintiff
in Way did not serve either the chief executive officer of the state organization or the state attorney
general, but merely “mailed a copy of the summons and complaint to” a local branch manager of
the defendant organization.  Way, 840 F.2d at 305.  Thus, although the defendant organization had
“actual notice” of the lawsuit, process had not been delivered to the correct individual for purposes
of service.  Here, Zaghia’s motion and attached exhibits, in addition to the proof of service filed
by Lycée Français, confirm that a copy of the  summons and complaint was handed to Zaghia
personally – a method of service specifically authorized by Rule 4.  Lycée Français is not simply
relying on Zaghia’s actual notice of the lawsuit, but it relies on the statutorily authorized manner
in which service was accomplished.  Hence, service on Zaghia was not defective on this basis.

D. Server Qualifications
Service of process may be made by “[a]ny person who is at least 18 years old and not a
party.”  Fed. R. Civ. P. 4(c).  “At the plaintiff’s request, the court may order that service be made
by a United States marshal or deputy marshal or by a person specifically appointed by the court.”
Fed. R. Civ. P. 4(c)(3) (emphasis added).  Zaghia again cites Louisiana law to support his argument
that service here must first have been attempted by the sheriff or a court-appointed official.37  Yet,
again, because Lycée Français  followed federal procedure  in making service , Louisiana’s
requirements are inapplicable.  In short, Zaghia cannot look to Louisiana law to challenge whether
the constable met the federal requirements.  And Zaghia does not contradict Lycée Français’s claim
that the constable “is over 18,”38 nor does he claim that the constable is a party to the action.39  As
such, the constable was qualified under Rule 4(c) to serve process on Zaghia in this matter.
E. The Misspelling of Defendant’s First Name
“[S]ervice of process is not legally defective simply because the complaint misnames the
defendant in some insignificant way.”  Morrel v. Nationwide Mut. Fire Ins. Co., 188 F.3d 218, 224
(4th Cir. 1999); cf. Cigna Ins. Co. v. Huddleston, 986 F.2d 1418, 1993 WL 58742, at *12 (5th Cir.
Feb. 16, 1993) (refusing to reverse an arbitration award for “technically identif[ying] the wrong
party”).  The Court acknowledges that the complaint and summons both contain a misspelling of
Zaghia’s first name.  But “[a]t every stage of the proceeding, the court must disregard all errors
and defects that do not affect any party’s substantial rights.”  Fed. R. Civ. P. 61.  As the Supreme
Court teaches, “the core function of service is to supply  notice of the pendency of a legal action,
