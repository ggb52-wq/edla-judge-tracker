I. LEGAL STANDARD
It is well established that “the public has a common law right to inspect
and copy judicial records.”  See SEC v. Van Waeyenberghe , 990 F.2d 845,
848 (5th Cir. 1993) (citing Nixon v. Warner Commc'ns, Inc. , 435 U.S. 589,
597 (1978); Belo Broad. Corp. v. Clark , 654 F.2d 423, 429 (5th Cir. 1981)).
The right of access serves to prom ote “trustworthiness of the judicial
process,” check judicial abuses, and provide the public with “a more
complete understanding of the judicial system.”  Id. at 849 (quoting

Littlejohn v. BIC Corp. , 851 F.2d 673, 682 (3d Cir. 1988)).  Public access is
essential to preserving confidence in the judicial system because allowing
important judicial decisions to be ma de “behind closed doors” and then
publicly announced “with the record supporting the court’s decision sealed
from public view” seriously jeopardizes that confidence.  United States v.
Holy Land Found. for Relief & Dev. , 624 F.3d 685, 690 (5th Cir. 2010)
(citation modified).
The common law right of access to judicial records, though, “is not
absolute.”  Van Waeyenberghe, 990 F.2d at 848.  “A court may deny access
to records if the records become a vehicle for improper purposes.”  Holy
Land, 624 F.3d at 689 (citing Nixon, 435 U.S. at 597–98).  When deciding
whether to deny access to records, “t he court must balance the public’s
common law right of access against th e interests favoring nondisclosure.”
Van Waeyenberghe, 990 F.2d at 848.  This balance requires considering “the
presumption in favor of the public ’s access to judicial records.”  See id. at
849; see also June Med. Servs., L.L.C. v. Phillips, 22 F.4th 512, 521 (5th Cir.
2022) (“the working presumption is th at judicial records should not be
sealed”).  The Court must use “caution” in exercising its discretion to seal
public records.  See Holy Land, 624 F.3d at 689.  In other words, “the district
court’s discretion to seal the record of judicial proceedings is to be exercised

charily.”  Van Waeyenberghe, 990 F.2d at 848 (quoting Fed. Savings & Loan
Ins. Corp. v. Blain, 808 F.2d 395, 399 (5th Cir. 1987)); see also Binh Hoa Le
v. Exeter Fin. Corp., 990 F.3d 410, 418 (5th Cir.  2021) (“In our view, courts

any necessary sealing must be “congruent to the need.”  June Med., 22 F.4th

Ultimately, the decision as to access is left to the discretion of the trial
court, which must consider “the rele vant facts and circumstances of the

That the protective order covers the information in the proposed

met the higher standard for a motion to seal, the Court must deny the
motion.
