II. Legal Standard
“The court may impose an appropriate sanction —including the reasonable
expenses and attorney’s fees incurred by any party—on a person who impedes, delays,
or frustrates the fair examination of the deponent.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 30(d)(2).  “The
examination and cross -examination of a deponent proceed as they would at trial
under the Federal Rules of Evidence.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 30(c)(1).  “An objection must be
stated concisely in a nonargumentative and nonsuggestive manner.” Fed. R. Civ. P.
30(c)(2). Conduct impermissible in the courtroom during the questioning of a witness
is usually impermissible at a deposition. S. La. Ethanol, L.L.C. v. Fireman ’s Fund
Ins. Co., No. 1-2715, 2013 WL 1196604, at *7 (E.D. La. Mar. 22, 2013) (citing Landers
v. Kevin Gros Offshore, L.L.C. , No. 08 -1293, 2009 WL 2046587, at *1, *4  (E.D. La.
July 13, 2009) (“[Counsel] repeatedly failed to conduct his examination of Landers as
he would at the trial and denied him the same rights and privileges due a witness
testifying in court at a trial. Sanctions will be imposed.”)). “In trying to solve problems
of misconduct during depositions, Rule 30(c) sweeps broadly.” § 2113 Conduct of
Examination, 8A Fed. Prac. & Proc. Civ. § 2113 (3d ed.). “In general, counsel should
not engage in any conduct during a deposition that would not be allowed in the
presence of a judicial officer.”  Amendments to Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, 146
F.R.D. 401, 666 (U.S. 1993). Leading questions are generally impermissible on direct
examination so as to “guard against the risk of improper suggestion.” Sec. & Exch.
Comm’n v. Goldstone , 317 F.R.D. 147, 165 (D. N.M. 2016) (citing Ellis v. City of
Chicago, 667 F.2d 606, 612 (7th Cir. 1981)).

“Because depositions take place in law offices rather than courtrooms,
adherence to professional standards is vital, for the judge has no direct means of
control.” Redwood v. Dobson, 476 F.3d 462, 469–70 (7th Cir. 2007). Abusive, profane,
and pugnacious behavior in a deposition “degrades the legal profession and mocks
the search for truth that is at the heart of the litigation process.” Carroll v. Jacques
Admiralty L. Firm, P.C. , 110 F.3d 290, 294 (5th Cir. 1997).  “While isolated acts of
discourtesy or loss of temper can be expected . . . systematic and deliberate
abuses. . . cannot go unsanctioned as they are destructive of the very fabric which
holds together the process of pretrial discovery—cooperative exchange of information
without the need for constant court intervention.” Freeman v. Schointuck, 192 F.R.D.
187, 189 (D. Md. 2000).
Courts apply Rule 30’s prospective remedy broadly. S. La. Ethanol, L.L.C. v.
Fireman’s Fund Ins. Co. , 2013 WL  1196604, at * 8; see also Soule v. RSC Equip .
Rental, Inc. , No. 11 -2022, 2012 WL 5060059, at * 3. (E.D. La. Oct. 18, 2012)
(terminating a deposition when a n attorney yelled , pound ed on the table, us ed
confrontational or argumentative language, accus ed a witness of lying or providing
false or untrue testimony, and disru pted witness testimony). Rule 30(d)(2) does not
expressly set forth a list of available sanctions . S. La. Ethanol, L.L.C., 2013 WL
1196604, at *8 (citing Glick v. Molloy , 2013 WL 140100, at *2 (D. Mont. Jan. 10,
2013)). Payment of attorney’s fees for the deposition of a non -party can be an
appropriate sanction under 30(d)(2). Howell v. Avante Services, LLC, No. 12-293, 2013
WL 824715, at *5 (E.D. La. Mar. 6, 2013).

Plaintiff’s counsel took advantage of the opportunity for a one-sided deposition
and, in doing so, abused the discovery process. See Mitnor Corp. v. Club
Condominiums, 339 F.R.D. 312, 316 (N.D. Fla. 2021) (“Unfortunately, some counsel
use the lack of judicial oversight inherent in depositions to obstruct the proceedings
or to intimidate parties, witnesses, or counsel.”) (citing Ethicon Endo-Surgery v. U.S.
Surgical Corp. , 160 F.R.D. 98, 99 (S.D. Ohio 1995) ). “[T]he witness comes to the
deposition to testify, not to indulge in a parody of Charlie McCarthy, with lawyers
coaching or bending the witness’s words to mold a legally convenient record. It is the
witness—not the lawyer—who is the witness.” Hall v. Clifton Precision , 150 F.R.D.
525, 527 (E.D. P a. 1993) . The numerous procedural and substantive flaws in
Johnson’s deposition bely this fundamental premise.
The Judges of the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Louisiana
adopted the Louisiana State Bar Association Code of Professionalism on August 4,
1999. That Code sets forth fifteen principles:
• My word is my bond.
• I will conduct myself with honesty, dignity, civility, courtesy and fairness and
will not engage in any demeaning or derogatory actions or commentary
toward others.

• I will not knowingly make statements of fact or law that are untrue or
misleading and I will clearly identify for other counsel changes I have made
in documents submitted to me.

• I will be punctual in my communication with clients, other counsel and the
court. I will honor scheduled appearances and will cooperate with other
counsel in all respects.

• I will allow counsel fair opportunity to respond and will grant reasonable
requests for extensions of time.

Plaintiff’s counsel’s conduct in this case implicates many of these principles.
It can be difficult to detect tone or demeanor from a cold record. Not so here.
The transcript shows that Johnson remained polite, patient, and even apologetic for
the duration of the deposition. By contrast, Plaintiff’s counsel’s behavior was grossly
unprofessional under the standards set forth in the Code of Professionalism and the
jurisprudence discussed above. The transcript does not suggest Plaintiff’s counsel

“When there is bad -faith conduct in the course of litigation that could be
adequately sanctioned under the Rules, the court ordinarily should rely on the Rules
rather than the inherent power. ” Chambers v. NASCO, Inc., 501 U.S. 32, 50 (1991) .
Rule 30 is  “up to the task” of imposing an appropriate consequence for Plaintiff
counsel’s lack of professionalism. See id. While the Court reserves the authority to
exercise its inherent power to sanction, including for reasons that overlap with those
discussed herein, it will not do so at this stage and on this record.
IV. Conclusion
For the foregoing reasons, Johnson’s deposition may not be used for any
purpose unless the District Court provides express authorization. The unavailability
of a usable deposition supports allowing the parties a second opportunity to depose
Johnson (provided they do so timely). A second deposition can remedy the procedural
and substantive problems that occurred during the first deposition. Pursuant to Rule
30(d)(2), Defendants are entitled to the ir reasonable fees and expenses incurred in
connection with the instant motion.
 Accordingly,
 IT IS ORDERED that Defendants’ Motion to Quash (R. Doc. 90) is
GRANTED. The August 11, 2026 deposition of Michelle Johnson shall not be used
for any purpose absent express authorization from the District Court.
