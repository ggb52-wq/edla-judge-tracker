SUMMARY JUDGMENT STANDARD
 “Under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 56, summary judgment is appropriate if ‘there is
no genuine dispute as to any material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of
law.’”37 Typically, “the party who moves for summary judgment bears the initial burden . . . . [of]
showing that a reasonable jury could not find for the nonmovant, based on the burdens that would
apply at trial.”38 “For a defendant, this means showing that the record cannot support a win for the
plaintiff—either because the plaintiff has a failure of proof on an essential element of its claim or
because the defendant has insurmountable proof on its affirmative defense to that claim.”39 “If the
defendant succeeds on that showing, the burden shifts to the plaintiff to demonstrate that there is
a genuine issue of material fact and that the evidence favoring the plaintiff permits a jury verdict
in the plaintiff’s favor.”40

“The doctrine of qualified immunity shields officials from civil liability so long as their conduct
‘does not violate clearly established statutory or constitutional rights of which a reasonable person
would have known.’ ”42 This creates an affirmative defense “that shields public officials sued in
their individual capacities[.]”43 Accordingly, “[w]hen reviewing a motion for summary judgment
based upon the affirmative defense of qualified immunity, we engage in a two-pronged inquiry.”44
The first prong is “the constitutional question, asking whether the officer’s conduct violated a
federal right.”45 The second prong asks “whether that right was clearly established at the time of
the violation.”46 “Courts are ‘permitted to exercise their sound discretion in deciding which of the
two prongs of the qualified immunity analysis should be addressed first in light of the
circumstances in the particular case at hand.’”47
 Whether or not qualified immunity is involved, “[a] plaintiff suing for a constitutional
violation has the ultimate burden” in proving the first prong—that is, “to show that the defendant
violated a constitutional right.”48 “But when qualified immunity is involved . . . a plaintiff has the
additional burden to show that the violated right was ‘clearly established’ at the time of the alleged
violation.”49 “The touchstone of this inquiry is whether a reasonable person would have believed
that his conduct conformed to the constitutional standard in light of the information available to
him and the clearly established law.” 50 “For a right to be clearly established, the ‘constitutional

III. Assuming a constitutional violation exists,  Defendants are entitled to qualified
immunity on all § 1983 claims, except for the Monell claim, because of the lack of
clearly established law.
 Even if Defendants’ conduct violated Plaintiff’s Fourth and Fourteenth Amendment rights,
Defendants are entitled to qualified immunity  because it was not clearly established in June of
2020 that Defendants’ conduct was prohibited by the Fourth and Fourteenth Amendments.
 When analyzing whether a defendant is entitled to qualified immunity, courts engage in a
two-prong inquiry. First, the Court must determine “the constitutional question, asking whether
the officer’s conduct violated a federal right.” 94 Second, the Court asks “whether that right was
clearly established at the time of the violation.”95
 For the reasons discussed above, it is unclear whether the order to deploy tear gas, and the
subsequent deployment of tear gas, on the night of June 3, 2020, constitute a violation of Plaintiff’s
constitutional rights. The Court is , therefore, free to begin, and, in this case, end its qualified
immunity analysis with the second prong: whether the right was clearly established.96
