II. LEGAL STANDARD
A litigant generally has no right to a court-appointed attorney in a civil case.5
The Court’s power to “appoint counsel” is derived from 28 U.S.C. § 1915(e)(1), which
provides that the “court may request an attorney to represent any person unable to
afford counsel.”  The United  States District Court for the Eastern District of
Louisiana’s Resolution for Appointment of Pro Bono Counsel in Civil Cases requires
demonstration of the inability to pay privately  retained counsel under 28 U.S.C. §
1915(e)(1). The decision whether to appoint counsel rests within the sound discretion
of the trial court, 6 and the C ourt may not appoint counsel as a matter of course or
ordinary practice.7 Thus, a Plaintiff, even if demonstrably indigent, is not entitled to
appointed counsel as a matter of right. 8  Rather, the C ourt may request the
appointment of counsel for an indigent litigant when exceptional circumstances exist
to warrant such appointment. 9 And while compulsory appointment of counsel is not
authorized by 28 U.S.C. § 1915(e)(1)’s in forma pauperis statute, 10 the Court has
inherent authority to appoint counsel.11 Indeed, while a Court has great discretion in

determining whether to appoint  counsel, once it determines that a particular case
presents exceptional circumstances, it abuses its  discretion by declining to appoint
counsel.12
The Fifth Circuit has identified several factors that should be considered in
determining whether a case presents exceptional circumstances: (1) the type and
complexity of the case; (2) whether the indigent is capable of adequately presenting
his case;13 (3) whether the indigent is in a position to investigate adequately the case;
and (4) whether the evidence will consist in large  part of conflicting testimony so as
to require skill in the presentation of evidence and in cross examination.14 The Court
should also consider whether appointment would be a service to the C ourt and all
parties in the case by sharpening the issues and shaping the examination of
witnesses, and thus shortening the trial and assisting in a just determination.15

Plaintiff has submitted the responsive information required by the AO Form
240, which reflects that Plaintiff satisfies the financial eligibility requirements and
is unable to afford private counsel. Plaintiff advised during the November 2, 2026
telephone status conference that he made significant efforts to retain counsel. Having
reviewed that completed form, the Court finds that Plaintiff has met that initial

financial eligibility threshold.  The Court now turns to the factors set forth by the
Fifth Circuit.
All of the factors identified by the Fifth Circuit in determining the exception
case for appointment of counsel are satisfied in this case. This matter involves claims
of employment discrimination and retaliation, which requires specialized legal
knowledge and involves significant complexity. Current law on employment
discrimination, including potential shifting burdens, is also complex .16 Due to this
complexity, the Plaintiff is likely unable to adequately present or investigate his case.
The evidence will likely consist of conflicting testimony, which may be difficult for the
Plaintiff to present and cross-examine. The Court further finds that appointment will
be of service to the Court in sharpening the issues for trial. C onsidering Plaintiff’s
inability to pay, the likely difficulty the Plaintiff will face in properly investigating
and presenting his claims, and the convenience of the Court, there is a sufficient basis
to seek a volunteer attorney from the EDLA’s Civil Pro Bono Panel.
