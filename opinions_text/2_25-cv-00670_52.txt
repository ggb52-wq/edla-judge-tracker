III. LAW & ANALYSIS
A. SCI’s Motion to Dismiss for Failure to Prosecute
I
n the alternative to its Motion for Summary Judgment, SCI moves to dismiss Plaintiff’s
claims for failure to prosecute. Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 41(b) provides that “[i ]f the
plaintiff fails to prosecute or to comply with these rules or a court order, a defendant may move to
dismiss the action or any claim against it.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 41(b). Unless otherwise specified,
dismissal under Rule 41(b) operates as an adjudication on the merits. Fed. R. Civ. P. 41(b).
The Fifth Circuit requires “a clear record of delay or contumacious conduct by the
plaintiff,” together with an express finding that “no lesser sanction would suffice to prompt diligent
prosecution,” before dismissal under Rule 41(b) is appropriate. See Meyers v. Jefferson Parish
Sheriff’ s Office, No. CV 19-5094, 2021 WL 1894862, at *2 (E.D. La. May 11, 2021) (citing Raborn
v. Inpatient Mgmt. Partners Inc., 278 F. App’x 402, 404 (5th Cir. 2008)). A clear record of delay
Case 2:25-cv-00670-ILRL-KWR     Document 52     Filed 09/22/26     Page 7 of 14
8
exists where there have been “significant periods of total inactivity.” Id.  (quoting Berry v.
CIGNA/RSI CIGNA, 975 F.2d 1188, 1191 n.5 (5th Cir. 1992)). Once a clear record of delay is
established, at least one of the following “aggravated factors” will ordinarily also be present: “(1)
the delay was caused by the plaintiff, as opposed to his attorney; (2) the defendant suffered actual
prejudice; or (3) the delay was caused by intentional conduct.” Id.  Additionally, Federal Rule of
Civil Procedure 37(b)(2)(A) authorizes a court to impose sanctions when a party “fails to obey an
order to provide or permit discovery.” Available sanctions include dismissal of the action. Fed. R.
Civ. P. 37(b)(2)(A).
As SCI explains in its motion, Plaintiff appears to have ceased participating meaningfully
in the litigation since October 29, 2025. Plaintiff has failed to participate in initial disclosures,
discovery, or discovery conferences; failed to submit witness lists, exhibit lists, and expert
disclosures as required by the Scheduling Order; and failed to comply with the Court’s August 5,
2026, Order directing her to respond to outstanding discovery. (Rec. Doc. 51-1 at 3.) The Court’s
August 5, 2026, Order afforded Plaintiff fourteen (14) days to respond to the outstanding discovery.
The Court further noted that Plaintiff’s counsel had failed to respond to discovery pursuant to
Rules 33 and 34 and had failed to participate in a Rule 37.1 conference. This shows that Plaintiff
is engaging in the conduct described by Rule 41(b), warranting the Defendant to move for
dismissal. Fed. R. Civ. P. 41(b).
Plaintiff has also failed to respond to SCI’s pending motions. Plaintiff’s response to SCI’s
Motion for Summary Judgment and Motion to Compel was due on July 28, 2026, pursuant to
Local Rule 7.2, but no response was filed. As of August 25, 2026, Plaintiff has not responded to
the Motion for Summary Judgment, has not complied with the discovery obligations identified in
SCI’s Motion to Compel, and has not otherwise demonstrated meaningful participation in this

B. SCI’s Motion for Summary Judgment
Summary judgment is appropriate when the pleadings, depositions, interrogatory answers,
and admissions, together with any affidavits, show that there is no genuine issue as to any material
fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(c); see
also Celotex Corp.  v. C atrett, 477 U.S. 317, 327 (1986). Material in support or opposition of a
motion for summary judgment may be considered if it is “capable of being ‘presented in a form
that would be admissible in evidence.’” LSR Consulting, LLC v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A., 835 F.3d
530, 534 (5th Cir. 2016) (emphasis in original) (quoting Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(c)(2)). Courts view all
facts and evidence in the light most favorable to the non-moving party, but “refrain from making
Case 2:25-cv-00670-ILRL-KWR     Document 52     Filed 09/22/26     Page 9 of 14
10
credibility determinations or weighing the evidence.” Delta & Pine Land Co. v. Nationwide
Agribusiness Ins. Co., 530 F.3d 395, 398 (5th Cir. 2008).
Where the movant bears the burden of proof, it must “demonstrate the absence of a genuine
issue of material fact” using competent summary judgment evidence. Celotex, 477 U.S. at 323.
However, “where the non-movant bears the burden of proof at trial, the movant may merely point
to an absence of evidence.” Lindsey v. Sears Roebuck & Co., 16 F.3d 616, 618 (5th Cir. 1994).
Should the movant meet its burden, the burden shifts to the non-movant, who must show by
“competent summary judgment evidence” that there is a genuine issue of material fact. See
Matsushita Elec. Indus. Co., Ltd. v. Zenith Radio Corp., 475 U.S. 574, 586 (1986); Lindsey, 16
F.3d at 618. Accordingly, conclusory rebuttals of the pleadings are insufficient to avoid summary
judgment. Bargher v. White , 928 F.3d 439, 444–45 (5th Cir. 2019). Additionally, conclusory
allegations, denials, improbable inferences, unsubstantiated assertions, speculation, and legalistic
argumentation do not adequately substitute for specific facts showing a genuine factual issue for
trial. See Little v. Liquid Air Corp., 37 F.3d 1069, 1075 (5th Cir. 1994). There is no genuine issue
of material fact if, even viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the non-moving party,
no reasonable trier of fact could find for the non-moving party, thus entitling the moving party to
judgment as a matter of law. Smith v. Amedisys, 298 F.3d 434, 440 (5th Cir. 2002).
In a discrimination case, the plaintiff bears the ultimate burden of proving that the
defendant intentionally discriminated against her because of her race. St. Mary’s Honor Ctr. v.
Hicks, 509 U.S. 502, 508 (1993). A plaintiff may establish intentional discrimination through
either direct or circumstantial evidence. Wallace v. Methodist Hosp. Sys., 271 F.3d 212, 219 (5th
Cir. 2001). Where a defendant moves for summary judgment on an employment discrimination
claim based on circumstantial evidence, the Court utilizes the burden-shifting framework
Case 2:25-cv-00670-ILRL-KWR     Document 52     Filed 09/22/26     Page 10 of 14
11
established in McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green, 411 U.S. 792 (1973). See Turner v. Kansas
City Southern Ry. Co., 675 F.3d 887, 892 (5th Cir. 2012). The first step of the  McDonnell
Douglas analysis requires “[t]he plaintiff [to] establish a prima facie case that the defendant made
an employment decision that was motivated by a protected factor.”  Id. (quoting Mayberry v.
Vought Aircraft Co., 55 F.3d 1086, 1089 (5th Cir.1995)). If the plaintiff establishes a  prima
facie case, the court proceeds to the next stage of the analysis, where “the defendant bears the
burden of producing evidence that its employment decision was based on a legitimate
nondiscriminatory reason.”  Id. If the defendant carries its burden, the analysis moves to the
third McDonnell Douglas step, where “[t]he burden ... shifts back to the plaintiff to prove that the
defendant's proffered reasons were a pretext for discrimination.” Id.
At the pretext stage, a plaintiff’s subjective belief that discrimination motivated an
employment decision, standing alone, is insufficient to defeat summary judgment. Conclusory
allegations, speculation, and unsubstantiated assertions likewise cannot establish pretext. Clark v.
Sw. Bell Tel. Co., No. CA3:96-CV-2913-BC, 1998 WL 292366, at *3 (N.D. Tex. May 27, 1998);
Hervey v. Miss. Dep’t of Educ. , 404 F. App’x 865, 869 (5th Cir. 2010). Consistent with this
principle, the Fifth Circuit has affirmed summary judgment where a plaintiff relies merely on her
subjective belief that discrimination occurred to establish pretext. See Price v. Marathon Cheese
Corp., 119 F.3d 330, 337 (5th Cir. 1997); Lawrence v. Univ. of Tex. Med. Branch at Galveston,
163 F.3d 309, 313 (5th Cir. 1999) (per curiam); McVille v. Inter-Cmty. Healthcare, Inc., 460 F.
App’x 353, 356 (5th Cir. 2012).
McKinley has not established a prima facie case of race discrimination under a disparate-
treatment theory. She identifies eight alleged instances of disparate treatment: (1) exclusion from
high-earning areas; (2) hostile remarks by managers, including remarks concerning her

12
compensation; (3) denial of promotional opportunities in favor of less-qualified white employees;
(4) reassignment through a lateral transfer after engaging in protected EEO activity; (5) denial of
access to at-need services and key sales events; (6) fabri cated write-ups and disciplinary actions
intended to undermine her credibility and secure her termination; (7) a company culture in which
only non-African American employees were permitted to advance to management positions; and
(8) retaliation for opposing discrimination. (Rec. Doc. 32 at 20–22.) As noted above, the first step
of the McDonnell Douglas analysis requires “[t]he plaintiff [to] establish a prima facie case that
the defendant made an employment decision that was motivated by a protected factor.” Turner,
675 F.3d at 892 (quoting Mayberry v. Vought Aircraft Co., 55 F.3d 1086, 1089 (5th Cir. 1995)).
McKinley, however, has offered no evidence establishing that any of these alleged actions were
motivated by her race. Indeed, the allegations in her Amended Complaint acknowledge that she
was subject to a workplace policy that applied universally and that she was terminated for
insubordination. Accordingly, McKinley has failed to establish a prima facie case of disparate
treatment.
Even assuming McKinley could establish a prima facie case, SCI has satisfied the second
step of the McDonnell Douglas framework by articulating legitimate, nondiscriminatory reasons
for the challenged employment actions. McDonnell, 411 U.S. at 802. Specifically, each of the three
disciplinary measures, including McKinley’s termination, resulted from violations of company
policy. (Rec. Doc. 48-1 at 5.) The September 26, 2022, incident that resulted in McKinley’s first
written warning arose from her failure to comply with the Lead Protection Policy and her
subsequent inappropriate and unprofessional conduct. (Rec. Doc. 48 -1 at 15.)  Likewise, the
August 25, 2023, incident that resulted in her second written warning arose from her failure to
comply with the Lead Protection Policy. (Id.) Following that second warning, McKinley was
Case 2:25-cv-00670-ILRL-KWR     Document 52     Filed 09/22/26     Page 12 of 14
13
expressly informed that it constituted her final warning. (Id.) McKinley was ultimately terminated
after she accepted an employee’s resignation despite having been specifically instructed not to do
so because doing so would make it more difficult to transfer the employee to another location. (Id.)
SCI also has presented evidence that the workplace policy that allegedly excluded McKinley from
high-earning areas and sales events was applied uniformly to employees regardless of race. (Id. at
8.) SCI also has shown that the compensation plan applied to McKinley was a nationwide
compensation plan applied to all sales managers in New Orleans. (Id. at 9.)  Thus, SCI has
identified legitimate, nondiscriminatory reasons for the challenged disciplinary actions and
McKinley’s termination and has satisfied its burden under the second step of the McDonnell
Douglas framework.
McKinley has failed to identify any evidence demonstrating that SCI’s stated reasons were
pretextual. Her conclusory allegations that the disciplinary actions were fabricated and motivated
by racial animus are insufficient to create a genuine dispute of material fact, particularly where she
has identified no evidence contradicting SCI’s stated reasons or demonstrating that similarly
situated employees outside her protected class were treated more favorably. McKinley has not
responded to SCI’s Motion for Summary Judgment or to SCI’s discovery requests, despite SCI’s
Motion to Compel. Accordingly, she has not identified any evidence creating a genuine dispute of
material fact as to whether SCI’s stated reasons were false or whether its actions were instead
motivated by racial discrimination. McKinley therefore has failed to carry her burden under the
McDonnell Douglas framework.
For the reasons set forth above, SCI is entitled to judgment as a matter of law on
McKinley’s remaining disparate -treatment claim. McKinley has failed to produce sufficient
evidence to establish a genuine dispute of material fact as to an essential element of her claim.
