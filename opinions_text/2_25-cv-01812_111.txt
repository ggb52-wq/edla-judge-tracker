 To define the “participation in a venture” element, the Court looks to the text
of the statute. For purposes of the criminal provisions, Section 1591(e)(4) defines
“participation in a venture” as “knowingly assisting, supporting, or facilitating a
violation of subsection (a)(1),” which criminalizes the use of force or coercion to cause
someone to engage in a commercial sex act. Section 1595 does not include a definition
for the phrase, thus raising the question of its interpretation for civil liability.
 While the Fifth Circuit has not “substantively engaged with the beneficiary
liability theory in § 1595(a) in the sex trafficking context ,”61 a t least three  other
circuit courts have analyzed this provision. The Eleventh Circuit in Red Roof applied
the plain meaning of “participation in a venture ,” rather than incorporate the
definition from Section 1591.  The Eleventh Circuit held that the text of the statute
overcame the usual presumption that Congress intends phrases in the same statute
to mean the same thing for two reasons: (1) § 1591 states that its definition of
“participation in a venture” only applies to that section, and (2) the application of the
criminal definition in the civil provisions “makes no sense”  because it creates a
nonsensical sentence. 62 Ultimately, drawing upon Black’s Law Dictionary and the
Oxford English Dictionary, the Eleventh Circuit interpreted “participation in a
venture” to mean “[taking] part in a common undertaking or enterprise involving risk
and potential profit.”63 The Second and Seventh Circuits likewise declined to import

3. Knowledge that the venture engaged in an act in violation of § 1591
 The parties also dispute which mens rea standard appl ies to TAMS. TAMS
equates “knew or should have known” to “actual knowledge or reckless disregard” as
to Doe’s personal trafficking.77 Doe argues that § 1595(a) does not require that a hotel
had notice of a plaintiff’s tracking specifically.78

particular victim is required. The Eleventh Circuit holds the victim -specific view—
the defendant must have knowledge as to the plaintiff.79 The Seventh Circuit rejected
the victim-specific approach because Congress would have “simply said so” if it meant
to require actual or constructive knowledge of the specific victim.80
 The Court finds that the Seventh Circuit’s interpretation is consistent with the
plain meaning of the statutory text.  Section 1595 does not require a civil litigant to
show that the defendant knew that a particular litigant was a victim. Rather, the
plain text requires only that that person benefited from participation in a venture he
