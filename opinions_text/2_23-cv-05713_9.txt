II. Standards of Review for Frivolousness
Pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1915(e)(2), § 1915A  and 42 U.S.C. § 1997e(c), the Court is
required to sua sponte  dismiss cases filed by prisoners proceeding in forma pauperis  upon a
determination that they are frivolous.  The Court has broad discretion in determining the frivolous
nature of the complaint.  See Cay v. Estelle, 789 F.2d 318, 325 (5th Cir. 1986), modified on other
grounds, Booker v. Koonce, 2 F.3d 114 (5th Cir. 1993).  However, the Court may not sua sponte
dismiss an action merely because of questionable le gal theories or unlikely factual allegations in
the complaint.
Under this statute, a claim is frivolous when it lacks an arguable basis either in law or fact.
Neitzke v. Williams , 490 U.S. 319, 324-25 (1989); Talib v. Gilley , 138 F.3d 211, 213 (5th Cir.
1998).  “A [claim] lacks an arguable basis in law if  it is based on an indis putably meritless legal
theory, such as if the complaint alleges the viol ation of a legal interest which clearly does not
exist.”  Harper v. Showers, 174 F.3d 716, 718 (5th Cir. 1999) (quoting Davis v. Scott, 157 F.3d
882, 889 (5th Cir. 1998)).  It lacks an arguable fact ual basis only if the fact s alleged are “clearly
baseless,” a category encompassing fancif ul, fantastic, and delu sional allegations.  Denton v.
Hernandez, 504 U.S. 25, 32-33 (1992) (citing Neitzke, 490 U.S. at 327-28).  Thus, the Court must
determine whether plaintiff’s claims are based on an indisputably meritless legal theory or clearly
baseless factual allegations.  Reeves v. Collins , 27 F.3d 174, 176 (5th Cir. 1994); Jackson v.
Vannoy, 49 F.3d 175, 176-77 (5th Cir. 1995); Moore v. Mabus, 976 F.2d 268, 269 (5th Cir. 1992).
III. Analysis
A. No Claims Asserted Against Named Defendants
During the Spears Hearing, Robertson testified that he had no intention to sue any of the
prison officials named as defendants.  He indi cated, instead, that the person who helped him

prepare his pro se complaint must have included Sheriff Soignet, Warden Ledet, and Ms. Tomlin
(a person he did not even know) as  defendants, but they are not who he meant to sue.  Robertson
acknowledged that these officials should not be in his lawsuit, because he intended to sue the
company that packaged the sardines.
Thus, Robertson has no claims or basis for li ability against the three named defendants,
Sheriff Soignet, Warden Ledet, and Ms. Tomlin.  A state actor may only be liable under § 1983 if
he or she “was personally involved in the acts causing the deprivation of his constitutional rights
or a causal connection exists between an act of the official and the alleged constitutional violation.”
Douthit v. Jones, 641 F.2d 345 (5th Cir. 1981); Watson v. Interstate Fire & Casualty Co., 611 F.2d
120 (5th Cir. 1980).  Robertson concedes that none of the named defendants, nor any other prison
official, had any involvement in the packaging of the sardines or  a role in the cause of his mouth
injury.  Robertson simply has no basis for a § 1983 claim against these defendants.
Robertson also indicated that he did not plan to sue Warden Ledet for her response to his
grievance complaint appeal.  Even if he did, that would not be sufficient to state a cognizable claim
against the Warden, because plaintiff has no protected right to an effective or successful grievance
process.  Bonneville v. Basse, 536 F. App’x 502, 503 (5th Cir. 2013); Propes v. Mays, 169 F. App’x
183, 184-85 (5th Cir. 2006); Geiger v. Jowers, 404 F.3d 371, 373-74 (5th Cir. 2005).
Robertson also would have no non-frivolous claim against defendant Tomlin, who is sued
as the supervisor of the medical unit.  Robertson testified that he did not know who Tomlin was or
why she would be included in his suit.  In his complaint, when broadly construed, Robinson sought
damages for the denial of medical care for his “broken tooth.”  This prayer for relief was made in
the complaint even though Robinson testified at the hearing that he had no teeth in the back of his
mouth where the metal piece cut his gum because all of the teeth had been pulled.

Nevertheless, assuming a nurse or medical care provider at the jail did not provide him
follow up care for his pain and suffering, his claims against Tomlin as a supervisor are insufficient
under § 1983.  As noted above, since Robertson did not know who Tomlin was, he offered no
allegation of her personal involvement in the medical care he was provided (or denied) to hold her
personally liable under § 1983.  Douthit, 641 F.2d at 345.  Further, as a supervisory official, Tomlin
cannot be held responsible pursuant to § 1983 under any theory of respondeat superior or simply
because an employee or subordina te allegedly violated the plai ntiff’s constitutional rights.  See
Alton v. Tex. A&M Univ., 168 F.3d 196, 200 (5th Cir. 1999); see also, Oliver v. Scott, 276 F.3d 736,
742 (5th Cir. 2002) (“Section 1983 does not create supervisory or respondeat superior liability.”).
Thus, any intended claims against Tomlin as a supervisor are frivolous and otherwise fail to state
a claim for which relief can be granted and should be dismissed.
Therefore, based on Robertson’s testimony at the Spears Hearing4 and his concession that
he did not intend to sue the thre e named defendants, the claims against Sheriff Soignet, Warden
Ledet, and Medical Supervisor Tomlin must be dismissed pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1915(e) and
§ 1915A as frivolous and for failure to state a claim for which relief can be granted.

First, the TPSO is not a suab le entity.  A plaintiff bringing a § 1983 complaint is required

491 U.S. 58 (1989).  A party’s capacity to sue or be sued is a requirement under Fed. R. Civ. Proc.
17(b) and per the rule, this capacity is determined by reference to the law of the state in which the
district court sits.  The State of Louisiana grants no such legal status to any law enforcement office
or department.  Liberty Mut. Ins. Co. v. Grant Parish Sherriff’ s Dep’t, 350 So. 2d 236 (La. App. 3d
Cir. 1997), writ refused, 352 So. 2d 235 (La. 1977).  For this reason, “a parish sheriff’s office is
not a legal entity capable of being sued in a federal civil rights action.”  Francis v. Terrebonne Par.
Sheriff’ s Office, No. 08-4972, 2009 WL 4730707, at *2 (E.D. La. Dec. 9, 2009) (citing Cozzo v.
Tangipahoa Par. Council-President Gov’t, 279 F.3d 273, 283 (5th Cir. 2002)); see also Williamson
v. Louisiana, No. 08-4598, 2008 WL 5082911, at *3 (E.D. La. Nov. 24, 2008); Fitch v. Terrebonne
Par. Sheriff Dep’t, No. 06-3307, 2006 WL 2690077, at *2 (E.D. La. Sept. 18, 2006).

Because the TPSO is not a juridical or suable entity or person to be held liable under § 1983,
any claims Robertson would have against it would be subject to dismissal as frivolous or for failure
to state a claim for which relief can be granted.
In addition, to the extent Robertson arguably alleges a claim for denial of access to the
courts or some infringement on his ability to fi le suit against the sardine company, it would be
futile to allow him to name a potential responsible person or to expound on that claim.  Prisoners
have a constitutional right to access the courts.  Bounds v. Smith, 430 U.S. 817, 821 (1977).  The
right “is founded in the Due Process Clause and assures that no person will be denied the
opportunity to present to the judiciary allegations concerni ng violations of fundamental
constitutional rights.”  Wolff v. McDonnell, 418 U.S. 539, 579 (1974).  This right is not unlimited
and provides only that a prisoner  or any person be provided “‘a reasonably adequate opportunity
to present claimed violations of fundamental constitutional rights to the courts.’”  See Lewis v.
Casey, 518 U.S. 343, 351 (1996) (quoting Bounds, 430 U.S. at 825).  Furthermore, the Supreme
Court has not extended the right of access the courts to encompass more than the ability to prepare
and transmit a necessary legal document to a court. See Brewer v. Wilkinson, 3 F.3d 816, 821 (5th
Cir. 1993).
The Supreme Court also has held that the ri ght to access the courts “rest[s] on the
recognition that the right is ancillary to the underlying claim, without which a plaintiff cannot have
suffered injury by being shut out of court.”  Christopher v. Harbury, 536 U.S. 403, 415 (2002).  A
prisoner alleging such a violation must  demonstrate an actual injury.  Lewis, 518 U.S. at 351.
Because of the actual injury requirement, “the underlying cause of action, whether anticipated or
lost, is an element that must be  described in the complaint, just  as much as allegations must
describe the official acts frustrating the litigation.”  Christopher, 536 U.S. at 415.  The “injury

9

requirement is not satisfied by just any type  of frustrated legal cl aim.”  (emphasis added) Lewis,
518 U.S. at 353.  Instead, a plaintiff must demonstrate that the lack of access has prevented him
from filing or caused him to lose a pending case that attacks eith er his convicti on or seeks “to
vindicate ‘basic constitutional rights’” in a civil rights action under § 1983.  Id. at 353-54 (quoting
Wolff, 418 U.S. at 579).  Without a showing of this type of actual injury, a plaintiff lacks standing
to pursue a claim of denial of access to courts.  Id. at 349.
Thus, a prisoner’s right to acces s courts is limite d to allowing opportun ities to submit
nonfrivolous claims challenging their convictions or the conditions of their confinement.  Jones v.
Greninger, 188 F.3d 322, 325 (5th Cir. 1999).  “ Bounds does not guarantee inmates the
wherewithal to transform themselves into litigating engines capable of filing everything from
shareholder derivative actions to slip-and-fall claims.”  (emphasis added) Lewis, 518 U.S. at 355;
Johnson v. Rodriguez, 110 F.3d 299, 311 (5th Cir. 1997) (holding that secondary legal activity not
protected by constitution).
Robertson’s claims about biting into someth ing packaged with his sardines does not
involve a challenge to his conviction or the conditions of his confinement at TPCJC.  As he readily
admitted in the Spears Hearing, the presence of the object in  his pre-packaged sardines was not
caused by or related to the prison officials.  Ev en if it did, such a claim would be grounded in
negligence, which does not invoke a pr otected right under the Constitution.  Daniels v. Williams,
474 U.S. 327, 328 (1986) (“[T]he Due Process Clause is simply not implicated by a negligent act
of an official causing unintended loss or injury to life, liberty or property.”); Davidson v. Cannon,
474 U.S. 344, 347 (1986); see Doe v. Taylor Indep. Sch. Dist., 975 F.2d 137, 142 (5th Cir. 1992),
vacated on other grounds, 15 F.3d 443 (5th Cir. 1994) (“Even when constitutional liberty interests
are implicated, not all bodily injuries caused by state actors give rise to a constitutional tort, for it

is well settled that mere negligence does not constitute a deprivation of due process under the
Constitution.”).
In truth, Robertson’s claims focus on neglig ence or products liability against the sardine
packaging company.  As discussed further in the next section of  this Report, Robertson was not
prevented from preparing and tr ansmitting a complaint asserting that claim brought in his prior
civil rights case in Civ. Action 23-2681“J”(1).  Therefore, even if the claims were somehow related

For these reasons, Robertson is not entitled to amend or supplement his existing claims to
add TPSO as a defendant or to expound on his vague reference to and insufficient claim for denial
of access to the courts made in his request for relief.  Any such amendment would be futile.
2. Claims Against Sardine Packaging Company and TPCJC Commissary
As noted above, a prisoner’s Spears Hearing testimony may be construed to amend his
complaint or provide a more definite statement.  Wilson, 926 F.2d at 482.  During the Spears
Hearing, Robertson made very clear his intent to sue the sardine packaging company which he
referred to as a “Geisha company.”  Robertson in fact did file suit in July of 2023, against the jail
commissary and the Kawasho Food USA, Inc. in Civ. Action 23-2681“J”(1).  In that case,
Robertson alleged, as he does here, that he broke off a piece of a tooth wh en he bit into a rock
contained in the package of sardines from Kawasho Food USA, Inc. that he bought in the TPCJC
commissary.  Id., ECF No. 7.  With his complaint in that case, he also submit ted as exhibits the
sardine packet and metal piece or rock.  Id., ECF No. 2.  The Court dismissed Robertson’s case on
October 20, 2023, for lack of subject matter jurisdiction because he had failed to name a state actor

or constitutional violation under § 1983 for purposes of federal questions jurisdiction and failed to
allege any basis to proceed under diversity jurisdiction.  Id., ECF Nos. 7, 8, 9.
While a prisoner’s Spears Hearing testimony is in the nature of an amendment, it would be
futile in this instance to allow Robertson to amend his complaint to include claims and defendants
the Court has already dismissed.  The Court has already determined in Robertson’s prior case that
there is no basis for him to proc eed in this Court under § 1983 or any other federal jurisdictional
grounds against Kawasho Food USA, Inc. or the jail commissary.  Such repetitive claims in an in
forma pauperis prisoner civil rights action would be considered malicious and subject to dismissal
for that reason.  Pursuant to § 1915(e) and § 1915A, a plaintiff is not allowed “to relitigate claims
which allege substantially the same facts arising from a common series of events which have
already been unsuccessfully litigated by the [prisoner].”  Wilson v. Lynaugh, 878 F.2d 846, 850
(5th Cir. 1989).  “[I]t is malicious for a pauper to file a lawsuit that duplicates allegations of another
pending federal lawsuit by the same plaintiff.”  Pittman v. Moore, 980 F.2d 994, 995 (5th Cir. 1993)
(emphasis added) (internal quotation marks omitted); see Lewis v. Sec’y of Pub. Safety & Corr.,
508 F. App’x 341, 344 (5th Cir. 2013); Bailey v. Johnson, 846 F.2d 1019, 1021 (5th Cir. 1988)
(“[A]n IFP complaint that merely repeats pending or previously litigated claims may be considered
abusive and dismissed under . . . section 1915(d).”).  For this reason, the Court need not allow an
amendment of this complaint to allow Robertson to pursue repetitive claims.
