II. APPLICABLE LAW
A. Dismissal Without Prejudice Under Rule 4(m)
 Timely service of process is important.  A lengthy delay between filing and service risks
prejudice to the defendant.2  Rule 4(m) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure requires a plaintiff
to serve a defendant within ninety days of filing the complaint.  If the plaintiff fails to : (1) serve
within that timeframe or (2) show good cause for an extension, the Court “must dismiss the action
without prejudice against that defendant or order that service be made within a specified time.”3
B. Heightened Standard for Dismissal of a Time-Barred Claim
When a court dismisses a claim without  prejudice but a re -filed action would be time -
barred, it effectively dismisses the claim with  prejudice.4  “[D]ismissal with prejudice ‘is an
extreme sanction that deprives a litigant of the opportunity to pursue his claim.’”5  So courts apply
a heightened standard to dismiss without prejudice a claim that would be time -barred if re -
instituted later.6

“A district court’s ‘dismissal with prejudice is warranted only where “a clear record of
delay or contumacious conduct by the plaintiff” exists and a “lesser sanction would not better serve
the interests of justice.”’” 7  While this “test is conjunctive, . . . only one of the first element’s
prongs must be present—either a clear record of delay or contumacious conduct.”8
A clear record of delay “must be longer than just a few months; instead, the delay must be
characterized by significant periods of total inactivity.” 9  Dismissals with prejudice are generally
saved for cases of “egregious and sometimes outrageous delays.” 10  A repeated failure to comply
with a court’s pretrial order “amounts to a clear record of delay.”11  Contumacious conduct, on the
other hand, occurs when a party’s “stubborn resistance to authority” supports dismissal with
prejudice.12
Historically, t he Fifth Circuit has affirmed dismissals with prejudice when at least one
aggravating factor has been present: “(1) delay caused by the plaintiff himself and not his attorney;
(2) actual prejudice to the defendant; or (3) delay caused by intentional conduct.’”13  The presence
of an aggravating factor, however, is not required. Satisfaction of the two principal elements can
justify dismissal with prejudice.14

 c.    Heightened Standard
 Because Plaintiff’s Title VII retaliation claim would be time -barred, a dismissal without
prejudice would effectively operate as a dismissal with prejudice.  Therefore, I will apply the
heightened standard to determine if that result is appropriate.  While dismissal is an “extreme
sanction,” if Plaintiff does not file proof of service before the period for objecting to this Report
and Recommendation expires, dismissal in this case is warranted.
As of today’s date, over six months have passed since Plaintiff filed her lawsuit.  ECF No.
1.  And her last filing in the Court’s docket was over three months ago. 40  ECF No. 29.  Further,
Plaintiff has been warned on three separate occasions that failure to serve would result in
dismissal.41  This behavior sufficiently shows a significant period of delay and contumacious
conduct that warrants dismissal.  Additionally, at least one aggravating factor is present: a “delay
caused by the plaintiff h[er]self and not h[er] attorney.” 42  Here, Plaintiff is proceeding pro se  so
the delay cannot be attributed to her attorney. She is responsible for the failure to serve.
“Assessments of fines, costs, or damages against the plaintiff or his counsel, attorney
disciplinary measures, conditional dismissal, dismissal without prejudice, . . .  explicit warnings”
or other lesser sanction s would not better serve the interests of justice in this case. 43  Because
plaintiff is proceeding pro se , attorney discipline would not be effective.  And fines, costs, or
damages assessed against Plaintiff would not be prudent.  Explicit warnings have already been
implemented without effect.  Dismissal of the lawsuit in its entirety is now warranted.
