C. The Applicability and Validity of 20 C.F.R. § 416.920c
Barnes argues that non-tre ating medical opinions, such as that by SAMCs, cannot
constitute substantial evidence, effectively arguing that the ALJ applied the wrong legal standard
by giving more weight to the SAMC opinions than his treating sources because the ALJ found the
former “persuasive” and “rejected” the latter.  ECF No. 14-2 at 3-4.  Though he claims otherwise,
Barnes also argues 20 C.F.R. § 416.920c is inconsistent with 20 U.S.C. § 423(d)(5)(B).27
On January 18, 2017, the SSA promulgated new regulations applicable to disability claims
filed on or after March 27, 2017, found at 20 C.F.R. §§ 404.1520c and 416.920c, “revising . . . the
rules regarding the evaluation of medical evidence.” 28  The new regulations  “eliminate the old
hierarchy of medical opinions, no longer provide for inherent or presumptive weight, and do away
with the examining and non-examining phy sician terminology.”29  Rather than considering a
medical opinion’s weight, ALJ must determine how  “persuasive” the opinion is by considering
five factors: (1) supportability, (2) cons istency, (3) relationship with the claimant,
(4) specialization and (5) other factors that tend  to support or contradict a medical opinion’s
weight.30  A “medical source’s ‘treatment relationship’ with a claimant” is a factor under this new
