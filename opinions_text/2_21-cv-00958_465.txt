II. Standard of Law
When, as here, a party moves for leave to amend  or supplement after the
deadline set forth in a scheduling order, the motion  is governed by the good cause
requirements of Rule 16(b). Rule 16(b) provides that a scheduling order “may be
modified only for good cause and with the judge’s consent.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 16(b)(4).
“The good cause standard requires the ‘party seeking relief to show that the deadlines
cannot reasonably be met despite the diligence of the party needing the extension.’”
S&W Enterprises, L.L.C. v. SouthTrust Bank of Alabama, NA, 315 F.3d 533, 535 (5th
Cir. 2003) (citation omitted).
The Fifth Circuit has established a four -part test for the exercise of the trial
court’s discretion in determining whether a movant has established good cause. See

Lampkin v. UBS Fin. Servs., Inc., 925 F.3d 727, 741 (5th Cir. 2019). The trial court
considers: “(1) the explanation for the failure to timely move for leave to amend, (2)
the importance of the amendment, (3) the potential prejudice the other party would
suffer if the amendment was allowed, and (4) the availability of a continuance to cure
that prejudice.” Id. (citing S&W Enters., 315 F.3d at 536). Even if a party establishes
good cause under Rule 16(b), it must still satisfy the additional requirements of Rule
15(a) or Rule 15(d).
Rule 15(a) applies to amended pleadings—i.e. , when the facts underlying the
new or modified claim occurred before the original pleading was filed. 6A Charles A.
Wright & Arthur R. Miller, Fed. Prac. & Proc. § 1504 (3d ed. 2024). Under Rule 15(a),
a party may amend its pleading once as a matter of course within 21 days or service
or, “if the pleading is one to which a responsive pleading is required, 21 days after
service of a responsive pleading or 21 days after service of a motion under Rule 12(b),
(e), or (f), whichever is earlier.” If that deadline has passed, then the party may amend
its pleading only with the opposing party’s written consent or the court’s leave. Fed.
R. Civ. P. 15(a)(2). “The court should freely give leave when justice so requires.” Id.
Rule 15(d) applies to supplemental pleadings—i.e., when the facts underlying
the new or modified claim occurred after the original pleading was filed . 6A Wright
et al., supra § 1504. Under Rule 15(d), “the court may, on just terms, permit a party
to serve a supplemental  pleading setting out any transaction,  occurrence, or event
that happened after the date of the pleading to be supplemented.” See Fed. R. Civ. P.
15(d). “[T]he discretion exercised in deciding whether to grant leave” is similar with

III. Analysis
A. Pelnor has clarified the narrow temporal scope of its
proposed bad faith claim.

On the face of the proposed pleading, the  time period underlying Pelnor’ s
proposed bad faith claim is  unclear. Read broadly, the claim could encompass any
action by First State in connection with the indemnity dispute. The briefing, however,
indicates that Pelnor is basing the claim on  more recent events. A recent  s tatus
conference clarified the scope of the proposed claim.15
During the status conference, Pelnor, through counsel, confirmed that it is not
attempting to bring a bad faith claim based on its entire history of interactions with
First State. Rather, Pelnor explained, the proposed bad faith claim dates back to the
September 4, 2024 settlement, at the earliest, and focuses  on First State’s response
(or lack thereof) to the District Court’s January 20, 2025 grant of summary judgment.
Thus, because all relevant events occurred after the original crossclaim was filed, the
motion for leave refers only to a supplemental crossclaim. The two potential start
dates for Pelnor’s proposed supplemental  crossclaim will be addressed separately
because they lead to opposite results under Rule 16(b).

B. Pelnor should not be permitted to supplement its crossclaim to
address events dating back to September 4, 2024.

 The first potential start date for the proposed  bad faith  crossclaim is the
September 4, 2024 settlement between Sentilles and Pelnor . The District Court
granted summary judgment on January 20, 2025.  Pelnor suggests that its bad faith
claim may arise from events that occurred in the interim period. That suggestion
lacks merit because this interim period was the direct result of the parties’ multiple
joint requests that the Court delay ruling on summary judgment.
The parties filed their cross motions for summary judgment on September 18,
2024.16 The original submission date for both motions was October 3, 2024. 17 The
parties jointly requested that the District Court delay ruling on the summary
judgment motions while they engaged in settlement discussions. 18 Ultimately, after
granting four separate joint  motions for a continuance of the submission date, the
District Court took the motions under advisement on January 9, 2025.19 The District
Court ruled on the motions for summary judgment on January 20, 2025.20 Thus, the
period between Pelnor’s settlement with Sentilles and the summary judgment is
nearly coextensive with the parties’ requests that the District Court delay ruling.

Having asked the District Court to delay clarifying First State’s obligations via
summary judgment, Pelnor cannot now capitalize on that delay to support a bad faith
claim. Virtually every Rule 16(b) factor confirms this commonsense proposition. See
Lampkin, 925 F.3d 727, 741 (reciting factors). First, the reason for Pelnor’s delay was
a decision to focus on settlement rather than pursue a summary judgment ruling from
the District Court. Thus, the delay was not only voluntary—it was strategic.21 Second,
the proposed bad faith claim is unimportant because Pelnor cannot plausibly allege
that First State was in bad faith for delaying payment when the parties jointly asked
the District Court to postpone clarifying First State’s contractual liability. Third, any
bad faith claim dating back to September 2024 would cause signi ficant unfair
prejudice to First State, including because the District Court could have ruled much
earlier on the pending motions had the parties not jointly requested a delay. And
fourth, a continuance cannot cure that prejudice.  Pelnor’s actions (in which First
State joined) created the delay period at issue—and that delay cannot be undone or
mitigated with a continuance. Thus, Pelnor’s supplemental claim relative to
September 4, 2024, through January 20, 2025, fails under Rule 16(b).

to grant or deny leave to supplement is within the sound discretion of the district
court.”  See Gentilello v. Rege, 627 F.3d 540, 546 (5th Cir. 2010) . By December 2024,
both parties agreed that, if the matter did not settle, they would “await adjudication

address events beginning on January 20, 2025.

Virtually every Rule 16(b)  factor, however, supports allowing Pelnor to file a

was impossible for Pelnor to raise this  claim by the March 2024 deadlin e. And
Pelnor’s delay in acting after summary judgment was de minimis. Pelnor apparently
waited 30 days after summary judgment for First  State to pay.  When First State
failed to do so, Pelnor promptly brought its motion for leave within a matter of days.
Pelnor’s delay is not unreasonable under the circumstances. Second, the proposed
amendment is important because it allows Pelnor to rai se a statutory claim, with
significant additional remedies, that was not available earlier. Third, while First
State will be prejudiced in  confronting new allegations and new requests for relief,
that is true with virtually every supplemental pleading. First State cannot establish
any unfair prejudice in connection with the supplemental crossclaim  here. And
fourth, any prejudice can be cured by a continuance should the District Court believe
