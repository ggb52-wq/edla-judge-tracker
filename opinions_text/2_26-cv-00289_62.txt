If the nonmovant will bear the burden of proof at trial, then the movant “can
meet its summary judgment obligation by pointing the court to the absence of
admissible evidence to support the nonmovant’s claim.” Clark v. Dep’t of Pub. Safety
& Corr., 141 F.4th 653, 659 (5th Cir. 2025); Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(c)(1)(B). “When the
movant also carries the burden of proof at trial, as when he asserts an affirmative
defense, his burden is even higher; he must ‘establish beyond peradventure all of the
essential elements of the claim or defense.’” Guzman v. Allstate Assurance Co., 18
F.4th 157, 160 (5th Cir. 2021) (emphasis in original) (quoting Fontenot v. Upjohn Co.,
780 F.2d 1190, 1194 (5th Cir. 1986)).
If the moving party meets its burden, “the nonmovant must go beyond the
pleadings and designate specific facts showing that there is a genuine issue for trial.”
Little v. Liquid Air Corp., 37 F.3d 1069, 1075 (5th Cir. 1994) (en banc) (per curiam).
This requires more than “conclusory allegations, unsubstantiated assertions, or only
a scintilla of evidence” Freeman v. Tex. Dep’t of Crim. Just., 369 F.3d 854, 860 (5th
Cir. 2004). Instead, the evidence must be “such that a reasonable jury could return a
verdict for the nonmoving party.” Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 248
(1986). “If the nonmoving party fails to meet this burden, the motion for summary
judgment must be granted.” Little, 37 F.3d at 1076.

III. ANALYSIS
A. The McCorpen Defense
“[A] seaman injured in his employ enjoys a right to maintenance and cure —a
small daily stipend to pay for food, lodging, and basic medical care.” Boudreaux v.
Transocean Deepwater, Inc., 721 F.3d 723, 725 (5th Cir. 2013). However, maintenance
and cure are  not owed if  the “seaman procure[d] his employment by ‘ intentionally’
and ‘fraudulently’ concealing a material medical condition causally related to the
injury later sustained .” Id. at 726  (quoting McCorpen, 396 F.2d at  548–49). If an
employer “ require[d] the seaman to submit to medical examination as part of its
hiring process ,” to successfully invoke the McCorpen defense, the employer must
prove that “(1) the claimant intentionally misrepresented or concealed medical facts;
(2) the non- disclosed facts were material to the employer ’s decision to hire the
claimant; and (3) a connection exists between the withheld information and the injury

complained of in the lawsuit.” Jauch v. Nautical Servs., Inc., 470 F.3d 207, 212 (5th
Cir. 2006) (per curiam); see McCorpen, 396 F.2d at 548–49.
The first prong of the McCorpen  defense “is essentially an objective inquiry.”
Brown v. Parker Drilling Offshore Corp. , 410 F.3d 166, 174  (5th Cir. 2005) . “The
employer need only show that the seaman ‘[f]ail[ed] to disclose medical information
in an interview or questionnaire that is obviously designed to elicit such
information.’” Meche v. Doucet , 777 F.3d 237, 247 (5th Cir. 2015)  (alterations in
original) (quoting Brown , 410 F.3d at  174); see also Jauch , 470 F.3d at 212 (“[A]
seaman who misrepresents or conceals any material medical facts, disclosure of
which is plainly desired, risks forfeiture of his maintenance and cure benefits.”). The
employer need not prove that the seaman had the “subjective intent to conceal ” his
medical condition. Meche, 777 F.3d at 247.

In Miles v. Apex Marine Corp. , the Supreme Court limited general maritime
actions for wrongful death to the recovery of pecuniary losses. 498 U.S. 19, 30 –33
(1990). The Court determined that this limitation was necessary to create “a uniform
rule applicable to all actions for the wrongful death of a seaman, whether under [the
Death on the High Seas Act (“DOHSA”)], the Jones Act, or general maritime law.” Id.
at 33. The Fifth Circuit has interpreted Miles to stand for the proposition that when
“the factual setting of a case . . . is covered by a statute like the Jones Act or DOHSA,
and the statute informs and limits the available damages, the statute directs and
delimits the recovery available under the ge neral maritime law as well.”
Scarborough, 391 F.3d at 667 (quoting Guevara v. Mar. Overseas Corp., 59 F.3d 1496,
1506 (5th Cir. 1995) (en banc)).
Scarborough concerned a general maritime action by the survivors of a Jones
Act seaman against a non -employer third party. Id.  at 666. The Jones Act  does not
allow a seaman to recover punitive damages from his employer. McBride v. Estis Well
Serv., L.L.C., 768 F.3d 382, 388 (5th Cir. 2014) (en banc) . However, b ecause the
defendant in Scarborough was not the seaman’s employer, the survivors’ claim was
governed by general maritime law rather than the Jones Act.  391 F.3d at 667. The
Scarborough court held that the Miles “uniformity principle” prevents a seaman from
recovering nonpecuniary damages against a  non-employer third part y pursuant to
general maritime law. Id. at 668.

disregard Scarborough, arguing that it has been overruled by an intervening

This analysis is guided by the doctrine of superseding cause, which applies in
admiralty law. Bommarito v. Belle Chasse Marine Transp., L.L.C., 159 F.4th 297, 306
(5th Cir. 2025) “The superseding cause doctrine applies where the defendant’s
negligence in fact substantially contributed to the plaintiff ’s injury, but the injury
was actually brought about by a later cause of independent origin that was not
foreseeable.” Id. (quoting Stolt Achievement, Ltd. v. Dredge B.E. LINDHOLM, 447
F.3d 360, 367–68 (5th Cir. 2006)); see also Donaghey v. Ocean Drilling & Expl. Co. ,
974 F.2d 646, 652 (5th Cir. 1992).
Even if defendants are at fault  for Jones’s accident,  his stroke constitutes a
superseding cause of some of his alleged damages. The stroke was of a different origin
than the accident and it “was not reasonably foreseeable” to defendants that he would
have the stroke. Bommarito, 159 F.4th at 308. Therefore, defendants are not liable
for Jones’s lost earnings caused by the  stroke, even if he would have lost those
earnings due to the accident alone .57 However, contrary to defendants’ argument,
they remain liable for lost earnings that are attributable to Jones’s injuries from the
accident, but not attributable to the stroke.
