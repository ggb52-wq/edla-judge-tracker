II. Standard of Review
A) Scope of Discovery
Rule 26 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedur e provides that unless otherwise limited by
the court, “[p]arties may obtain discovery regarding any nonprivileged matter that is relevant to

any party’s claim or defense and proportional to the needs of the case, ” considering factors such
as the parties’ access to the information and the importance of the discovery in resolving the issues.
FED. R. CIV. P. 26(b)(1).
However, the court must limit discovery if it determines that the discovery is duplicative .
FED. R. CIV. P. 26(b)(2)(C). Also, discovery may be limited if it can be obtained from some other
more convenient source, or if the proposed discovery is outside the scope of Rule 26(b)(1). Id.
B) Privileged Information
Rule 26 protects against the discovery of privileged information. See FED. R. CIV. P.
26(b)(1). A party who invokes a privilege in response to a discovery request has the burden of
proving the privilege’s applicability. Meador v. Starr Indem. & Liab. Co., No. 19-2378, 2020 WL
8256370, at *3 (E.D. La. Sept. 1, 2020) (citing Hodges, Grant & Kaufmann v. United States, 768
F.2d 719, 721 (5th Cir. 1985)).
i) Work-Product Doctrine
The work-product doctrine provides that “a party may not discover documents and tangible
things that are prepared in anticipation of litigation or for trial by or for another party or its
representative (including the other party’s attorney, consultant . . . or agent).” FED. R. CIV. P.
26(b)(3)(A). For documents or things to be considered work product, litigation is not required to
be imminent “as long as the primary motivating purpose behind the creation of the document was
to aid in possible future litigation.” U.S. v. Davies , 636 F.2d 1028, 1040 (5th Cir. 1981), cert.
denied, 454 U.S. 862 (1981).
The factors considered to determine the primary purpose for creating a document are “the
retention of counsel and his involvement in the generation of the document .” Canter v. Cleco
Corp., No. 08-0343, 2009 WL 902435, at *3 (E.D. La. Mar. 30, 2009) (internal citations omitted).

Also, the court should consider “whether it was a routine practice to prepare that type of document
or whether the document was instead prepared in response to a particular circumstance.” Id.
Materials prepared in the ordinary course of business are not considered to be prepared in
anticipation of litigation. Therefore, such materials are excluded from the work-product doctrine.
Meador, 2020 WL 8256370, at *4 (citing United States v. El Paso Co., 682 F.2d 530, 542 (5th Cir.
1982); additional citation omitted).
The involvement of an attorney is not dispositive , but it “ is a highly relevant factor . . .
making materials more likely to have been prepared in anticipation of litigation.” Fieldwood
Energy, L.L.C. v. Diamond Servs. Corp., No. 14-650, 2015 WL 1415501, at *2 (E.D. La. Mar. 27,
2015) (quoting Wikel v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc., 197 F.R.D. 493, 495 (N.D. Okla. 2000)).
ii) Attorney-Client Privilege
“The attorney –client privilege is the oldest of the privileges for confidential
communications known to the common law.” Upjohn Co. v. United States , 449 U.S. 383, 389
(1981) (citation omitted) . It shields from disclosure confidential communication between an
attorney and client. See id. The purpose of the attorney –client privilege “is to encourage full and
frank communication between attorneys and their clients and thereby promote broader public
interests in the observance of law and administration of justice.” Id. In federal question cases such
as this one, federal common law determines the scope of the attorney –client privilege. See In re
EEOC, 207 Fed.Appx. 426, 431 (5th Cir. 2006).
Where, as here, the existence of a federal question provides the basis for federal subject
matter jurisdiction, federal common law governs the resolution of the privilege issue. See United
States v. Zolin, 491 U.S. 554, 562 (1989) (citing FED. R. EVID. 501). Under federal common law,
the elements of the attorney-client privilege are: (1) a confidential communication; (2) made to a

6

lawyer or his subordinate; (3) for the primary purpose of securing either a legal opinion, legal
services, or assistance in a legal proceeding. See United States v. Robinson, 121 F.3d 971, 974 (5th
Cir.1997), cert. denied, 522 U.S. 1065 (1998). The burden is on the party asserting the privilege
to demonstrate how each document satisfies all the elements of the privilege. See Navigant
Consulting, Inc. v. Wilkinson , 220 F.R.D. 467, 473 (N.D. Tex. Mar. 29, 2004) ( citing Hodges,
Grant & Kaufmann v. United States, 768 F.2d 719, 721 (5th Cir.1985)).
A general allegation of privilege is insufficient to meet this burden. Id. Instead, “a clear
showing must be made which sets forth the items or categories objected to and the reason for that
objection.” Id. (quoting Caruso v. Coleman Co., No. 93–CV–6733, 1995 WL 384602, at *1 (E.D.
Pa. Jun.22, 1995). The proponent must provide sufficient facts by way of detailed affidavits or
other evidence to enable the court to determine whether the privilege exists. Id.
Although a privilege log and an in-camera review of documents may assist the court in
conducting its analysis, a party asserting the privilege still must provide “a detailed description of
the materials in dispute and state specific and precise reasons for their claim of protection from
disclosure.” Id. at 473 –74 (quoting Pippenger v. Gruppe , 883 F.Supp. 1201, 1212 (S.D.  Ind.
1994)). In fact, “resort to in camera review is appropriate only after the burdened party has
submitted detailed affidavits and other evidence to the extent possible.” Id. at 474 (quoting Caruso,
1995 WL 384602, at *1 (emphasis in original)).

contends that the statements were improperly withheld by the Defendants’ questionable privilege
log. Id. Plaintiff contends that his requests for production  contemplated production of the

statements. Id. Plaintiff contends that when this issue was raise d, Defendants acknowledged that
the statements were produced but then later sought to claw them back. Id.
Defendants contend that the statements were both inadvertently identified to Plaintiff’s
counsel and released to Defendants’ own expert witness, Mr. Marc Fazioli. R. Doc. 91. Defendants
contend that disclosing the statements to their expert did not waive privilege because Mr. Fazioli
did not “consider” the three statements in his analysis.  Id. Defendants also point out that before
the Plaintiff filed the subject motion, the Defendants produced a Second Amended Privilege Log
allegedly curing the problem of asserting the attorney-client privilege rather than the work-product
doctrine. Id.
The work-product doctrine is very different from the attorney -client privilege regarding
possible waiver. Although the attorney -client privilege exists to protect the confidential
communications between an attorney and client and, thus, is generally waived by disclosure of
confidential communications to third parties, the work -product doctrine exists to “promote the
adversary system by safeguarding the fruits of an attorney's trial preparations from the discovery
attempts of an opponent.” Shields v. Sturm, Ruger & Co ., 864 F.2d 379, 382 (5th Cir.  1989)
(citation omitted). “Therefore, the mere voluntary disclosure to a third person is insufficient in
itself to waive the work product privilege.” Id. (citation omitted). That is, “because the work
product privilege looks to the vitality of the adversary system rather than simply seeking to
preserve confidentiality, it is not automatically waived by the disclosure to a third party.”  In re
Grand Jury Subpoena, 220 F.3d 406, 409 (5th Cir.2000).
However, such a “disclosure does waive protection if it has substantially increased the
opportunities for potential adversaries to obtain the information.” Ecuadorian Plaintiffs v. Chevron
Corp., 619 F.3d 373, 378 (5th Cir. 2010) (internal quotation marks and citation omitted). And, for

example, “the work product privilege is waived when the attorney requests the witness to disclose
the information or when the attorney discloses the information to the court voluntarily or makes
no objection when it is offered.” Shields, 864 F.2d at 382.
“Unlike the attorney -client privilege, the burden of proving waiver of work product
immunity falls on the party asserting waiver.” S.E.C. v. Brady, 238 F.R.D.  429, 444 (N.D. Tex.
Oct. 16, 2006); accord Ecuadorian Plaintiffs, 619 F.3d at 379 & n.10 (party asserting work-product
protection is not required to prove non-waiver). “If the party is successful, waiver of work product
immunity will be limited to the materials actually disclosed.” Brady, 238 F.R.D. at 444.
The evidence shows that the statements of Haleigh Dardar and Joseph Dardar were not
made in anticipation of litigation but in the ordinary course of business. Counsel acknowledged
that she produced the statement of Haleigh Dardar and that it was not obvious to her that the records
were privileged when she took over the file in June 2026.
Neither Haleigh Dardar nor Joseph Dardar prepared the ir statements at the request of
Counsel but instead recorded their statements to memorialize what they heard  and/or observed
regarding the events underlying Plaintiff’s claims. Specifically regarding the statement of Haleigh
Dardar, Counsel acknowledged that she submitted an affidavit which was prepared by her but
signed by Ms. Dardar. Further, Defense Counsel admitted that she claimed only the attorney-client
privilege on the first privilege log, and that the work-product doctrine was not raised until a later
iteration of the privilege log was produced.
Also, Counsel acknowledged that the documents were voluntarily provided to their expert
and only when he was later contacted did he suggest that he did not “consider” the statements in
his expert report. Given that the statements were not protected by the attorney client privilege, the
Court considered whether they would be protected by the work product doctrine.

Counsel also argues that the work product doctrine applies even before the lawyer is
retained, as in this case where the lawyer was not retained until one week after the three witness
statements were created. H ere, Plaintiff’s demand letter was sent on October 8, 2026, and
Defendant CP Marine secured counsel only one week later. However, the Court notes that it is a
common practice of the Defendant to conduct an accident investigation , including taking
statements after the accident regarding Plaintiff’s return to work and his termination. The Court
notes that on July 26, 2026, Defendants gave the statements to their expert, Mr. Faziol i, which
constituted a waiver. Mr. Fazioli’s report was later issued identifying the statements on August 22,
2026.  The Court therefore also denies the request to claw back the Dardar statements.
Regarding the statement of Jason Schouest, like the other statements, Schouest’s statement
was made prior to the retention of counsel regarding his observations whether heard or personally
felt. The Court finds that Jason Schouest’s statement is subject to production because it is not
protected by either the attorney-client privilege nor the work-product doctrine.
