II. Standard of Review
 Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6) provides that an action may be dismissed “for
failure to state a claim upon which relief can be granted.” “To survive a motion to dismiss, a
complaint must contain sufficient factual matter, accepted as true, to ‘state a claim for relief that
is plausible on its face.’” Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009) (quoting Bell Atl. Corp. v.
Twombly, 550 U.S. 544, 570 (2008)).
 “Factual allegations must be enough to raise a right to relief above the speculative
level.” Twombly, 550 U.S. at 555. A claim is plausible on its face when the plaintiff has pled facts
that allow the court to “draw a reasonable inference that the defendant is liable for the misconduct
alleged.” Iqbal, 556 U.S. at 678 (citing Twombly, 550 U.S. at 556). Although a court must liberally
construe the complaint in the light most favorable to the plaintiff, accept the plaintiff’s allegations
as true, and draw all reasonable inferences in favor of the plaintiff, courts do not accept as true
conclusory allegations, unwarranted factual inferences, or legal conclusions. Warner v. Hutson,
No. 23-2981, 2023 WL 6880434, at *3 (E.D. La. Oct. 18, 2023) (internal citations omitted).

 The United States Supreme Court has e xplained the facial plausibility standard in
detail:
A claim has facial plausibility when the plaintiff pleads factual content that allows the court
to draw the reasonable inference that the defendant is liable for the misconduct alleged.
The plausibility standard is not akin to a “probability requirement,” but it asks for more
than a sheer possibility that a defendant has acted unlawfully. Where a complaint pleads
facts that are merely consistent with a defendant's liability, it stops short of the line between
possibility and plausibility of entitlement to relief.

Iqbal, 556 U.S. at 678 (citations and quotation marks omitted).
 In determining whether a complaint states a claim that is plausible on its face, the Court
“draw[s] on its judicial experience and common sense.”  Id. at 679. Thus, as mentioned above, to
avoid dismissal, “a complaint must contain sufficient factual matter, accepted as true, to ‘state a
claim to relief that is plausible on its face.”’ Id. at 677-78 (quoting Twombly, 550 U.S. at 570). For
a claim to be plausible at the pleading stage, the complaint need not strike the reviewing court as
probably meritorious, but it must raise “more than a sheer possibility” that the defendant has
violated the law as alleged. See id.
 On a Rule 12(b)(6) review, although generally the court may not look beyond the
pleadings, the c ourt may examine the complaint, documents attached to the complaint, and
documents attached to the motion to dismiss to which the complaint refers and which are central
to the plaintiff's claim(s), as well as matters of public record. Lone Star Fund V (U.S.), L.P. v.
Barclays Bank PLC, 594 F.3d 383, 387 (5th Cir. 2010); Cinel v. Connick , 15 F.3d 1338, 1341,
1343 n.6 (5th Cir.  1994); see also United States ex rel. Willard v. Humana Health Plan of Tex.,
Inc., 336 F.3d 375, 379 (5th Cir. 2003) (“the court may consider ... matters of which judicial notice
may be taken”). Taking judicial notice of public records directly relevant to the issue in dispute is

A. Qualified Individual with a Disability
Marathon contends that Rainey’s own allegations establish that he was not a qualified
individual with a disability as defined by the ADA. R. Doc. 38-1. Marathon contends that Rainey’s
allegations and the documents attached to his First Amended Complaint affirmatively demonstrate
that he was categorically disqualified under federal regulations governing commercial motor
vehicle drivers. Marathon contends that Rainey’s inability to satisfy DOT medical certification
requirements renders him unqualified as a matter of law, irrespective of any accommodations.  Id.
Therefore, according to Marathon, Rainey’s First Amended Complaint should be dismissed with
prejudice.  Id.
Rainey addresses Marathon’s argument that he admits in his own amended complaint that
Marathon’s in-house physician assistant failed him medically because Rainey’s kidney disease
was “likely to interfere with [his] ability to drive a commercial motor vehicle safely.”  R. Doc. 46.
Rainey argues that he made no such allegation in his amended complaint.  Id.
The FMCSA provides safety oversight to commercial motor carriers, and under FMCSA
regulations, a motor carrier may not allow a driver to operate a commercial motor veh icle if the
driver may suffer from an impairment that makes it unsafe for her to operate the vehicle. 49 C.F.R.
§ 392.3. To comply with that requirement, the employer must generally adhere to the FMCSA
Medical Guidelines regarding drivers' qualifications. See 49 C.F.R. § 391.11; 49 C.F.R. § 391.41.
FMCSA review is merely a process for resolving disputes between medical examiners about a
driver's physical qualifications. 49 C.F.R. § 391.47.
A disabled individual is qualified for the job in question if, inter alia, he can perform the
essential functions of the job with or without reasonable accommodation. Gonzales v. City of New
Braunfels, 176 F.3d 834, 837 (5th Cir.  1999). “Essential functions ” are those duties that are
Case 2:25-cv-02047-KWR     Document 51     Filed 10/20/26     Page 8 of 15

9

fundamental to the job at issue”; the term does not include the marginal functions of the position.
Kapche v. City of San Antonio, 176 F.3d 840, 843 (5th Cir. 1999); 29 C.F.R. § 1630.2(n)(1).  The
EEOC implementing regulations provide that “[a] job function may be considered essential” if, for
example, “the reason the position exists is to perform that function.” 29 C.F.R. § 1630.2(n)(2)(i).
Under 49 C.F.R. § 391.11, “[a] person shall not drive a commercial motor vehicle unless
he/she is qualified to drive a commercial motor vehicle.” As a result, “a motor carrier shall not
require or permit a person to drive a commercial motor vehicle unless that person is qualified to
drive a commercial motor vehicle.” Id.  To be deemed “physically qualified”  is an additional
requirement in accordance with the FMCSR’s “Physical Qualifications and Examinations”
provision. 49 C.F.R. § 391.11(b)(4); see also 49 C.F.R. §§ 391.41—391.49.
To drive commercially, an applicant must either pass the physical examination or obtain a
formal medical variance from the FMCSA under 49 C.F.R. § 391.41(a)(3)(ii).  Likewise, an
individual is not physically qualified if they suffer from a condition that is “likely to interfere with
his/her ability to drive a commercial motor vehicle safely.” 49 C.F.R. § 391.41(b)(9).
Plaintiff concedes that at the time he sought employment with Marathon, he suffered from
a chronic and life -threatening kidney disease which required self -administered daily dialysis. R.
Doc. 22 at ¶¶ 18–21.  He further admits that a DOT-certified medical examiner evaluated him and
found him medically unqualified as a result. Id.  at ¶ 33.  The DOT -certified medical examiner’s
report attached to the First Amended Complaint confirms that Plaintiff failed to pass the physical
examination due to this functional disease, specifically, “ESRD/Peritoneal Disease.”  Id.  at 44.
Because Rainey failed the physical examination, he was not qualified to drive a commercial motor
vehicle.

 The evidence record shows that on July 27, 2022, Rainey underwent an examination by a
DOT-certified medical examiner Dr. Joseph Patrick who noted his renal nephropathy, that it was
being treated, and that it was not a problem for driving. R. Doc. 22 at 37. The examiner noted that
Rainey required peritoneal dialysis and he had a catheter in place but concluded that it should not
make for unsafe driving. Id. This certificate was valid through July 27, 2024.  However, se ven
months later, Marathon’s DOT -certified medical examiner, Jas on Gharamani, examined Rainey
on March 22, 2023, and determined that because he required peritoneal dialysis that he was not
qualified.
Although a driver may have a medical examiner's certificate, DOT regulations establish
that a motor carrier is not required to treat the certificate as determinative of whether the driver is
physically qualified to drive. 49 CFR § 391.47. One way in which they do that is to create a
mechanism by which to resolve disagreements between a carrier's physician and a driver's
physician about the driver's qualification to drive. Id. It follows then that a carrier may reject a
driver's physician's determination that the driver is physically qualified to drive if the carrier's
physician reaches a contrary conclusion.
Marathon points out that as the employer with whom the individual seeks employment, it
had the discretion to rely on its own DOT-certified examiner’s opinion, not the opinion of a prior
unrelated examiner.  R. Doc. 48 at 4 n.2. Marathon contends that prior medical opinions are
immaterial to its determination during its DOT-regulated medical examination, relying on Harris
v. P.A.M. Transp., Inc., 339 F. 3d 635, 639 (8th Cir. 2003) (which held that initial certification was
of no consequence where second medical examiner determined driver not qualified under
regulation). See also Campbell v. Fed. Express Corp., 918 F.Supp. 912, 918 (D. Md. 1996) (“In a
case where there are conflicting medical evaluations, such as the conflict [the driver] faced

between [two separate companies'] examination outcomes, the driver may submit an application
for resolution of the conflict to the [FMCSA].” (emphasis supplied)); Hill v. Houff Transfer, Inc.,
2012 WL 5194080, at *3 (E. D. Va. Oct. 19, 2012) (driver's physician “disagree[d]” with third-

Based on the allegations in the amended complaint and its attachments, the Court finds that
Rainey has failed to state a claim for which relief may be granted.  While Rainey contends that he
already received a medical certification from a different doctor which was still in effect through
July 2024, Marathon was not obligated by the regulations to accept that certification.  It had the
right and discretion to rely upon its own doctor’s conclusions. Rainey has failed to present any
case that contradicts this conclusion and the Court was not able to locate any.  Therefore, Rainey
has failed to establish that he was a qualified individual and therefore he is unable to state a claim
for discrimination in violation of the ADA.

that he alleged in the alternative that his employment offer was revoked because Marathon believed
that Rainey would occasionally need modified truck- driving schedules to facilitate his self -
administration of daily peritoneal dialysis.  Rainey alleges further in the amended complaint that
he may have required an accommodation.
The ADA prohibits covered entities from “discriminat[ing] against a qualified individual
on the basis of disability.” 42 U.S.C. § 12112(a). Discrimination includes “not making reasonable
accommodations to the known physical or mental limitations of an otherwise qualified individual
with a disability  . . . unless such covered entity can demonstrate that the accommodation would
impose an undue hardship on the operation of the business of such covered entity.”  42 U.S.C. §
12112(b)(5)(A).
“[A] plaintiff must prove the following statutory elements to prevail in a failure -to-
accommodate claim: (1) the plaintiff is a ‘qualified individual with a disability;’ (2) the disability
and its consequential limitations were ‘known’ by the covered employer; and (3) the employer
failed to make ‘reasonable accommodations’ for such known limitations.” Feist v. La., Dep't of
Just., Office of the Att'y Gen., 730 F.3d 450, 452 (5th Cir. 2013).  However, a plaintiff must plead
that the defendant had “actual knowledge that an accommodation is necessary.” J.W. v. Paley, 81
F.4th 440, 450 (5th Cir. 2023).
Rainey does not allege that dialyzing might interfere with his duties but instead states that
if it did that there were reasonable accommodations available that would have allowed him to
perform his job. R. Doc. 22 at 25. The amended complaint further fails to allege how Rainey’s
autoimmune kidney disease or dialysis requirement substantially limited one or more major life
activity and therefore constituted a disability under applicable law.

C.  Regarded As Disabled Claim
Finally, Marathon contends that while Rainey seeks to assert an alternative claim under the
ADA that Marathon “regarded him as greater disabled than  he really was, ” his claim fails as a
matter of law.  R. D oc. 38-1 at 13 (cleaned up) .  Marathon contends that such a claim can only
arise when a person not actually disabled under the ADA is “nonetheless regarded as having such
an impairment,” citing Hale v. King, 642 F.3d 492, 502 (5th Cir. 2011).
Rainey contends that his regarded as claim states a claim and should be allowed to proceed.
R. Doc. 46 at 20. Rainey contends that Marathon’s decision- makers regarded him as more or
differently disabled than he really was. Id. Rainey contends that Marathon’s argument that Rainey
did not “ask” for such an accommodation is irrelevant given that Marathon refused to tell him why
it revoked its offer in the first place. Id. at 21 -22. Therefore, Rainey contends that because
Marathon refused to engage in the interactive process after it was put on notice of his “disability”
and daily self-dialysis, it should not be allowed to avoid its responsibility to do so. Id. at 22.
The ADA makes it unlawful for an employer to “discriminate against a qualified individual
on the basis of disability in regard to job application procedures, . . . discharge of employees, . . .
Case 2:25-cv-02047-KWR     Document 51     Filed 10/20/26     Page 13 of 15

14

and other terms, conditions, and privileges of employment.” 42 U.S.C. § 12112(a).  Under the
ADA, a disability is:
(A) a physical or mental impairment that substantially limits one or more of the major life
activities of such individual;
(B) a record of such an impairment; or
(C) being regarded as having such an impairment.
42 U.S.C. § 12102(2).
According to ADA regulations, “substantially limit s” means “unable to perform a major
life activity that the average person in the general population can perform; or significantly
restricted as to the condition, manner or duration under which an individual can perform a
particular major life activity as compared to the condition, manner, or duration under which the
average person in the general population can perform that same major life activity.”  Kemp v.
Holder, 610 F.3d 231, 237 (5th Cir. 2010) (citation omitted)  (cleaned up); see also 29 C.F.R.
1630.2(j)(ii). In contrast, “regarded as” disability claims are grounded in the foundational
distinction that the plaintiff’s impairment is not substantially limiting. See Rodriguez v. ConAgra
Grocery Prods. Co. 436 F.3d 468, 479 (5th Cir. 2006).
 In order to assert the alternative “regarded as ” claim, Rainey must have been a qualified
individual who otherwise was denied employment because the employer viewed him as disabled.
In this case, Rainey conceded in his amended complaint that he was disabled due to his peritoneal
dialysis and was restricted by the condition from engaging in a major life activity.
 Further, Marathon’s doctor did not certify Rainey as medically able to work.  Because Rainey
was not medically able to work, the federal safety rules prohibited him from working as a
commercial truck driver.  Because the federal safety rules prohibited Rainey from working in the
Case 2:25-cv-02047-KWR     Document 51     Filed 10/20/26     Page 14 of 15

15

absence of a medical certificate, Marathon could not have violated the ADA as a matter of law.
The Court therefore finds that Rainey’s “regarded as” claim fails as a matter of law and is
dismissed.
