II. Law and Analysis
A. Entitlement to Attorney’s Fees
Louisiana law follows the “American Rule,” under which courts cannot award
attorneys’ fees unless authorized by contract or statute.   See, e.g., Maloney v. Oak Builders,
Inc., 235 So. 2d 386, 390 ( La. 1970).  In interpreting a contractual fee- shifting provision,
“[t]he reasonable intention of the parties to a contract is to be sought by examining the words
of the contract itself.”   Prejean v. Guillory, 38 So. 3d 274, 279 (La. 2010).  And “[w]hen the
words of a contract are clear and explicit and lead to no absurd consequences, no further
interpretation may be made in search of the parties ’ intent.”  Id.   A contract “must be
interpreted in a common-sense fashion, according to the words of the contract their common
and usual significance.”  Lambert v. Maryland Cas. Co., 418 So. 2d 553, 559 (La. 1982).

With those principles in mind, and because neither party asserts that a fee- shifting
statute applies, the language of the Agreement’s fee shifting provision will govern if clear.
Article 15(e) of the Agreement states:
Any Defaulting Party hereunder shall indemnify and hold harmless the Non-
Defaulting Party for any loss, claims or liabilities which the Non- Defaulting
Party may incur arising out of any breach of this Joint Venture Agreement by
the Defaulting Party. The Defaulting Party further agrees to pay all legal
expenses required of or by the Non-Defaulting Party to protect their interests
or defend any action arising out of the Defaulting Party’s breach, including
court costs and disbursements and fees on a solicitor and his own client basis.

Article(c) of the Agreement, in relevant part, states:

If any party hereto (the “Defaulting Party”) shall default in any of its
obligations under this Agreement including . . . then the other party (the “Non-
Defaulting Party”) may give written notice to the Defaulting Party specifying
the event of default.

In the event that the Defaulting Party does not cure its default within seven (7)
days of receipt of such notice then the Non-Defaulting Party may terminate the
Defaulting Party’s interest in the Joint Venture.

(Rec. doc. 254-3 at 16).  TMG argues that it is entitled to attorney’s fees pursuant to
Article15(e) because AWC breached the Agreement by improperly demanding capital
contributions.  According to TMG, it provided written notice to AWC that it was in default for
improperly demanding capital contributions, and AWC did not cure the default within 7 days.
That, TMG argues, makes AWC a defaulting party under Article(c), satisfying Article 15(e)’s
requirements for entitlement to attorney’s fees.  (Rec. doc. 254-2 at 4-5).
AWC argues that TMG is not entitled to attorney’s fees because entitlement to
attorney’s fees under Article 15(e) is expressly conditioned on a breach of the Agreement,
and the court did not find AWC in breach of the Agreement.  AWC further argues that TMG
never raised the issue of AWC’s breach, that it was never litigated, and now  any such claim
would be barred as a compulsory counterclaim.  AWC also argues that TMG’s pre- litigation

opinions that AWC was in default do not constitute a finding of default or breach as required
by Article 15(e).  (Rec. doc. 256).
In its Reply, TMG argues again that AWC is a defaulting party, because it properly
placed AWC in default when it gave AWC written notice of its alleged breach.  TMG further
argues that Article 15(e) does not require that a claim of breach be litigated or determined
by the court.  In essence, TMG argues that because it placed AWC in default, and AWC failed
to cure that default, Article 15(e) entitles it to attorney’s fees without need to litigate AWC’s
breach or to have raised it as a counterclaim.  (Rec. doc. 261).
As explained above, the language of Article 15(e) and intent of the parties, as
evidenced by that language, controls here.  Regardless of whether AWC is a defaulting party,
to trigger Article 15(e), the defaulting party must be in breach.  The fact that the Article 15(e)
uses both the terms default and breach is evidence that the parties did not intend for the
terms to be synonymous.  Myriad Dev., Inc. v. Alltech, Inc., 817 F. Supp. 2d 946, 965 (W.D. Tex.
2011) (finding that the meaning of the terms breach and default, and whether they may be
used interchangeably, depends on the particular contract).  Thus, TMG allegedly placing AWC
in default does not necessarily equate to AWC being in breach.
Moreover, to interpret the Agreement as not requiring a judicial determination of
breach would be contrary to the plain language of Article 15(e).  That would essentially allow
breach to be presumed by placing a party in default.  To interpret the Agreement that way
would mean either party could unilaterally declare the contract breached. That would not be
a reasonable interpretation of the language of Article 15(e).  Article 15(e) only applies when
the non-defaulting party protects their interests or defends any action “ arising out of ” the
defaulting party’s  breach, suggesting  that the fee shift is triggered only after a judicial

determination of breach .  Nothing about that language suggests that breach should be
presumed without any litigation of that issue.
The Court declines to delve into the substance of whether AWC is a defaulting party
in breach of the Agreement.  A post- judgment motion for attorney’s fees is not the proper
vehicle to bring that issue before the court.  Because AWC has not been judicially determined
to be a defaulting party in breach of the Agreement, TMG is not entitled to attorney’s fees
under Article 15(e) of the Agreement.
III. Conclusion
For the foregoing reasons,
IT IS RECOMMENDED  that Defendant, The McDonnel Group, L.L.C.’s,  Motion for
Attorneys’ Fees (rec. doc. 254) be DENIED.
