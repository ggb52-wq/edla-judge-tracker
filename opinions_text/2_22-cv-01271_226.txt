III. LAW AND ANALYSIS
A. Legal Standard
A scheduling order “may be modified only for good cause and with the judge’s consent.”54
The Amended Scheduling Order likewise permits deadlines to be extended only “upon timely
motion filed in compliance with Local Rules and upon a showing of good cause.”55 The “good
cause standard requires the party seeking relief to show that the deadlines cannot reasonably be
met despite the diligence of the party needing the extension.”56 The burden rests on the party
seeking the modification.57 The Court considers four factors: “(1) the explanation of the failure to
timely comply with the scheduling order; (2) the importance of the modification; (3) potential
prejudice in allowing the modification; and (4) the availability of a continuance to cure such
prejudice.”58 The same standard governs a request to file a dispositive motion after the deadline

As the Court explained in denying the School Board’s motion, a scheduling order “is not a
mere formality,” and district courts “must have the power to control their dockets by holding
litigants to a schedule.” 61 At the same time, summary judgment is an integral part of the Federal
Rules, and Rule 16 contemplates that the Court may act before trial to “eliminat[e] frivolous claims
or defenses.”62 A dispositive-motion deadline may therefore yield in a rare case, such as when the
movant obtains information it could not have obtained before the deadline despite its diligence,
when the motion presents a discrete legal question that can be decided without disrupting the trial
date or prejudicing the opposing party, or when a party that timely sought summary judgment asks
the Court to address a limited matter closely related to its timely motion.63 The deadline does not
yield, however, merely because a party, long after the deadline has passed, develops evidence it
believes is helpful and that it could have pursued earlier.64

B. Timeliness Under Rule 16(b)(4)
Lopinto, unlike the School Board, met the dispositive -motion deadline by timely filing a
motion for summary judgment. In Enlow v. Tishomingo County , the Fifth Circuit recognized that
“[s]uccessive motions for summary judgment . . . are not always aberrational” and that whether to
entertain one lies within the district court’s discretion.67 In Enlow, however, the plaintiffs had filed
a fourth amended complaint asserting a new theory of recovery against the movant, considerable
discovery had followed the first motion, and no party objected that the second motion was
untimely.68 Here, Plaintiffs object to the motion as untimely. The claims against Lopinto have not
changed since he answered the operative complaint in 2022, and, apart from the single deposition
the Court allowed for trial, discovery closed in August 2023. Lopinto did not ignore the deadline.69
The question is whether he has shown good cause to raise, weeks before trial, issues his timely
motion did not raise.

2. The Importance of the Relief Sought
Lopinto’s motion is important to him. If he is correct that Plaintiffs cannot prove their
remaining claims against him, resolving those claims before trial would spare him, and the Court,
the burden of trying them. The Court recognizes that interest, but the importance of the relief
sought “cannot singularly override the enforcement of local rules and scheduling orders.” 92 The
importance of this motion is also limited by the circumstances of this case. Plaintiffs’ claims
against the School Board, which arise from the same incident and will involve many of the same
witnesses, will be tried regardless of how Lopinto’s motion is resolved. Lopinto also retains a
remedy at trial. If Plaintiffs’ evidence fails to support their claims against him, he may move for
judgment as a matter of law once Plaintiffs have been fully heard.93 The importance factor weighs
in Lopinto’s favor, but it does not outweigh the others.

4. Availability of a Continuance
A continuance could give the parties additional time, but it would not cure the prejudice
described above, and it would come at substantial cost. A continuance “does not cure the prejudice
of additional expense and work” a party would incur in responding to what amounts to a second
“bite at the apple.”99 Nor would it “deter future dilatory behavior, nor serve to enforce local rules
or court imposed scheduling orders.”100 The events giving rise to this lawsuit occurred more than
five years ago, and trial has already been reset three times.101 No party has asked for a continuance,
and a continuance would undercut the principal justification Lopinto offers for his motion, which
is to avoid the expense and delay of trial. This factor weighs against modifying the scheduling
order.

C. Reconsideration Under Rule 54(b)
Lopinto’s request for reconsideration requires separate treatment, because Rule 54(b)
permits the Court to revise an interlocutory order “at any time” before final judgment.108
The Court’s June 17, 2026 ruling decided only the one element of Plaintiffs’ claim for
negligent infliction of emotional distress that Lopinto chose to challenge, and the Court expressly
made “no determination as to the remaining elements.” 109 To the extent Lopinto now challenges
those remaining elements, his motion does not ask the Court to reconsider anything it decided.
Rather, it is a new motion for summary judgment on elements he did not previously challenge, and
it is untimely for the above-stated reasons. Rule 54(b) does not provide a means to avoid the good-
cause requirement for arguments a party chose not to raise before the deadline.
As to the element the Court did decide, the question is one of discretion. Lopinto relies on
J.H.’s deposition testimony, and the parties sharply dispute what that testimony shows.110 Disputed

IV. CONCLUSION
This ruling is a procedural one. It does not address whether J.H.’s testimony undermines
any of Plaintiffs’ claims against Lopinto, and it does not prevent Lopinto from presenting that
testimony at trial, using it on cross-examination, or moving for judgment as a matter of law once
Plaintiffs have been fully heard.113 For the foregoing reasons,
IT IS ORDERED that Lopinto’s Motion for Summary Judgment and for Reconsideration
