II. GOVERNING LAW
“State law controls both the award of and the reasonableness of fees
awarded where state law supplie s the rule of decision.”  Mathis v. Exxon
Corp., 302 F.3d 448, 461 (5th Cir. 2002).  In this case, a Louisiana contract
supplied the rule of decision, so Louisiana law governs the Court’s
consideration of ANKOR’s motion for attorneys’ fees.

B. Amount of Attorneys’ Fees
Under Louisiana law, “attorney fees must be reasonable.  The amount
is regulated by the Rules of Professional Conduct.”  Childers v. Laurel Lakes
Ests. Homeowners Ass’n, Inc. , 380 So. 3d 46, 54 (La. App. 1 Cir. 2023)
(citation modified).  Rule 1.5(a) of  the Louisiana Rules of Professional
Conduct provides the following factors to determine the reasonableness of
attorneys’ fees:

Party giving such notice shall at its option [ ] take the necessary
action to collect the amount due plus interest, damages, and costs.

The Court need not expressly consider all the factors an d may multiply the
number of hours worked by an hourly rate the Court deems reasonable.  See
Fourchon Docks, Inc. v. Milchem Inc., 849 F.2d 1561, 1568 (5th Cir. 1988).
Here, the Court finds ANKOR’s requested amount of attorneys’ fees to
be reasonable.  ANKOR has calculat ed this amount by multiplying the
number of hours worked by its attorney s on this case by their respective
hourly rates.  These rates are in line  with New Orleans market rates for
similarly experienced attorneys.  The parties litigated this case for two years.

The case involved complex legal analys is and required plaintiff to defend
against a motion to dismiss and a moti on for summary judgment and to file
three motions for partial summary judgment (one on liability for
decommissioning expenses, one on qu antum of decommissioning expenses,
and one on lease operating expenses) .  In addition, ANKOR’s attorneys
secured a significant monetary award fo r their clients (nearly $6.5 million),
and the attorneys’ fees ANKOR seeks to recover ($451,918.52) are not out of
proportion to that award.  These fees amount to only approximately 7% of the
award ANKOR’s counsel secured.  See, e.g., Hobbs v. EVO Inc. , 7 F.4th 241
(5th Cir. 2021) (upholding a district court’s award of $240,588 in attorneys’
fees, which amounted to 134% of the $179,374 recovered in damages)
(applying federal law); Williamson, 597 So. 2d at 443 (finding $175,000 in
attorneys’ fees—17% of the total aw ard—reasonable where counsel secured
$1,021,171 for their clients).
Neither of the arguments Merit raises  against this amount has merit.
First, Merit argues that it would be  duplicative for the Court to award
attorneys’ fees both to the operator  (ANKOR) and to non-operators.  But
ANKOR does not seek an award of attorneys’ fees to non-operators.  ANKOR
itself has brought this case, and ANKOR itself has incurred attorneys’ fees.
These are the fees it seeks to collect.

Second, Merit argues that the requ ested fees are excessive because
many of the hours billed by ANKOR’s attorneys involved review and
production of documents in ANKOR’s own possession for the purpose of
proving the amount Merit owed ANKO R.  But Merit provides neither
rationale nor legal authority to expla in why attorney time should be
discounted when spent either (1) re viewing a party’s own documents or
(2) attempting to prove the extent of  injury—an essential element of a
plaintiff’s claim.  Merit al so does not identify any time entries it believes
should be stricken, nor does Merit sugge st an award of attorneys’ fees it
believes would be reasonable.  The Court  therefore has no reason to reduce
the amount requested by ANKOR.
C. Costs
Merit does not contest the amount of costs requested by ANKOR, and
ANKOR has provided evidence to substantiate its claim to this amount.  The
Court therefore grants ANKOR’s motion for $11,026.49 in costs.
