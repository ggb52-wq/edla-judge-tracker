III. LAW AND ANALYSIS
A. Legal Standard
 Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure allows a party to move for dismissal
of a complaint for failure to state a claim upon which relief can be granted. To survive a motion to
dismiss under Rule 12(b)(6), a plaintiff ’s complaint “must contain ‘enough facts to state a claim
to relief that is plausible on its face. ’” Varela v. Gonzales , 773 F.3d 704, 707 (5th Cir. 2014)
(quoting Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly , 550 U.S. 544, 570 (2007)). In other words, a plaintiff ’s
“[f]actual allegations must be enough to raise a right to relief above the speculative level. ”
Twombly, 550 U.S. at 555 (discussing Fed. R. Civ. P. 8(a)). “A claim has facial plausibility when
the plaintiff pleads factual content that allows the court to draw the reasonable inference that the
defendant is liable for the misconduct alleged.” Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009) (citing
Twombly, 556 U.S. at 556).
When deciding whether a plaintiff has met its burden, a court “accept[s] all well-pleaded
factual allegations as true and interpret[s] the complaint in the light most favorable to the plaintiff,
but ‘[t]hreadbare recitals of the elements of a cause of action, supported by mere conclusory
statements’ cannot establish facial plausibility. ” Snow Ingredients, Inc. v. SnoWizard, Inc., 833
F.3d 512, 520 (5th Cir. 2016) (quoting Iqbal , 556 U.S. at 678) (some internal citations and
quotation marks omitted). Plaintiffs must “ nudge[] their claims across the line from conceivable
to plausible.” Twombly, 550 U.S. at 570. A complaint does not meet the plausibility standard “if it
offers only labels and conclusions, or a formulaic recitation of the elements of a cause of action.”

Whitley v. Hanna, 726 F.3d 631, 638 (5th Cir. 2013) (internal quotation marks omitted) (citing
Twombly, 556 U.S. at 555).
Although motions to dismiss are evaluated by the content in the complaint, the United
States Supreme Court has described the extent of possible evidence: “ [C]ourts must consider the
complaint in its entirety, as well as other sources courts ordinarily examine when ruling on Rule
12(b)(6) motions to dismiss, in particular, documents incorporated into the complaint by reference
and matters of which a court may take judicial notice.” Tellabs, Inc. v. Makor Issues & Rights, Ltd,
551 U.S. 308, 322 (2007) (citation omitted). Further, “ [d]ocuments that a defendant attaches to a
motion to dismiss are considered part of the pleadings if they are referred to in the plaintiff ’s
complaint and are central to [the plaintiff’s] claims.” Collins v. Morgan Stanley Dean Witter, 224
F.3d 496, 498–99 (5th Cir. 2000) (quotation omitted); see also Lormand v. US Unwired, Inc., 565
F.3d 228, 251 (5th Cir. 2009) (citing Tellabs, Inc. v. Makor Issues & Rts., Ltd., 551 U.S. 308, 322
(2007)).
B. Analysis
i. FMLA Interference & Retaliation
 To prove FMLA retaliation, the employee must demonstrate: “ 1) he was protected under
the FMLA; 2) he suffered an adverse employment action; and 3) he was treated less favorably than
an employee who had not requested leave under the FMLA or the adverse decision was made
because he sought protection under the FMLA.” Acker v. Gen. Motors, L.L.C., 853 F.3d 784, 790
(5th Cir. 2017) (quoting Mauder v. Metro. Transit Auth. of Harris Cnty., 446 F.3d 574, 583 (5th
Cir. 2006)). The third element requires the employee to show “there is a causal link” between the
FMLA-protected activity and the adverse action. Richardson v. Monitronics Int ’l, Inc., 434 F.3d
327, 332 (5th Cir. 2005).

 Unlike Hammler’s claim for retaliation, her interference claim does not require a showing
of discriminatory intent. To establish a prima facie interference case, Hammler must show that (1)
she was an eligible employee, (2) her employer was subject to the FMLA ’s requirements, (3) she
was entitled to leave, (4) she gave proper notice of her intention to take FMLA leave, (5) her
employer interfered with, restrained, or denied her the benefits to which she was entitled under the
FMLA, and (6) she was prejudiced. See Acker, 853 F.3d at 788.
 As to interference, Hammler alleges that MTM hired her on August 24, 2024, and that she
requested FMLA leave for a personal medical condition on August 20, 2025. (Rec. Doc. 1 at 2.)
Although her request came four days before her one-year anniversary, eligibility is determined as
of the date leave begins, and any FMLA-qualifying leave taken after the employee reaches twelve
months of employment is FMLA leave. 29 C.F.R. § 825.110(d). Hammler therefore became
eligible on August 24, 2025, while still on leave. Further, MTM approved her FMLA leave (Rec.
Doc. 1 at 2), which confirms both that she gave proper notice and that MTM treated her as entitled
to leave. On August 29, 2025, while Hammler was on approved FMLA leave, MTM terminated
her. (Id. at 3.) Termination during protected leave denies the employee her right to be restored to
her position upon return, 29 U.S.C. § 2614(a)(1), and she was prejudiced by the loss of her
employment. Hammler’s termination while on protected leave shows a plausible claim sufficient
to satisfy Twombly that her entitlements to FMLA were interfered with.
 As to retaliation, Hammler was protected under the FMLA as she requested and was
granted FMLA leave (Rec. Doc. 1 at 2), and she was terminated while on FMLA leave which
satisfies the requirement for adverse employment action. She was terminated nine days after
requesting leave. (Id. at 3.) When evaluating whether the adverse employment action was causally
related to the FMLA protection, the court shall consider the “temporal proximity” between the

7

FMLA leave, and the termination. Mauder v. Metro. Transit Auth. of Harris Cnty., Tex., 446 F.3d
574, 583 (5th Cir. 2006).
 Although MTM points to an internal investigation into Hammler’s bereavement records to
justify the termination, the Court need not heighten the pleading standard and scrutinize whether
the termination was because of discrimination, the Court need only find that the facts alleged
“nudge[ ] their claims across the line from conceivable to plausible.” Twombly, 550 U.S. at 547.
Taking Hammler ’s allegations as true, the timing and proximity show a plausible claim.
Accordingly, Defendant’s motion to dismiss Plaintiff’s FMLA Retaliation Claim and FMLA
Interference claim is DENIED.
ii. Defamation
 Hammler fails to state a claim for defamation because she has not alleged that the
challenged statements were published to a third party.
 To state a claim for defamation, a plaintiff must allege (1) a false and defamatory statement
concerning another; (2) an unprivileged publication to a third party; (3) fault on the part of the
publisher amounting to at least negligence; and (4) resulting injury. See Costello v. Hardy, 864 So.
2d 129, 139–40 (La. 2004). Hammler alleges that MTM falsely accused her of falsifying funeral
records and that the accusation was published when her sister overheard the termination call and
when multiple MTM supervisors discussed it. (Rec. Doc. 1 at 5; Rec. Doc. 10 at 5.) MTM argues
that the statements are privileged under La. Stat. Ann. § 23:291(A) (2014) and that they were not
published. Hammler responds that § 23:291(A) applies only to disclosures made to prospective
employers after the employment relationship ends. (Rec. Doc. 10 at 6.) Because Hammler has not
adequately alleged publication, the Court does not reach the scope of statutory immunity.

8

 Under Louisiana law, “statements between employees, made within the course and scope
of their employment, are not statements communicated or publicized to third persons.”
Commercial Union Ins. Co. v. Melikyan, 424 So. 2d 1114, 1115 (La. Ct. App. 1982). The Melikyan
court derived this rule from Cangelosi v. Schwegmann Bros. Giant Super Markets, 390 So. 2d 196,
[198] (La. 1980), in which the Louisiana Supreme Court held that statements made to an employee
about an altered check, by and in the presence of supervisory personnel essential to the
investigation, did not constitute publication. Melikyan, 424 So. 2d at 1115. In another case, the
court, relying on Cangelosi and Melikyan, granted summary judgment for the employer where the
plaintiff alleged that the employer’s separation records falsely attributed his termination to failure
to follow company guidelines and low sales, because the record contained no evidence that the
information was circulated outside the company. Marshall v. Circle K Corp. 715 F. Supp. 1341,
1343 (M.D. La. 1989), aff’d, 896 F.2d 550 (5th Cir. 1990).
 The Fifth Circuit has likewise stated that “[i]n Louisiana, statements between employees,
made within the course and scope of their employment, are not statements communicated or
publicized to third persons for the purposes of a defamation claim.” Williams v. United Parcel
Serv., Inc. , 757 F. App’ x 342, 345 (5th Cir. 2018) (citing Melikyan , 424 So. 2d at 1115). In
Williams, the plaintiff failed to report workplace injuries despite being told by a supervisor to do
so. The plaintiff alleged that the supervisor lied about having told him to report his injuries, and
those statements were the basis of the Defamation claim. Id. The district court dismissed the
claims, and the Fifth Circuit upheld the District Court’s ruling stating that the alleged defamatory
statements were made exclusively within UPS's organizational structure during a company
investigation and that under Louisiana Law this does not constitute publication. Id.

 Hammler’s allegations are consistent with the circumstances addressed in these cases. The
challenged statements were communicated to Hammler during her termination and discussed
among MTM supervisors acting within the scope of their duties. (Rec. Doc. 1 at 5.)
To the extent that Hammler alleges publication, and then only an instance where her sister
overheard the phone call when she was terminated, a third party simply overhearing a phone call
is not the equivalent to directing statements at the third party. Cook v. Parish of Jefferson, 2022
U.S. Dist. LEXIS 76, *31 (E.D. La. Jan. 3, 2022) (“[t]o be considered ‘published,’ for purposes of
a defamation claim, statements must be made to a third party, other than the person allegedly
defamed.”). In Cook the court held that a plaintiff had not met their burden of showing publication
when they merely alleged the statements were made at a hearing that was public in nature. Id. The
court stated that the plaintiff failed to properly plead facts showing the hearings were public in
nature. Id. Because Hammler has not plead facts indicating that MTM directed statements to a
third party or  any person outside the company she has failed to plead an essential element of her
defamation claim. See Williams, 757 F. App’x at 345.  Accordingly, Defendant’s motion to dismiss
Plaintiff’s Defamation claim is GRANTED.
iii. Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress
 Hammler has only alleged a single incident of harassment by her employer MTM. (Rec.
Doc. 1 at 3.) This single instance was their accusation, after conducting an investigation, that she
forged her father’s death -related documents. (Id.) The result of this determination was her
termination. (Id.) This Court has held that for a claim of intentional infliction of emotional distress
to succeed against an employer, there must be a pattern of harassment or abuse. For example, in
Martin v. American Midstream Partners, LP, the extreme, albeit singular, instance of a noose being
left on an African American employee ’s desk was not sufficient to show a pattern of harassment

10

or abuse. 386 F. Supp. 3d 733, 742 (E.D. La. 2019); see also Harper v. Boise Paper Holdings,
L.L.C., 575 F. App’x 261, 265 (5th Cir. 2014) (holding that plaintiff failed to state a claim for IIED
when he alleged only a single incident, rather than a pattern of repeated harassment, and he did not
allege that his employer intended to inflict the emotional distress he suffered); Kell v. Iberville
Bank, 352 F. Supp. 3d 650, 663 (E.D. La. 2018) (dismissing plaintiff ’s claim for IIED because
“the facts alleged do not permit an inference of a pattern of abuse—which Louisiana courts require
a plaintiff to prove in a workplace intentional infliction of emotional distress claim”).
 Here, Hammler points to one instance of harassment or abuse , her being terminated after
an internal investigation revealed she allegedly fabricated funeral documents for bereavement
leave. Under Louisiana law this is insufficient to state a claim for intentional infliction of emotional
distress. See Kell, 352 F. Supp. 3d at 663.
 Additionally, a plaintiff who wishes to succeed on an intentional infliction of emotional
distress claim against an employer must point to facts that show she suffered severe emotional
distress and  offer more than a conclusory statement that she suffered  such distress . Under
Louisiana law, serious emotional distress includes conditions such as neuroses, psychoses, chronic
depression, phobia, and shock. Delouise v. Iberville Par. Sch. Bd., 8 F. Supp. 3d 789, 810 (M.D.
La. 2014) (citing to Held v. Aubert, 02–1486 (La. App. 1 Cir. 5/9/03), 845 So.2d 625, 634)).
 Here, Hammler has not pointed to evidence to show that her emotional distress falls within
the jurisprudential definition. Delouise v. Iberville Par. Sch. Bd., 8 F. Supp. 3d 789, 810 (M.D. La.
2014). She only alleges that she did. (Rec. Docs. 1 at 5; 10 at 8.) Accordingly, Defendant’s motion
to dismiss Plaintiff’s claim for intentional infliction of emotional distress is GRANTED.

v. Fair Labor Standards Act
 The Fair Labor Standards Act sets a minimum wage and mandates that employers pay their
hourly employees one and a half times their regular rate of pay for all hours worked in excess of
40 hours per week. 29 U.S.C. §§ 206(a), 207(a)(1).  Section 216(b) provides hourly employees
wrongly denied overtime wages a cause of action against an employer to recover those unpaid
overtime wages, liquidated damages, and attorney’s fees from their employers. 29 U.S.C. § 216(b).
 To state a claim to recover unpaid overtime wages, the Fifth Circuit has reiterated, “ a
plaintiff must plausibly allege: (1) that an employer-employee relationship existed during the time
that she worked in excess of forty hours per week; (2) that she engaged in activities covered by the
FLSA; (3) that the employer violated the FLSA’s overtime-wage requirements; and (4) the amount
of overtime-pay due.” White v. U.S. Corr., L.L.C., 996 F.3d 302, 307 (5th Cir. 2021).
 Hammler’s allegations do not meet these requirements. She alleges only that she worked
as a “Remote Customer Service Representative,” (Rec. Doc. 1 at 2), but pleads no facts showing
that her duties involved interstate commerce or that MTM is an enterprise engaged in commerce.
See 29 U.S.C. §§ 203(s)(1), 207(a)(1). Hammler does not plead specific dates, wages, or an
estimate of hours owed. A plaintiff need not plead precise amounts, attach a pre -discovery
timesheet, or plead payroll-level calculations to survive Rule 12(b)(6). See Molina-Aranda v. Black

12

Magic Enters., L.L.C., 983 F.3d 779, 788 (5th Cir. 2020) (finding allegations sufficient at the
pleading stage to put the defendants on notice of the minimum and overtime wage claims where
plaintiffs alleged that for “several pay periods during late August and September of 2015,” they
“worked 50 to 80 or more hours a week” but “were not paid fully or paid at all”). However, a
plaintiff must do more than just recite the elements and announce coverage. Id. The Fifth Circuit
has found, for example, allegations identifying specific weeks, with a month range and a statement
of how many hours per week were worked, minimally sufficient. Id.
 Accordingly, Defendant’s motion to dismiss Plaintiff’s claim for Unpaid Wages under the
FLSA is GRANTED.

vii. Hostile Work Environment
Hammler asserts disability discrimination claims under the Louisiana Employment
Discrimination Law (“LEDL”). The LEDL’s disability provisions are modeled after the Americans
with Disabilities Act ( “ADA”), and Louisiana courts analyze LEDL disability claims under the
same substantive framework as ADA claims, relying on federal ADA jurisprudence for
interpretive guidance. Credeur v. Louisiana Through Off. of Att ’y Gen., 860 F.3d 785 fn. 3 (5th
Cir. 2017). Accordingly, the Court analyzes Hammler ’s LEDL disability claims under the same
standards governing her ADA claims.
At the outset Hammler’s LEDL/ADA claims fail because Hammler has not pleaded
exhaustion of her administrative remedies. (Rec. Doc. 10 at 10.) A plaintiff asserting an ADA
claim must exhaust administrative remedies by filing a charge with the EEOC and obtaining a
right-to-sue notice. Melgar v. T.B. Butler Publ’g Co., 931 F.3d 375, 378– 79 (5th Cir. 2019) ( per
curiam) (citing Price v. Southwestern Bell Telephone Co., 687 F.2d 74, 77 (5th Cir. 1982).
Hammler’s Complaint does not allege that she filed any EEOC charge let alone one for hostile
work environment under the ADA. (Rec. Doc. 1.)  She also failed to plead that she received a
Case 2:26-cv-00474-ILRL-JVM     Document 15     Filed 10/16/26     Page 13 of 18
14

right‑to‑sue letter, or that she filed suit within the statutory time -delay window. (Id.) Her ADA
hostile‑work‑environment claim therefore fails at the threshold.
Additionally, Hammler’s hostile work environment claims under LEDL/ADA fail because
the Court cannot plausibly conclude based on the factual allegations that Hammler had a qualifying
disability under LEDL/ADA guidelines. To establish a prima facie  case of disability -based
harassment, a Plaintiff must demonstrate: (1) that she belongs to a protected group; (2) that she
was subjected to unwelcome harassment; (3) that the harassment complained of was based on her
disability or disabilities; (4) that the harassment complained of affected a term, condition, or
privilege of employment; and (5) that the employer knew or should have known of the harassment
and failed to take prompt, remedial action. Credeur, 860 F.3d at 795–96. Under the LEDL, a person
qualifies as having a disability if they: (1) have a physical or mental impairment that substantially
limits one or more major life activities; (2) have a record of such an impairment; or (3) are regarded
as having such an impairment. La. Stat. Ann. §§ 23:322 (b) (9) (2025).
First, Hammler’s  complaint does not sufficiently state a physical or mental impairment that
substantially limits one or more major life activities. The LEDL defines “ impairment” as “an
intellectual disability, any physical or physiological disorder or condition, or prior mental disorder
or condition.” Id. Louisiana courts have consistently held that an impairment qualifies as a
disability only if it substantially limits a major life activity (i.e. caring for one ’s self, performing
manual tasks, walking, seeing, hearing, speaking, breathing, learning, working, etc.), meaning the
limitation is considerable or significant rather than merely minor, slight, or marginal. See,
e.g., Beaumont v. Exxon Corp., 2002-2322 (La. App. 4 Cir. 3/10/04), 868 So. 2d 976; McClain v.
City of New Orleans , 2013-1291 (La. App. 4 Cir. 3/5/14), 137 So. 3d 671. The Fifth Circuit has
also long held that whether an individual is disabled depends not on the name or diagnosis of the
Case 2:26-cv-00474-ILRL-JVM     Document 15     Filed 10/16/26     Page 14 of 18
15

impairment, but rather the effects it has on an individual ’s life, requiring a plaintiff to plead facts
showing how the impairment substantially limits a major life activity. See, e.g., Deas v. River W.,
L.P., 152 F.3d 471, 478 (5th Cir. 1998); Hale v. King, 642 F.3d 492, 500 (5th Cir. 2011).
Although Hammler need not identify her specific diagnosed condition to survive a Rule
12(b)(6) motion, her claim that she suffers from “a serious health condition that substantially limits
one or more major life activities, qualifying her as a disabled individual ” (Rec. Doc. 1 at 8) is
insufficient. Because Hammler pleads only the conclusory assertion that her impairment
substantially limits a major life activity, without alleging facts showing the nature, severity,
duration, or effect of the limitation, the Court cannot evaluate whether the alleged impairment is
“substantially limiting” within the meaning of the statute. Accordingly, Hammler  fails to state a
plausible disability claim under the first prong. As the Fifth circuit explained, “[a]bsent allegations
that [Plaintiff’s] ailments substantially limited [her] in the performance of a major life activity,
[Plaintiff] has failed to state a claim for relief under subsection A of the ADA ’s definition of
disability.” Hale v. King, 642 F.3d at 501.
Second, Hammler’s  complaint does not include record of a physical or mental impairment
that substantially limits one or more major life activities. While Hammler may be able to point to
a record of impairment, she does not plead any facts for the court to consider its impact. Therefore,
“[Plaintiff’s] allegations are flawed under subsection B for the same reason that they are flawed
under subsection A —[Plaintiff] failed to allege facts from which we can reasonably infer that
[Plaintiff’s] medical conditions substantially limited a major life activity.” Id. at 502.
Third, based on the pleaded facts, Hammler cannot be considered as being “ r egarded as
having such an impairment.” Under the current standard, a plaintiff establishes a “ regarded as”
claim by showing that the employer perceived the plaintiff as having an impairment and took

However, this prong does not apply to impairments that are transitory (actually or expected to be

While the Fifth Circuit has not determined whether the transitory -and-minor exclusion is an

concluded that an impairment may be considered objectively transitory and minor where the actual

establishes that the impairment is temporary and minor in nature. Id. at 303.
Hammler states in her complaint that, “ [o]n or about August 20, 2025, Plaintiff put
Defendant MTM on notice of her disability by requesting protected medical leave and submitting
short-term disability paperwork to accommodate her condition.” (Rec. Doc. 1 at 9.) Based on the

has failed to allege that she was disabled under subsection C of the ADA’s definition of disability.
 While Hammler claims that the complaint meets the Swierkiewicz/Patrick fair notice

viii. Detrimental Reliance
 Plaintiff brings claims for “ Promissory Estoppel”, asserting that she detrimentally relied
upon the MTM’s bereavement leave policy and the granting of the leave.  Under Louisiana Law
the doctrine of Promissory Estoppel is referred to as Detrimental Reliance.
See generally Brown v. Phoenix Life Ins. Co., 843 F. App'x 533, 545 (5th Cir. 2021). A party may
be obligated by a promise when he knew or should have known that the promise would induce the
other party to rely on it to his detriment and the other party was reasonable in so relying. La. Civ.
Code Ann. art. 1967 (2025). Recovery may be limited to the expenses incurred or the damages
suffered as a result of the promisee’s reliance on the promise. Reliance on a gratuitous promise
made without required formalities is not reasonable. Id.
To state a claim for detrimental reliance, a plaintiff must establish three elements: “ (1) a
representation by conduct or word; (2) justifiable reliance on the representation; and (3) a change
in position to one’s detriment because of the reliance.” Cenac v. Orkin, L.L.C., 941 F.3d 182, 198
(5th Cir. 2019). Consistent with the text of Article 1967, a plaintiff must also plead that “ the
defendant knew or should have known that the promise would induce the plaintiff to rely on it to
[their] detriment.” Shaw v. Restoration Hardware, Inc., 93 F.4th 284, 290 (5th Cir. 2024) (citing
Wooley v. Lucksinger, 2006-1167 (La. App. 1 Cir. 5/4/07); 961 So. 2d 1228, 1238).
 Applying Louisiana law, the Fifth Circuit has held that an employer ’s staff handbook did
not create enforceable rights on which the employee could rely. Gilbert v. Tulane Univ., 909 F.2d
124, 126 (5th Cir. 1990). In Gilbert, the plaintiff argued that Tulane’s Staff Handbook modified
Case 2:26-cv-00474-ILRL-JVM     Document 15     Filed 10/16/26     Page 17 of 18
18
his at-will employment status and gave him a contractual right to invoke its grievance procedures.
Id. He contended that the handbook extended this right to all “ staff employees,” that he qualified
as a “staff employee” under the handbook’ s definition, and that he was therefore entitled to a
grievance proceeding. Id. He further argued that Louisiana courts had found staff handbooks to
create contractual rights in other cases. Id. The Fifth Circuit rejected these contentions. Id.
In reaching that conclusion, the Fifth Circuit relied on Wall v. Tulane University , 499 So.
2d 375 (La. Ct. App. 1986), writ denied, 500 So. 2d 427 (La. 1987), in which the Louisiana Fourth
Circuit Court of Appeal held that the same Tulane Staff Handbook was not a contract of
employment. Id. at 375 –76. The Wall  court determined that the handbook was “ primarily
informational in nature and did not . . . constitute a binding promise by Tulane to continue
indefinitely the benefits described therein.” Id. at 376. The court also noted that Tulane had made
no promises to Wall regarding those benefits that would have made them “ part of Wall ’s
employment agreement with Tulane.” Id.
Here, the bereavement policy, and the leave granted under it, were benefits afforded to
Hammler as an incident of her employment. Like the handbook in Gilbert and Wall, such a policy
does not “constitute a binding promise . . . to continue indefinitely the benefits described therein.”
Gilbert, 909 F.2d at 126 (quoting Wall, 499 So. 2d at 376). Hammler identifies no separate contract
or promise beyond the leave MTM granted under its generally applicable policy. She therefore
fails to state a claim for detrimental reliance upon which relief can be granted. Accordingly,
Defendant’s motion to dismiss Plaintiff’s claim for detrimental reliance is GRANTED.
