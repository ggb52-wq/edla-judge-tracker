LEGAL STANDARD
To survive a Rule 12(b)(6) motion to dismiss, the plaintiff must plead sufficient
facts to “state a claim to relief that is plausible on its face.” Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S.
662, 678 (2009) (quoting Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly, 550 U.S. 544, 570 (2007)). A claim
is facially plausible when the plaintiff pleads facts that allow the court to “draw the
reasonable inference that the defendant is liable for the misconduct alleged.” Id. The
factual allegations in the complaint “must be enough to raise a right to relief above
the speculative level.” Twombly, 550 U.S. at 555. “[D]etailed factual allegations” are
not required, but the pleading must present “more than an unadorned, the -
defendant-unlawfully-harmed-me accusation.” Iqbal, 556 U.S. at 678.  Pro se
plaintiffs are held to a more lenient standard when the Court analyzes a complaint,
but they “must still plead factual allegations that raise the right to relief above the
speculative level.” Chhim v. University of Texas at Austin, 836 F.3d 467, 469 (5th Cir.

2016) (citations omitted).  The Court must accept all well -pleaded facts as true and
must draw all reasonable inferences in favor of the plaintiff. Lormand v. US Unwired,
Inc., 565 F.3d 228, 232 (5th Cir. 2009). However, “conclusory allegations or legal
conclusions masquerading as factual conclusions will not suffice to prevent a motion
to dismiss.” Beavers v. Metro. Life Ins. Co., 566 F.3d 436, 439 (5th Cir. 2009) (citation
omitted).
DISCUSSION
A. FACTUAL ALLEGATIONS IN THE COMPLAINT AND THE OPPOSITION
 Ms. Bradley’s opposition (Rec. Doc. 12) contains details relating to her claims
that are not included in her complaint. At the motion to dismiss stage, “courts must
limit their inquiry to the facts stated in the complaint and the documents either
attached to or incorporated in the complaint.” Wilson v. Birnberg, 667 F.3d 591, 600
(5th Cir. 2012) (quoting Lovelace v. Software Spectrum Inc., 78 F.3d 1015, 1017 (5th
Cir. 1996)) (internal quotation marks omitted). Generally, if “matters outside the
pleadings are presented to and not excluded by the Court, the motion shall be treated
as one for summary judgment.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(d) ; In re Katrina Canal Breaches
Litigation, 495 F.3d 191, 205 (5th Cir. 2007). “When a party bases a motion to dismiss
on matters outside the pleadings, the court has discretion either to accept the
extraneous material and convert the motion to dismiss into a motion for summary
judgment, or to decide the motion, as defendant styled it, under the principles of Rule
12(b)(6).” McDonald v. Kansas City S. Ry. Co., No. 16-15975, 2017 WL 1709353, at *2
(E.D. La. May 3, 2017) (citations omitted). This discretion is exercised based on a
Case 2:25-cv-02452-CJB-EJD     Document 17     Filed 09/21/26     Page 6 of 15
7

determination of whether the material outside the 12(b)(6) motion is likely to
facilitate disposition of the action. Ware v. Associated Milk Prods., 614 F.2d 413, 415
(5th Cir. 1980). If the material is incomplete or inconclusive, conversion is generally
rejected. Id.
 Ms. Bradley’s opposition lacks the substance necessary to facilitate disposition
of this action. While further context is provided regarding her AIDA, MMWA, and
FOA claims, Bayou Automotive correctly asserts that the claims in Ms. Bradley’s
opposition are not sufficiently supported by legal authority . (Rec. Doc. 16, at 2 –3).
Moreover, Ms. Bradley’s opposition introduces state-law claims, without elaboration,
that are not included in her complaint. (Rec. Doc. 12, at 3). Ms. Bradley’s opposition
does not meet the threshold that would prompt the Court to convert a 12(b)(6) motion
into a motion for summary judgment. For these reasons , the Court will treat this as
a 12(b)(6) motion to dismiss and limit its inquiry to the facts stated in Ms. Bradley’s
complaint.
B. CLAIM UNDER THE MONRONEY ACT (AIDA)
Ms. Bradley brings a claim against Bayou Automotive  under the Monroney
Act. However, this claim lacks any conceivable basis in law.
Under the AIDA, manufacturers are “not only authorized, but required, to affix
a suggested retail price sticker to each new car which is to be sold in the retail
market.” In re Nissan Antitrust Litigation , 577 F.2d 910, 916– 17 (5th Cir. 1978)
(citing 15 U.S.C. § 1231 et seq.). Withholding, altering, or removing such a sticker
constitutes a misdemeanor. Id. at 917 (citing 15 U.S.C. § 1233). There is no express
Case 2:25-cv-02452-CJB-EJD     Document 17     Filed 09/21/26     Page 7 of 15
8

provision in the AIDA that creates a private right of action. 15 U.S.C. § 1233 outlines
penalties for violation of the Act, but establishes no civil remedies for private parties.
To date, no Supreme Court or Fifth Circuit decision has recognized a private right of
action under the AIDA.
Such a n otion has been ex plicitly rejected at the district court level . The
Eastern District of New York was unable to find any statutory basis for finding a
private right of action under 15 U.S.C. § 1232(g), “which outlines the duties,
authorities, and enforcement powers” of the AIDA. Boateng v. Bayerische Motoren
Werke Aktiengesellschaft, No. 17-cv-00209, 2022 WL 4357555, at *27 (E.D.N.Y. Sep.
20, 2022) (citing 15 U.S.C. §§ 1231–1233). “The Act regulates basic information
vehicles should provide consumers on the new vehicle label, but does not provide a
cause of action for individual drivers.” Id. (citing 15 U.S.C. § 1232(g)). Without
evidence of congressional intent, “a cause of action does not exist and courts may not
create one, no matter how desirable that might be as a policy matter, or how
compatible with the statute.” Alexander v. Sandoval , 532 U.S. 275, 286– 87 (2001)
(citing Mass. Mut. Life Ins. Co. v. Russell , 473 U.S. 134, 145, 148 (1985);
Transamerica Mortg. Advisors, Inc. v. Lewis, 444 U.S. 11, 23 (1979); Touche Ross &
Co. v. Redington, 442 U.S. 560, 575–76 (1979)).
As a clear and established matter of law, Bayou Automotive correctly asserts
that the AIDA affords no private right of action. There is no civil remedy available to
Ms. Bradley. Therefore, dismissal of Ms. Bradley’s claim under the AIDA  is
warranted.

C. CLAIM UNDER THE MAGNUSON-MOSS WARRANTY ACT (MMWA)
Next, Ms. Bradley  brings a claim against Bayou Automotive  under the
Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act. However, her complaint lacks sufficient information
to state a valid claim.
“In addition to establishing standards governing the content of warranties, the
MMWA creates a statutory cause of action for consumers damaged by the failure of a
supplier, warrantor, or service contractor to comply with any obligation imposed by
the Act or established by a written warranty, implied warranty, or service contract.”
Walton v. Rose Mobile Homes LLC , 298 F.3d 470, 474 (5th Cir. 2002) (internal
quotation marks omitted)  (quoting 15 U.S.C. § 2310(d)(1)). “[A] consumer who is
damaged by the failure of a supplier, warrantor, or service contractor to comply with
any obligation under [the Act], or under a written warranty, implied warranty, or
service contract, may bring suit for damages and other legal and eq uitable relief . . .
in an appropriate district court of the United States,” if the amount in controversy of
any individual claim is at least $25 and the total amount in controversy  is at least
$50,000. 15 U.S.C. § 2310.
The MMWA defines a supplier as “any person engaged in the business of
making a consumer product directly or indirectly available to consumers,” and a
warrantor as “any supplier or other person who gives or offers to give a written
warranty or who is or may  be obligated under an implied warranty.” 15 U.S.C.
§ 2301(4)–(5). The Act provides that “only the warrantor actually making a written
affirmation of fact, promise, or undertaking shall be deemed to have created a written
Case 2:25-cv-02452-CJB-EJD     Document 17     Filed 09/21/26     Page 9 of 15
10

warranty, and any rights arising thereunder may be enforced under this section only
against such warrantor and no other person.” 15 U.S.C. § 2310(f). Warrantors are
permitted to designate representatives to perform duties under a warranty, “but no
such designation shall relieve the warrantor of his direct responsibilities to the
consumer or make the representative a cowarrantor.” 15 U.S.C. § 2307. “A supplier
who does no more than distribute or sell a consumer product covered by a written
warranty offered by another person or business and which identifies that person or
business as the warrantor is not liable for failure of the written warranty to c omply
with the Act or rules thereunder,” unless the supplier is deemed by state law to have
adopted “the written affirmation of fact, promise, or undertaking.” 16 C.F.R. § 700.4.
Under Louisiana state law, a manufacturer is primarily defined as “a person
or entity who is in the business of manufacturing a product for placement into trade
or commerce.” La. R. S. § 2800.53. Additional factors that classify a person or entity
as a manufacturer  include: the person or entity labeling a product as their own ;
holding themselves out to be the manufacturer; selling the product and exercising
control over design, construction, or quali ty characteristics ; incorporating a
component into the product from another manufacturer; or selling a product from an
alien manufacturer by acting as its alter ego and importing or distributing the
product for resale. Id. In line with this definition, t he Supreme Court of Louisiana
has held that a seller who assumes total responsibility for tasks including nationwide
marketing, selling, servicing, establishing franchise dealerships, and operating a
domestic vehicle distribution center for an alien manufacturer shall be regarded, for

liability purposes, as occupying the role of manufacturer. Media Prod. Consultants,
Inc. v. Mercedes-Benz of North America, Inc., 51475 (La. 5/1/72), 262 So. 2d 377, 380.
The parties agree that Ms. Bradley’s vehicle was covered by a 3 -year, 36,000-
mile written warranty . This warranty was issued by Dodge, the vehicle’s
manufacturer. Nothing in Ms. Bradley’s complaint suggests that Bayou Automotive
has held itself out as the manufacturer of her vehicle, exercised significant control

See La. R. S. § 2800.53. Even if the Court were to classify Dodge as an alien
manufacturer, Bayou Automotive is a  local, Louisiana -based dealer . No state -law
precedent supports the idea that Bayou Automotive a cted as Dodge’s alter ego in its
operations. See id.
D. CLAIM UNDER THE FEDERAL ODOMETER ACT (FOA)
Lastly, Ms. Bradley  brings a claim against Defendant under the Federal
Odometer Act. However, this claim has not been pled with sufficient particularity.
The FOA aims to prohibit tampering with motor vehicle odometers and provide
safeguards to protect purchasers in the sale of motor vehicles with altered or reset
odometers. 49 U.S.C. § 32701(b). Under the FOA, “[a] person may bring a civil action
to enforce a claim . . . in an appropriate United States district court or in another
court of competent jurisdiction.” 49 U.S.C. § 32710(b). The plaintiff must bring the
action “no later than 2 years after the claim accrues.” Id. A person who violates the
FOA with intent to defraud is “liable for 3 times the actual damages or $10,000,
whichever is greater.” 49 U.S.C. § 32710(a).

12

“The necessary elements required for a private cause of action pursuant to the
Odometer Act are (1) that the defendant violated the Act or its regulations, (2) with
intent to defraud.” Gable v. Nikou Grp. Invs., Inc., No. H-16-2927, 2017 WL 3671082,
at *1 (S.D. Tex. Jan. 13, 2017) (internal quotation marks omitted) (quoting Owens v.
Samkle Auto, Inc., 425 F.3d 1318, 1321 (11th Cir. 2005)) . A seller who lacks actual
knowledge of an incorrect odometer reading may not “close his eyes to the truth.”
Nieto v. Pence, 578 F.2d 640, 642 (5th Cir. 1978). If the seller “reasonably should have
known that a vehicle’s odometer reading was incorrect, although he did not know to
a certainty the transferee would be defrauded, a court may infer that he understood
the risk of such an occurrence.” Id.
Circuit courts are split regarding  whether a plaintiff’s alleged fraud must
specifically concern the vehicle’s mileage . The Eleventh Circuit rejected this notion,
holding that “[n]o language limits the meaning of the clause ‘with intent to defraud.’”
Owens, 425 F.3d at 1321. Conversely, the Seventh Circuit and Ninth Circuit agree
that “[t]o survive a Rule 12(b)(6) motion to dismiss, a plaintiff suing under 49 U.S.C.
§ 32710 must allege that the defendant violated the Odometer Act with intent to
defraud as to mileage.” Bodine v. Graco, Inc., 533 F.3d 1145, 1151 (9th Cir. 2008); see
Ioffe v. Skokie Motor Sales, Inc. , 414 F.3d 708, 715 (7th Cir . 2005) (holding that the
FOA “creates a private right of action . . . only where a transferor chose not to disclose
a vehicle’s mileage to the transferee in writing on the title with intent to defraud as
to the vehicle’s mileage”).

Claims under the FOA are subject to  a heightened pleading standard under
Rule 9(b) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure. Rule 9(b) “applies by its plain
language to all averments of fraud, whether they are part of a claim of fraud or not.”
Lone Star Ladies Inv. Club v. Schlotzky’s Inc. , 238 F.3d 363, 368 (5th Cir. 2001)
(citation omitted). “In alleging fraud or mistake, a party must state with particularity
the circumstances constituting fraud or mistake. Malice, intent, knowledge, and other
conditions of a person’s mind may be alleged generally.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 9(b). At a
minimum, a plaintiff must set forth the “who, what, when, where, and how” of the
alleged fraud under Rule 9(b). United State s ex rel. Thompson v. Columbia/HCA
Healthcare Corp., 125 F.3d 899, 903 (5th Cir. 1997)  (citing Williams v. WMX Tech.,
Inc., 112 F.3d 175, 179 (5th Cir. 1997)) (internal quotation marks omitted).
It is possible that Ms. Bradley has a valid claim under the FOA. However,
c
onsidering the factual allegations in her complaint alone, Ms. Bradley fails to satisfy
the heightened pleading standard under Rule 9(b). Bayou Automotive correctly
indicates that Ms. Bradley’s complaint fails to reference an odometer or the vehicle’s
mileage at all. (Rec. Doc. 11 -1, at 4). Moreover, Ms. Bradley’s allegations do not
specify, or effectively allude to, Bayou Automotive’s intent to defraud. (Rec. Doc. 1, at
4–5). There is no clear “who, what, when, where, and how” set forth in Ms. Bradley’s
complaint. For these reasons, the Court cannot ascertain whether Ms. Bradley’s claim
is valid. While a private right of action is afforded by the FOA, more information and
a more particular pleading are needed to determine whether this right applies to Ms.
Bradley.

E. ESTABLISHING JURISDICTION UNDER 28 U.S.C. § 1331
 T here is no diversity of citizenship between Ms. Bradley and Bayou
Automotive, as both parties are Louisiana residents. See 28 U.S.C. § 1332(a).
Moreover, Ms. Bradley did not claim diversity in her complaint or allege an amount
in controversy exceeding $75,000. See id.  As it stands, Ms. Bradley’s complaint
contains no valid federal claim.  To keep her case in federal court, Ms. Bradley will
need to file an amended complaint stating a federal claim that is “plausible on its
face,” accompanied by sufficient facts. Ashcroft v. Iqbal , 556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009)
(quoting Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly , 550 U.S. 544, 570 (2007)).  Further, the Court
declines to exercise supplemental jurisdiction over the state-law claims introduced in
Ms. Bradley’s opposition. See 28 U.S.C. § 1367(c).
