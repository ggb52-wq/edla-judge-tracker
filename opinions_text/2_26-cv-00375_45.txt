II. LAW & ANALYSIS
A. Legal Standard
1.Converting Rule 12(b)(6) motion to dismiss into a motion for summary judgment.
In general, Rule 12(b)(6) motions are “viewed with disfavor and rarely granted.”  See
Hodge v. Engleman, 90 F.4th 840, 843 (5th Cir. 2024) (quoting Collins v. Morgan Stanley Dean
Witter, 224 F.3d 496, 498 (5th Cir. 2000)). Application of Rule 12(b)(6) requires the district court
to “accept[] all well-pleaded facts as true and view[] those facts in the light most favorable to the
Case 2:26-cv-00375-ILRL-KWR     Document 45     Filed 11/05/26     Page 3 of 19
4
plaintiffs.” See Lindsay v. United States, 4 F.4th 292, 294 (5th Cir. 2021) (citing Anderson v. Valdez,
845 F.3d 580, 589 (5th Cir. 2016) ). To survive a Rule 12(b)(6) motion, a plaintiff must be plead
sufficient facts to state a claim that is plausible on its face; mere “labels and conclusions” are
insufficient. Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009) (quoting Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly, 550
U.S. 544, 555, 570 (2007)).
However, “[i]f, on a motion under Rule 12(b)(6)  […], matters outside the pleadings are
presented to and not excluded by the court, the motion must be treated as one for summary
judgment under Rule 56.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(d). “Under Rule 12(d), a district court may convert a
motion to dismiss to a motion for summary judgment so long as it gives the parties a ‘ reasonable
opportunity to present all the material that is pertinent to the motion.’” Trinity Marine Prods., Inc.
v. United States, 812 F.3d 481, 487 (5th Cir. 2016) (quoting Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(d)). An implicit
conversion to summary judgment occurs once the district court looks outside of the pleadings,
regardless of whether the parties were explicitly informed of the conversion. See e.g. , Boateng v.
BP , P .L.C., 779 F. App’x 217, 220 (5th Cir. 2019) (quoting Trinity Marine Prods., Inc., 812 F.3d at
487).
Although a district court “need not advise either party of its intention” to convert, Holguin
v. U.S. Dep’t of Army , 98 F.3d 1337 (5th Cir. 1996), the non- movant must be given ten days to
submit evidence of its own. See Hodge, 90 F.4th at 845 (citing Holguin, 98 F.3d at 1337). “[The]
ten-day ‘ period begins running when the non- movant is first put on notice that, based on its
acceptance of evidence outside the pleadings, the court could convert the Rule 12(b)(6) motion
into a summary judgment.’” Id.
The United States Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals has recognized a district court looking
“outside of the pleadings ” when it considered new factual evidence and legal theories submitted

exhibits to their memorandum in opposition, said exhibits “are outside the pleadings” and require
the district court to convert the motion to dismiss into a  motion for summary judgment. See
McNealy v. Becnel, No. CV 14-2181, 2016 WL 6070073, at *7 (E.D. La. Oct. 17, 2016), opinion
clarified, No. CV 14- 2181, 2016 WL 6441237 (E.D. La. Nov. 1, 2016), and on

2. Motion for summary judgment standard.
 Once converted, the court is to analyze the motion under the summary judgment standard.
See e.g., McQuirter v. Lehmann, No. CV 17-1370, 2017 WL 6623993, at *2-3 (E.D. La. Dec. 28,
2017). Summary judgment is appropriate when the “pleadings, depositions, answers to
interrogatories, and admissions on file, together with any affidavits, if any, show that there is no
genuine issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter
of law.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(a) & (c); see Celotex Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317, 322 (1986). Material
in support or opposition of a motion for summary judgment may be considered if it is “capable of
being ‘presented in a form that would be admissible in evidence.’” LSR Consulting, LLC v. Wells
Fargo Bank, N.A., 835 F.3d 530, 534 (5th Cir. 2016) (emphasis in original) (quoting Fed. R. Civ.
P. 56(c)(2)). Courts view all facts and evidence in the light most favorable to the non-moving party,
but “refrain from making credibility determinations or weighing the evidence.” Delta & Pine Land

2. Applying the summary judgment standard to Mr. Mosley’ s § 1983 claims.
Mr. Mosley raises four 42 U.S.C. § 1983 claims in his amended complaint: (1) a procedural
due process claim for deprivation of protected propert y (Count I); (2) a substantive due process
claim for arbitrary and coercive state action shocking the conscious  (Count II); (3) a claim for
denial of meaningful access to courts and administrative proceedings  (fourteenth amendment)
(Count III); and (4) a Monell  claim for unconstitutional policy, custom, and failure to supervise
Count IV). (Rec. Doc. 40-1 at 6-7.) “To pursue a claim under § 1983, a ‘plaintiff[] must (1) allege
a violation of rights secured by the Constitution or laws of the United States and (2) demonstrate
that the alleged deprivation was committed by a person acting under color of state law.’” E.g., Sw.
Bell Tel., LP v. City of Houston , 529 F.3d 257, 260 (5th Cir. 2008) (quoting Resident Council of
Allen Parkway Vill. v. U.S. Dep’ t of Hous. & Urb. Dev., 980 F.2d 1043, 1050 (5th Cir. 1993) ).
Summary judgment is proper where the plaintiff fails to establish the defendants as “persons”
acting under the color of state law. See Guidry v. La. Dep’t of Pub. Safety, No. 24-30754, 2026 WL
687932, at *3 (5th Cir. Mar. 11, 2026).
a. Defendants , the Louisiana Public Defender Oversight Board and Mr. Starns are not
“persons” under § 1983.
B
eginning with whether the Board and Mr. Starns are “persons” under § 1983, the Supreme
Court has recognized that the State and  its officials, acting within their official capacity, are not

“persons” under § 1983. Will v. Mich. Dep’t of State Police, 491 U.S. 58, 71 109 S. Ct. 2304, 105
L. Ed. 2d 45 (1989); Med RX/Sys., P .L.L.C. v. Tex. Dep’t of State Health Servs., 633 F. App’x 607,
610 (5th Cir. 2016)  (citing Lapides v. Bd. of Regents, 535 U.S. 613, 617, 122 S.Ct. 1640, 152
L.Ed.2d 806 (2002)). “This rule extends to ‘arms of the state…’” Martin v. Dep’t of Child. & Fam.
Servs., 500 F. Supp. 3d 527, 543 (E.D. La. 2020) (quoting MedRx.Sys., P .L.L.C., 633 F.App’x at
610) (finding that the State of Louisiana Department of Children and Family Services , an arm of
the state, was not a “person” within the meaning of § 1983).
(1) The Louisiana Public Defender Oversight Board
Mr. Mosley has filed a Fourteenth Amendment denial of meaningful access to courts and
administrative proceedings claim (Count III) and a Monell  claim against the Board (Count IV).
(Rec. Doc. 40-1 at 6-7.) The Board contends that it is not a “person” capable of being sued under
§ 1983. (Rec. Doc. 14-1 at 4.)
La. Stat. Ann. § 36:4(B)(21) provides that “ [t]he Louisiana Public Defender Oversight
Board (R.S. 15:141 et seq.) shall be placed within the office of the governor  as an independent
agency and shall exercise its powers, duties, functions, and responsibilities in accordance with the
provisions of R.S. 36:801.1.” La. Stat. Ann. § 36:4 (emphasis added). The office of the governor is
within “the executive branch of state government.” See La. Stat. Ann. § 36:4(B).
La. Stat. Ann. § 36:801 makes clear  that transferred agencies, like the Board, “ shall
continue to exercise all powers, duties, functions, and responsibilities provided or authorized for
each by the constitution or laws which are in the nature of policymaking, rulemaking, licensing,
regulation, enforcement, or adjudication.” In addition, the Board “shall continue to be composed
and selected as provided by law.” La. Stat. Ann. § 36:801.

Our sister court in the Middle District of Louisiana held that an executive branch agency
is not a person that can be sued under § 1983. See Wingard v. Louisiana through Dep’t of Pub.
Safety & Corr., 594 F. Supp. 3d 704, 714 (M.D. La. 2022).
Here, while the Board is designated as an “independent agency,” it is housed within the
Louisiana executive branch , under the office of the governor. La. Stat. Ann. § 36:4(B)(21). It
continues to exercise its “powers, duties, functions, and responsibilities under the c onstitution or
laws.” La. Stat. Ann. § 36:801. The Board was established “to provide supervision and oversight
to the office of the state public defender and to approve contracts in an amount of two hundred
fifty thousand dollars or more.” La. Stat. Ann. § 15:146(A)(1). By being housed within Louisiana’s
executive branch, and supervising a state agency , see La. Stat. Ann. § 15:145(A), the Board is
acting as an arm of the state—i.e. a n executive agency —that cannot be sued under § 1983.
Accordingly, the Board is granted summary judgment as to Mr. Mosley’s § 1983 claims against it
(Counts III and IV).
(2) Mr. Starns, in his official and individual capacities
As for Mr. Starns, Mr. Mosley has brought claims against him in his individual and official

Once more, a state official in their official capacity cannot be sued under § 1983. See Will,
491 U.S. at 71. Here, Mr. Starns is a state official, “appointed by the governor”  La. Stat. Ann. §
15:152(A), who leads the  Louisiana Office of the Public Defender. See Rémy Voisin Starns, Office

of the State Public Defender, Staff,  (last visited Oct. 21, 2026),

Regarding Mr. Starns in his individual capacity, “[a]  state official sued in his personal
capacity under § 1983 is entitled to qualified immunity from damages, which protects the official
from liability for any act that was not objectively unreasonable at the time of the act. ” Moses v.
Mahmoud, No. CV 20 -2361, 2021 WL 4132220, at *7 (E.D. La. Feb. 10, 2021), report and
recommendation adopted, No. CV 20- 2361, 2021 WL 4127405 (E.D. La. Sept. 9, 2021)  (citing
Waller v. Hanlon, 922 F.3d 590, 599 (5th Cir. 2019) ). “A government official is entitled to QI
[(qualified immunity)] unless a plaintiff establishes ‘that (1) the defendant violated the plaintiff ’s
constitutional rights and (2) the defendant ’s actions were objectively unreasonable in light of
clearly established law at the time of the violation.’” Hutcheson v. Dallas Cnty., Tex., 994 F.3d 477,
480 (5th Cir. 2021)  (quoting Cowart v. Erwin , 837 F.3d 444, 454 (5th Cir. 2016) ). When the
plaintiff cannot satisfy the first step in the analysis, there is no dispute of material fact and summary
judgment is proper. See Id. at 481.
“Conclusory allegations are insufficient to state a constitutional violation.” Terry v. City of
New Orleans, 523 F. Supp. 2d 486, 493 (E.D. La. 2007). Additionally, “[w]here plaintiffs fail to
cite or identify law clearly establishing a constitutional violation, denial of  qualified immunity is
not justified even if, given the state of the law, plaintiffs ‘could have done so.’” Johnson v. Smith,

In both his first  and amended complaint s, Mr. Mosley fails to cite law establishing a
constitutional violation. In his original complaint, Mr. Mosley lacks citations to cases or authorities
supporting his three causes of actions. (Rec. Doc. 1 at 4-6.) Concerning the § 1983 claims against
Mr. Starns (Counts I and III), Mr. Mosley merely states in his amended complaint that  (1)
“Louisiana law and Fourteenth amendment jurisprudence” gives him a vested property right in his
criminal file (Count I) (Rec. Doc. 40 -1 at 4)  and (2) that he “has been stripped of…documents
needed to file applications for post-conviction relief under La. C.Cr.P. art 930.3…” (Count III) (Id.
at 6.) Notably, Mr. Mosley has previously filed for post-conviction relief in state and federal court.
See Mosley v. Cain, No. CIV A 06-6259, 2009 WL 2982930, at *2 (E.D. La. Sept. 14, 2009). The
state courts denied his post -conviction relief, as did this Court. Id.  at *2, *6. T he Fifth Circuit
ultimately denied his certificate on appeal for such relief. Mosley v. Cain, No. 09- 30978 USDC
No. 2:06-CV-6259, at *2.
Here, Mr. Mosley has failed to show that Mr. Starns violated a constitutional right. He has
not clearly established a property interest in his case file, nor has he shown how lacking the file
denies him access to the courts. Further more, Mr. Mosley has already filed for post -conviction
relief in state and federal forums, where he was denied such relief. See Mosley v. Cain, WL
2982930, at *2; see Mosley v. Cain, No. 09-30978 USDC No. 2:06-CV-6259, at *2. Because Mr.
Mosley has failed to satisfy his burden of the first step in the analysis for qualified immunity, there
is no genuine issue of material fact. See Hutcheson , 994 F.3d at 481. As a result, Mr. Starns  is
entitled to qualified immunity and summary judgment.

3. Supplemental jurisdiction over Mr. Mosley’ s state law claims is rejected.
Mr. Mosley raises Louisiana state law claims for professional negligence, breach of
fiduciary duty, conversion, and spoilation. (Rec. Doc. 40 -1 at 7 -8.) When a district court has
original jurisdiction over federal claims pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1331, it  “also has supplemental
jurisdiction over related state law claims when the state law claims are ‘so related to the claims in
the action within such original jurisdiction that they form part of the same case or controversy
under Article III of the United States Constitution.’” Murillo v. Coryell Cnty. Tradesmen, LLC, No.
CV 15-3641, 2017 WL 2721513, at *4 (E.D. La. June 23, 2017)  (quoting 28 U.S.C. § 1367(a) ).
Claims arise from the same case or controversy when “they ‘derive from a common nucleus of
operative fact.’” CheckPoint Fluidic Sys. Int'l, Ltd. v. Guccione , No. CIV .A. 10-4505, 2012 WL
195533, at *3 (E.D. La. Jan. 23, 2012)  (citing Mendoza v. Murphy, 532 F.3d 342, 346 (5th Cir.
2008)).
A district court has wide discretion on whether to exercise or refuse supplemental
jurisdiction. See Noble v. White, 996 F.2d 797, 799 (5th Cir. 1993). That discretion is reviewed by
the Fifth Circuit “only for abuse of discretion.” Moon v. City of El Paso, 906 F.3d 352, 360 (5th
Cir. 2018).
A district court may decline to exercise supplemental jurisdiction if:
(1) the claim raises a novel or complex issue of State law, (2) the claim substantially
predominates over the claim or claims over which the district court has original

18
jurisdiction, (3) the district court has dismissed all claims over which it has original
jurisdiction, or (4) in exceptional circumstances, there are other compelling reasons
for declining jurisdiction.
28 U.S.C.A. § 1367(c)(1-4) (West).
U
ltimately, “‘[t] he general rule is that a court should decline to exercise
jurisdiction over remaining state-law claims when all federal -law claims are eliminated before
trial.’” Hicks v. Austin Indep. Sch. Dist., 564 F. App’x 747, 748 (5th Cir. 2014) (quoting Brookshire
Bros. Holding, Inc. v. Dayco Prods., Inc., 554 F.3d 595, 602 (5th Cir.2009) ). The general rule
applies where a district court dismisses a plaintiff’s claims on summary judgment within the same
opinion. See Culotta v. Sodexo Remote Sites P’ ship, No. CIV .A. 10-2946A, 2011 WL 3268274, at
*5 (E.D. La. July 29, 2011).
“In addition to the statutory factors, the court must also balance the factors of judicial
economy, convenience, fairness, and comity.” Lemly v. St. Tammany Par. Hosp., Dist. No. 1, 614
F. Supp. 2d 727, 732 (E.D. La. 2008) (quoting Smith v. Amedisys, Inc., 298 F.3d 434, 446 (5th Cir.
2002)). Considering these additional factors, this Court has declined supplemental jurisdiction
where: (1) the state law claims have yet been addressed and exclusively involve issues of state law,
Collins v. Gusman, No. CIV .A. 14-234, 2015 WL 1468298, at *7 (E.D. La. Mar. 30, 2015); and (2)
where the litigation is still in the early stages, Kast v. Greater New Orleans Expressway Comm’n,
719 F. Supp. 2d 662, 677 (E.D. La. 2010), as amended (Sept. 13, 2010); contrast with Threadgill
v. Orleans Par. Sch. Bd., No. CIV .A. 02-1122, 2013 WL 773032, at *4 (E.D. La. Feb. 28, 2013)
(where the district court exercised supplemental jurisdiction because “the suit was filed over ten
years ago.”). However, the Fifth Circuit stated that a district court abuses its discretion following
dismissal of all federal claims “after [it] invests a significant amount of judicial resources in the
litigation…” See Brookshire Bros. Holding, 554 F.3d at 602.
