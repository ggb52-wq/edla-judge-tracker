Before the Court is the motion of defendants Dillard’s, Inc. (“Dillard’s”)
and Higbee Louisiana, LLC (“Higbee”) to compel arbitration and stay

For the following reasons, the Court grants the motion.

seq.; (2) retaliation against her in viol ation of the Civil Rights Act; (3)
creation of a racially hostile environment in violation of the Civil Rights Act;
(4) discrimination and retaliation in violation of the Family Medical Leave
Act (“FMLA”); and (5) intentional infliction of emotional distress in violation

strong federal policy in favor of enforcing arbitration agreements.  See, e.g.,

Dean Witter Reynolds, Inc. v. Byrd, 470 U.S. 213, 217–18 (1985); Southland
Corp. v. Keating , 465 U.S. 1, 10 (1984); Moses H. Cone Mem’l Hosp. v.
Mercury Constr. Corp. , 460 U.S. 1, 24 (1983); Safer v. Nelson Fin. Group,
Inc., 422 F.3d 289, 294 (5th Cir. 2005).  Under the FAA, a written agreement
to arbitrate “shall be valid, irrevocabl e, and enforceable, save upon such
grounds as exist at law or in equity for the revocation of any contract.”
9 U.S.C. § 2.  This provision reflects  “both a liberal fede ral policy favoring
arbitration, and the fundamental princi ple that arbitration is a matter of
contract.”  AT&T Mobility LLC v. Concepcion , 563 U.S. 333, 339 (2011)
(cleaned up).  Section 3 of the FAA further provides that if any suit is brought
in federal court “upon any issue refera ble to arbitration,” the court, “upon
being satisfied that the issue involved in such suit or proceeding is referable
to arbitration . . . , shall on application of one of the parties stay the trial of
the action until such arbitration has b een had in accordance with the terms
of the agreement.”  9 U.S.C. § 3.
The Supreme Court has directed courts to “‘rigorously enforce’
arbitration agreements according to their terms.”  Am. Express Co. v. Italian
Colors Rest., 570 U.S. 228, 233 (2013) (quoting Dean Witter, 470 U.S. at
221).  The Act “leaves no place for the exercise of discretion by a district court,
but instead mandates that district courts shall direct the parties to proceed

to arbitration on issues as to whic h an arbitration agreement has been
signed.”  Dean Witter, 470 U.S. at 218.  Additionally, “any doubts concerning
the scope of an arbitration agreemen t should be resolved in favor of
arbitration.”  Safer, 422 F.3d at 294.
To determine whether to compel the parties to arbitrate, the Court must
conduct a two-step inquiry.  Id. at 293.  First, the Court must determine
whether the parties agreed to arbi trate the dispute in question.  Id.  T h i s
determination ordinarily involves two considerations: “(1) whether there is a
valid agreement to arbitrate between the parties; and (2) whether the dispute
in question falls within the scope of that arbitration agreement.”  Id.  But
when, as here, the parties’ agreement contains a delegation clause, the Court
simply analyzes whether there is a valid agreement, and if so, whether there
is “clear and unmistakable  evidence” that the partie s intended to arbitrate
the arbitrability issue.  See Lloyd’s Syndicate 457 v. FloaTEC, L.L.C. , 921
F.3d 508, 514 (5th Cir. 2019).
Second, if the Court determines that the parties have agreed to arbitrate
the dispute in question, it then must  ask whether “any federal statute or
policy renders the claim nonarbitrable.”  Sherer v. Green Tree Servicing
LLC, 548 F.3d 379, 381 (5th Cir. 2008).  If, as here, “neither party argues that

a federal statute or policy would bar arbitration,” the Court need not analyze
the second step.  Id.
When the Court is “satisfied that  the making of the agreement for
arbitration or the failure to  comply therewith is not in  issue, [it] shall make
an order directing the parties to proceed  to arbitration in accordance with
the terms of the agreement.”  9 U.S.C. § 4.  If the Court finds that the dispute
is subject to arbitration, and a party has requested a stay pending arbitration,
the Court must issue a stay ra ther than dismiss the case.  Id. § 3; see also
Smith v. Spizzirri , 601 U.S. 472, 475–76 (202 4) (holding that courts lack
“discretion to dismiss the suit on the ba sis that all the claims are subject to
arbitration”).
The movants seeking to compel arbitr ation bear the initial burden of
proving that an arbitration agreemen t exists by a preponderance of the
evidence.  Chester v. DirecTV, L.L.C., 607 F. App’x 362, 364 (5th Cir. 2015)
(citing Banks v. Mitsubishi Moto rs Credit of Am., Inc. , 435 F.3d 538, 540
(5th Cir. 2005) (per curiam)).  If a valid arbitration agr eement exists, “the
party resisting arbitration bears the bu rden of proving that the claims at
issue are unsuitable for arbitration.”  Green Tree Fin. Corp.-Ala. v.
Randolph, 531 U.S. 79, 91 (2000).

III. DISCUSSION
First, the Court must apply ordinary state-law principles of contract to
determine whether Riggins entered into a valid agreement to arbitrate with
defendants.  Will-Drill Res. Inc. v. Samson Res. Co. , 352 F.3d 211, 218 (5th
Cir. 2003); see also Klein v. Na bors Drilling USA L.P. , 710 F.3d 234, 236
(5th Cir. 2013) (“Because arbitration is simply a matter of contract between
the parties, the strong federal policy favoring arbitration does not apply to
the initial determination of whether there is a valid agreement to arbitrate.”)
(cleaned up).  The parties do not dispute that Louisiana law applies here.7
In Louisiana, the law’s favorable treatment of arbitr ation agreements
“echo[e]s the Federal Arbitration Act.”  Aguillard v. Auction Mgmt. Corp. ,
908 So. 2d 1, 7 (La. 2005); see also Duhon v. Activelaf, LLC, 411 So. 3d 605,
609 (La. 2016) (“Louisiana and federal law explicitly favor the enforcement
of arbitration clauses in written contracts.”); id. (noting that “federal
jurisprudence interpreting the FAA ma y be considered in construing the
[Louisiana Binding Arbitration Law (“ LBAL”)]”); La. Stat. Ann. §§ 9:4201,
9:4202 (expressing strong legislative policy favoring arbitration).  Louisiana
law requires that a valid and enforcea ble arbitration agreement contain the
elements of offer and acceptance.  La. Civ. Code Ann. art. 1927.  Additionally,

Court must determine only whether th ere is “clear and unmistakable
evidence that the parties in tended to arbitrate” the issue of arbitrability.
