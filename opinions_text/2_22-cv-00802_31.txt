REPORT AND RECOMMENDATION

 Defendants BP Exploration & Production Inc. and BP America Production Company
(collectively, “BP”) filed a Notice of Personal Service of Suggestion of Death and an Unopposed
Motion to Dismiss for Failure to Substitute a Party.  ECF Nos. 29, 30.
 Having considered the record, the parties’ submissions, and the applicable law , I
recommend that the Unopposed Motion to Dismiss be GRANTED for the following reasons.
I. BACKGROUND
Plaintiff William Douglas Koran filed this back-end litigation option (“ BELO”) suit for
Later Manifested Physical Conditions (“LMPC”), on March 28, 2022, seeking damages for alleged
exposure related to the BP oil spill .  ECF No. 1 , ¶¶ 5, 14.   Plaintiff was  allegedly exposed as a
Zone A Resident as defined by the Medical Service Agreement  and by his work cleaning up the
spill.  Id. ¶¶ 27–28.
On November 21, 2022, counsel for Plaintiff filed a Rule 25(a)(1) Suggestion of Death into
the court record reflecting that Plaintiff died on October 8, 2022 as evidenced by the attached death
certificate.  ECF Nos. 23, 23- 1.  That filing did not, however, indicate that the notice had been
served on a relevant non -party representative of decedent’s estate.  BP later filed a Motion to
Dismiss based on that notice, but withdrew that motion.  ECF Nos. 24, 26, 27.

II. APPLICABLE LAW AND ANALYSIS
Rule 25(a) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure provides:
(a) Death.
(1) Substitution if the Claim is Not Extinguished.  If a party dies and the claim is not
extinguished, the court may order substitution of the proper party. A motion for
substitution may be made by any party or by the decedent ’s successor or
representative.  If the motion is not made within 90 days after service of a statement
noting the death, the action by or against the decedent must be dismissed.
. . .
(3) Service.  A motion to substitute, together with a notice of hearing, must be served
on the parties as provided in Rule 5, and on nonparties as provided in Rule 4.  A
statement noting death must be served in the same manner.  Service may be made
in any judicial district.
“The rule is procedural, and federal courts must apply federal rules, not state law, to determine
proper procedure for substitution following a party’s death.” 1  While state law may govern the
substantive question of who qualifies as a proper party, “Rule 25 intends liberal substitution to be
permitted and does not require the plaintiff to invoke state machinery to produce a representative
of the estate.”2  Rather, “[t]he proper inquiry is whether the party to be substituted could qualify

Before this 90- day clock can begin to run, however, the suggestion of death “must be
served on the parties as provided in Rule 5 and on nonparties as provided in Rule 4.” 4  Although
Rule 25(a)(3) does not specify the non-parties to be served, “most courts have construed Rule 25(a)
as imposing a general obligation on the noticing party to serve notice upon a deceased party’ s
successor or representative.”5  Personal service on the non-party alerts him “to the consequences
of death for the pending suit, signaling the need for action to preserve the claim if so desired.” 6
This s ervice is generally required, “ even where it is difficult to determine who the personal
representative is.”7  Requiring the serving party to shoulder the burden of locating the personal
representative to serve is more appropriate than “ permitting the absence of notice to decedent ’s
representative to lead to forfeiture of the action. ”8  Moreover, notwithstanding the mandatory
language used in Rule 25, pursuant to F ED. R. CIV. P. 6(b), courts are vested with discretion to
extend the specified time period upon a showing of good cause.9
