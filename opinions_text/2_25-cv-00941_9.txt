B. Required Elements of a § 1983 Claim
Section 1983 creates a damages remedy for the violation of federal constitutional or
statutory rights under color of state law:
Every person who, under color of any statute, ordinance, regulation, custom, or
usage, of any State . . . subjects, or causes to be subjected, any . . . person within
the jurisdiction thereof to the deprivation of any rights, privileges, or immunities
secured by the Constitution and laws, shall be liable to the party injured in an action
at law . . . .14
“The purpose of § 1983 is to deter state actors from using their badge of authority to deprive
individuals of federally guaranteed rights and to provide relief to victims if such deterrence fails.”15
A plaintiff must satisfy three elements to establish § 1983 liability:

“The traditional definition of acting under color of state law requires that the defendant in
a § 1983 action have exercised power ‘possessed by virtue of state law and made possible only

provides a remedy for designated rights, rather than creating any substantive rights, “an underlying

theory of vicarious liability.21  There must be an affirmative link between the incident and some

B. Claims of Intentional Indifference to Serious Medical Needs
Broadly construing the complaint, Sampay asserts claim of intentional indifference to his
medical needs by jail staff discontinuing his prescribed treatment for a bulging disk.  “[D]eliberate
indifference to serious medical needs of prisoners constitutes the ‘unnecessary and wanton
infliction of pain’ proscribed by the Eighth Amendment.” 35  Deliberate indifference “is an
extremely high standard to meet.”36  A prison official acts with deliberate indifference “only if (a)
he knows that inmates face a substantial risk of serious bodily harm and (b) he disregards that risk
by failing to take reasonable measures to abate it.”37
Deliberate i ndifference may be “manifested by prison doctors in their response to the
prisoner’s needs or by prison guards in intentionally denying or delaying access to medical care or
intentionally interfering with the treatment once prescribed.” 38  This same subjective deliberate
indifference standard has been applied to pre -trial detainees under the Fourteenth Amendment. 39
To show deliberate indifference, a prisoner must establish that prison officials “refused to treat
him, ignored his complaints, intentionally treated him incorrectly, or engaged in any similar
conduct that would clearly evince a wanton disregard for any serious medical needs.”40  The Fifth

a. Medical Care Claims against EMT Guidry
Sampay names EMT Guidry as a defendant due to h is role as the medical personnel who
purportedly accused him of holding pills. ECF No. 8 at 1.
To hold a defendant individually liable under § 1983, plaintiff must establish either that
the defendant was “personally involved in the acts causing the deprivation of his constitutional
rights or that a causal connection exists between an act of [the def endant] . . . and the alleged
constitutional violation.”42 While there is a connection between Guidry’s alleged actions and the
decision to withhold medication , Guidry did not refuse to treat Sampay, ignore his complaints,
intentionally treat him incorrectly, or demonstrate a wanton disregard for any serious medical need.
Guidry reported the allegation to defendant Tomlin, who was the person responsible for deciding
to discontinue the prescribed course of medications.  Simply being involved in the series of events,
but not being involved in the  decision to deviate from the prescribed course of treatment and
withhold medication, does not rise to a constitutional violation for denial of medical care.43
Thus, Sampay’s claims against defendant EMT Guidry are frivolous and otherwise fail to
state a claim for which relief can be granted under § 1983 and should be dismissed pursuant to 28
U.S.C. § 1915, § 1915A and 42 U.S.C. § 1997e.

A delay in medical care constitutes an Eighth Amendment violation only if there was
deliberate indifference, as defined above, which resulted in substantial harm. 44 The Fifth Circuit
has noted that “because medical treatment was provided, even if it was negligent, disagreed-with,

does not equal denied treatment.” 46 Prison medical personnel have “discretion whether to follow
any medication prescriptions in [an inmate ’s/detainee’s] hospital-discharge instructions.”47  This
is true even if the alternative medication prescribed or treatment plan followed by medical
providers at the prison was not as effective, because federal constitutional protections are not
violated simply because an inmate’s medical treatment was unsuccessful or because pain persisted
despite treatment.48

discontinued by Tomlin, he was given no course of treatment at all. Prison officials failing to
