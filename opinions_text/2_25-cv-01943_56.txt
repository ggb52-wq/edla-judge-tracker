Before the Court is a motion for summary judgment filed by defendants the State of
Louisiana through the Department of Public Safety and Corrections, James LeBlanc, Travis Day,
and Gary Westcott (collectively, “Defendants”)  seeking dismissal of all of plaintiff Alford Lee
Rose’s claims for failure to exhaust administrative remedies .1  Rose, who is proceeding pro se,

law, the Court grants the motion because Rose failed to exhaust his administrative remedies.
I. BACKGROUND
 Rose, who is incarcerated at Rayburn Correctional Center (“RCC”) and completely blind,
is asserting claims against the Defendants under 42 U.S.C. §  1983 for violating his Eighth
Amendment right to be free from cruel and unusual punishment, denying him equal protection of
the law, violating the Americans with Disabilities Act, and failure to properly train, instruct, and
supervise Defendants’ employees. 3  On May 26, 2025 , Rose filed an Administrative Remedy
Procedure (“ARP”) complaint numbered RCC -2025-359 (the “request for remedy”), which was
received by the warden’s office the following day.4

A. Summary Judgment Standard
Summary judgment is proper if the pleadings, depositions, answers to interrogatories, and
admissions on file, together with the affidavits, if any, show that there is no genuine issue as to
any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to a judgm ent as a matter of law.  Celotex
Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317, 322 (1986); Fed. R. Civ. P. 56.  “Rule 56(c) mandates the entry of
summary judgment, after adequate time for discovery and upon motion, against a party who fails
to make a showing sufficient to establish the existence of an element essential to that party’s case,
and on which that party will bear the burden of proof at trial.”  Celotex, 477 U.S. at 322.  A party
moving for summary judgment bears the initial burden of demonstrating the basis for summary
judgment and identifying those portions of the record, discovery, and any affidavits supporting the
conclusion that there is no genuine is sue of material fact.  Id. at 323.  If the moving party meets
that burden, then the nonmoving party must use evidence cognizable under Rule 56 to demonstrate
the existence of a genuine issue of material fact.  Id. at 324.
A genuine issue of material fact exists if a reasonable jury could return a verdict for the
nonmoving party.  See Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 248 (1986).  The substantive
law identifies which facts are material.  Id.  Material facts are not genuinely disputed when a
rational trier of fact could not find for the nonmoving party upon a review of the record taken as a
whole.  See Matsushita Elec. Indus. Co. v. Zenith Radio Corp. , 475 U.S. 574, 587 (1986); EEOC
v. Simbaki, Ltd. , 767 F.3d 475, 481 (5th Cir. 2014).  Unsubstantiated assertions, conclusory
allegations, and merely colorable factual bases are insufficient to defeat a motion for summary

judgment.  See Anderson, 477 U.S. at 249-50; Little v. Liquid Air Corp., 37 F.3d 1069, 1075 (5th
Cir. 1994); Hopper v. Frank, 16 F.3d 92, 97 (5th Cir. 1994).  In ruling on a summary -judgment
motion, a court may not resolve credibility issues or weigh evidence.  See Delta & Pine Land Co.
v. Nationwide Agribusiness Ins. Co., 530 F.3d 395, 398-99 (5th Cir. 2008).  Furthermore, a court
must assess the evidence, review the facts, and draw any appropriate inferences based on the
evidence in the light most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment.  See Tolan v. Cotton,
572 U.S. 650, 656-57 (2014); Daniels v. City of Arlington, 246 F.3d 500, 502 (5th Cir. 2001).  Yet,
a court only draws reasonable inferences in favor of the nonmovant “when there is an actual
controversy, that is, when both parties have submitted evidence of contradictory facts.”  Little, 37
F.3d at 1075 (citing Lujan v. Nat’l Wildlife Fed’n, 497 U.S. 871, 888 (1990)).
After the movant demonstrates the absence of a genuine issue of material fact, the
nonmovant must articulate specific facts showing a genuine issue and point to supporting,
competent evidence that may be presented in a form admissible at trial.  See Lynch Props., Inc. v.
Potomac Ins. Co. , 140 F.3d 622, 625 (5th Cir. 1998); Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(c)(1)(A), (c)(2).  Such
facts must create more than “some metaphysical doubt as to the material facts.”  Matsushita, 475
U.S. at 586.  When the nonmovant will bear the burden of proof at trial on the dispositive issue,
the moving party may simply point to insufficient admissible evidence to establish an essential
element of the nonmovant’s claim in order to satisfy its summary-judgment burden.  See Celotex,
477 U.S. at 322-25; Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(c)(1)(B).  Unless there is a genuine issue for trial that could
support a judgment in favor of the nonmovant, summary judgment must be granted.  See Little, 37
F.3d at 1075-76.

B. Analysis
1. Timeliness of Defendants’ Motion
 Before addressing the merits of Defendants’ motion for summary judgment, the Court will
take up Rose’s procedural challenges to the motion.  Rose first argues that a summary-judgment
motion is improper at this juncture because Defendants have not yet filed an answer or a motion
to dismiss.22  Rule 56(b) provides that “[u]nless a different time is set by local rule or the court
orders otherwise, a party may file a motion for summary judgment at any time until 30 days after
the close of all discovery.”  Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(b) (emphasis added).  There is nothing in the local
rules regarding the timing of summary-judgment motions, and this Court did not issue in this case
an order altering in any way the commencement time for filing summary-judgment motions.  That
Defendants had not yet filed an answer or motion to dismiss was no impediment then to their filing
the motion for summary judgment.
 Rose also challenges the summary-judgment motion as premature because he asserts that
further discovery is required to support his opposition to Defendants’ motion. 23  But Rose’s
opposition “d[oes] not ‘identify specific facts … that would alter the [Court’s] analysis’ or in any
way ‘demonstrate how the additional discovery would likely create a genuine dispute of material
fact.’”  MDK Sociedad de Responsabilidad Limitada v. Poplant Inc. , 25 F.4th 360, 366 (5th Cir.
2022) (citation modified) (quoting Mendez v. Poitevent, 823 F.3d 326, 337 (5th Cir. 2 016)).  Nor
does Rose provide the affidavit or declaration required by Rule 56(d) to forestall the Court’s
consideration of the motion pending discovery of specific facts essential to justify his opposition.
See Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(b) (providing that a court may defer consideration of a summary-judgment
motion or deny it “[i]f a nonmovant shows by affidavit or declaration that, for specified reasons,

2. Exhaustion of Administrative Remedies
 Again, Defendants seek the dismissal of Rose’s claims for failure to exhaust administrative
remedies. However, Rose contends that he exhausted his administrative remedies when he
forwarded his rejected request for remedy to the Secretary of the Louisiana Department of Public
Safety and Corrections and thereafter never received a response.25
Pursuant to the Prison Litigation Reform Act (“PLRA ”), prisoner-plaintiffs must exhaust
their administrative remedies before bringing an action under §  1983, or any federal law, for
injuries related to prison conditions or events.  42 U.S.C. § 1997e(a) (“No action shall be brought
with respect to prison conditions under section 1983 of this title, or any other Federal law, by a
prisoner confined in any jail, prison, or other correctional facility until such administrative
remedies as are available are e xhausted.”); Porter v. Nussle , 534 U.S. 516, 520 (2002).  “[T]he
PLRA’s exhaustion requirement applies to all inmate suits about prison life, whether they involve
general circumstances or particular episodes, and whether they allege excessive force or some
other wrong.”  Porter, 534 U.S. at 532.  Because exhaustion is an affirmative defense, the
Defendants bear the burden to demonstrate that Rose failed to exhaust his administrative remedies.
Cowart v. Erwin, 837 F.3d 444, 451 (5th Cir. 2016).
Courts look to the prison’s grievance procedures “[t]o determine what remedies are
‘available’ and thus must be exhausted.” Huskey v. Jones , 45 F.4th 827, 831 (5th Cir. 2022)
(quotation omitted); Wilson v. Epps, 776 F.3d 296, 299 (5th Cir. 2015); Cowart, 837 F.3d at 451.

Louisiana’s ARP grievance process , which applies to all facilities o perated by the Louisiana
Department of Public Safety and Corrections, La. Admin. Code tit. 22, pt. I, § 325(D)(1), is a two-
step process, and “a prisoner has not exhausted his remedies until he has completed both.”  Bargher
v. White , 928 F.3d 439, 447 (5th Cir. 2019).   First, an inmate commences the “process by
completing a request for administrative remedy … or writing a letter to the warden, in which he
briefly sets out the basis for his claim, and the relief sought.”  La. Admin. Code tit . 22, pt. I,
§ 325(G)(1)(a)(i).  The prisoner must make the request within 90 days of the alleged circumstance
or event that forms the basis for his complaint.  Id. § 325(G)(1).
An ARP screening officer then screens the request before it is assigned to the first step and
notifies the prisoner as to whether his request has been accepted or rejected.  Id. § 325(I)(1).  If the
screening officer rejects the request, the prisoner must “be provided written notification of the
grounds upon which the rejection is based.”  Id. § 325(I)(1)(c)(ii).  The Louisiana Administrative
Code provides the exclusive reasons for which the screening officer may reject the request.
Relevant to the captioned matter, a request is rejected if “[t]he offender has requested a remedy
for more than one incident  (a multiple complaint) ” or if “[t]  here has been a time lapse of more
than 90 days between the event and the initial request.”  Id. § 325(I)(1)(c)(i)((g)), ((i)).  The
prisoner “has not properly exhausted administrative remedies if his request is rejected for any of
the reasons” enumerated in the Administrative Code.  Id. § 325(I)(1)(c)(iv).  The prisoner must
correct the noted deficiencies and resubmit his request  for remedy  to the screening officer,
otherwise it “will not be processed.”  See id. § 325(I)(1)(a)(ii), (c)(iii).  The prisoner cannot appeal
the rejection to step two until his request for remedy is accepted and processed pursuant to step
one.  See id. § 325(I)(1)(c)(iii)

 Once the request is accepted, it is assigned to step one, and  the warden has 40 days to
respond.  Id. § 325(I)(1), (J)(a)(ii).   If the warden fails to timely respond or if the prisoner is
dissatisfied with the warden’s response, the prisoner may then proceed to step two by appealing
the matter to the Secretary of the Louisiana Department of Public Safety and Corrections.   Id.
§ 325(J)(1)(b)(i).  Only after exhausting these steps may the prisoner file suit in district court if he
is not satisfied with the Secretary’s response or if the Secretary fails to respond within 45 days.
Id. § 325(J)(1)(b)(iv).
 Because Rose concedes that he did not follow the proper administrative procedure with

demonstrated that Rose failed to exhaust his administrative remedies, which requires the dismissal
of his complaint.  In both his initial complaint and opposition to the instant motion, Rose claims
that following the ARP screening officer’s rejection of his request  for remedy , he proceeded
directly to step two by forwarding the request for remedy to the Secretary.26  And he provides no
documentation or other evidence indicating that he resubmitted his request for remedy  to the
screening officer or warden after attempting to correct the noted deficiencies.  As Stacey Rowell’s
affidavit attests, the Department of Public Safety and Corrections’ ARP computer database  does

3. Availability of Administrative Remedies
Rose also contends that the ARP system does not qualify as an “available” remedy 29
because prison officials failed to follow the ARP procedure, 30 including the failure to timely
respond to other ARP requests.31  He supports this assertion by providing documentation and a
Louisiana court of appeal decision relating  to ARP requests filed by his fellow inmate Derrick
Jerome Allen.32
 The Supreme Court has held that a prisoner only needs to exhaust “available” remedies.
Ross v. Blake, 578 U.S. 632, 642 (2016).  A remedy is “available” if its “procedures … are ‘capable
of use’ to obtain ‘some relief for the action complained of.’”   Id. (quoting Booth v. Churner, 532
U.S. 731, 738 (2001)).  But a prisoner has no obligation to exhaust “unavailable” remedies.  Id.  A
remedy is “unavailable” when “(1) prison officials are unable or consistently unwilling to provide
any relief to aggrieved inmates, (2) the administrative scheme is so opaque that it becomes,

promptly and efficiently handled his request for remedy.  The ARP process does not set a deadline

should not unreasonably restrain the offender’s opportunity to seek a remedy.”  La. Admin. Code

26, 2025, and within three days received written notice of , and the reasons for , its rejection,34
giving him  ample opportunity to address the noted deficiencies and resubmit the request for
remedy as to any complained-of incidents that were not yet stale.
 Rose’s use of Allen’s documentation is immaterial to the disposition of th is matter.  The
manner through which  RCC officials processed another inmate’s ARP request s for remedy is
irrelevant to whether the officials properly handled Rose’s request for remedy  in this matter.
Because Defendants promptly and efficiently processed Rose’s request for remedy in compliance
with the applicable provisions of the Administrative Code , he had access to “available”
administrative remedies.  He simply failed to exhaust them.
