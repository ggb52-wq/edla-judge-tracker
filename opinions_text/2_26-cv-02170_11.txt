 State sovereign immunity, reflected in the Eleventh Amendment, protects
states or “arm[s] of the state” from suit in federal court. 3 Unless a state has waived
its sovereign immunity or Congress has abrogated it, federal courts do not have
jurisdiction over suits against a state, state agency, or state official acting in its
official capacity.4 The law in this area “comport[s] with the common-sense notion that
a plaintiff cannot avoid the sovereign immunity bar by suing a state agency or an arm
of a State rather than the State itself.”5 “[R]elief sought nominally against an officer
is in fact against the sovereign if the decree would operate against the latter.”6
 The Ex Parte Young  doctrine, however, provides an exception that allows a
plaintiff to sue a state actor in his official capacity when seeking injunctive relief to
prevent an ongoing violation of federal law.7 This exception recognizes that the state
cannot “impart to the official immunity from responsibility to the supreme authority
of the United States.”8 Thus, when deciding whether the Ex Parte Young doctrine
applies, a court considers “whether the complaint alleges an ongoing violation of
federal law” against a state official in his official capacity “and seeks relief properly
characterized as prospective.”9

The first question is whether the State of Louisiana has generally waived its
right to sovereign immunity in federal court. It has not. Rather, Louisiana law
provides that “[n]o suit against the state or a state agency or political subdivision
shall be instituted in any court other than a Louisiana state court.”10
The Court next determines whether the suit is against a state, arm of the state,
or state official. Because Taylor’s suit and current motion for a temporary restraining
order seek declaratory and injunctive relief against Delahoussaye and Jackson-Smart
in their capacities as officers of a state agency, it is functionally seeking relief against
the state agency itself, which is entitled to sovereign immunity.11
 The Ex Parte Young  exception does not save Taylor’s claims. “T he Ex Parte
Young doctrine does not permit ‘ a declaratory judgment that respondent violated
federal law in the past .’”12 Taylor’s requested declaratory relief is “fundamentally
retrospective because it does not relate to an ongoing violation of federal rights.” 13
Instead, it seeks to remedy the termination of her emergency housing voucher, the
denial of her portability request, and the failure to respond to her accommodation
request in April 2026.14 The same is true for the injunctive relief Taylor seeks. Rather
than there being any ongoing violation, the request is to prohibi t defendants from
recording or treating her  housing benefits as terminated or omitting her dwelling
