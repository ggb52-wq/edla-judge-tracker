II. Standard of Law
 “ The controlling law to be applied here is that of [Louisiana],  which governs
[Plaintiffs’] causes of action and, by extension, any assertion of attorney -client
privilege or putative waiver thereof.” See In re Itron, Inc., 883 F.3d 553, 557 (5th Cir.
2018). Louisiana law is clear: “[a] party who makes a pretrial partial disclosure of his
attorney-client communications waives his privilege as to all such communications
on the same subject unless he stipulates that he will not introduce any such
communications at trial. ” Smith v. Kavanaugh, Pierson & Talley , 513 So.2d 1138,
1141 (La. 1987) . There can be no partial waiver of privilege when it comes to
communications on the same subject matter. “[P]ermitting a party to make such an
incomplete disclosure, without losing his privilege with respect to the remainder of
the communication or communications on that subject, would be unfair to the
adversary because it would give the privilege-holder unchecked editorial control over
the available evidence to a degree that would practically ensure a distorted
presentation of the communication or communications.”  Id. at 1144. When a party
has “committed [itself]  to a course of action that will require the disclosure of a

 D. Other Law Firms
 As explained above , Smoothie King waived privilege by raising an advice-of-
counsel defense to demonstrate that its actions with respect to Plaintiffs were
undertaken in good faith. Under Louisiana law,  production of an attorney -client
communication results in a waiver of privilege that extends beyond the attorney or
law firm involved in that communication. See Smith, 513 So.2d at 1144 (“Accordingly,
a privilege-holder who testifies . . . at trial about his privileged communications with
his attorney or physician, waives his right to invoke the privilege as to cross -
examination or testimony of others with regard to communications on the same
subject.”). On the facts of this case, however, the waiver does not extend to Smoothie
King’s communications with other law firms for the purpose of receiving  a second
opinion or formulating a demand as to prior counsel’s25 allegedly erroneous advice.

  1. Morrison & Foerster LLP
 Smoothie King consulted with Morrison & Foerster LLP (“Morrison Foerster”)
in 2024 to obtain a second opinion relative to the advice provided by Baker Donelson
in 2020. 26 While the  fact of that consultation  is discoverable, Smoothie King’s
communications with Morrison Foerster remain subject to attorney-client privilege.
In the context of legal malpractice lawsuits, plaintiffs routinely waive attorney-
client privilege as to their prior counsel. Courts often hold, however, that these
plaintiffs may claim privilege as to communications with successor counsel  even
though they may involve the same subject matter in a general sense. See, e.g.,
Townley v. United States, No. 3:22-CV-107 (CDL), 2023 WL 7222681, at *2 (M.D. Ga.
Nov. 2, 2023); Cole v. AADCO Med., Inc., No. 2:15-CV-200, 2017 WL 11678099, at *3
(D. Vt. Oct. 19, 2017).27 This result reflects an understanding of subject matter waiver
that accounts for necessary litigation context. Under this framework, when a party
communicates with one firm to obtain advice, then later communicates with a second
firm to formulate  a claim or defense alleging that the first firm’s advice was
erroneous, the two sets of communications do not address the same subject matter
for purpose of waiver.28 This approach promotes compliance with the law while
