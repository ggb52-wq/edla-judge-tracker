II. Standards of Review for Frivolousness
Pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1915(e)(2) and § 1915A  and 42 U.S.C. § 1997e(c), the Court is
required to sua sponte  dismiss cases filed by prisoners proceeding in forma pauperis  upon a
determination that they are frivolous.  The Court has broad discretion in determining the frivolous
nature of the complaint.  See Cay v. Estelle, 789 F.2d 318, 325 (5th Cir. 1986), modified on other
grounds, Booker v. Koonce, 2 F.3d 114 (5th Cir. 1993).  However, the Court may not sua sponte
dismiss an action merely because of questionable le gal theories or unlikely factual allegations in
the complaint.

Under this statute, a claim is frivolous when it lacks an arguable basis either in law or fact.
Neitzke v. Williams , 490 U.S. 319, 324-25 (1989); Talib v. Gilley , 138 F.3d 211, 213 (5th Cir.
1998).  “A [claim] lacks an arguable basis in law if  it is based on an indis putably meritless legal
theory, such as if the complaint alleges the viol ation of a legal interest which clearly does not
exist.”  Harper v. Showers, 174 F.3d 716, 718 (5th Cir. 1999) (quoting Davis v. Scott, 157 F.3d
882, 889 (5th Cir. 1998)).  It lacks an arguable fact ual basis only if the fact s alleged are “clearly
baseless,” a category encompassing fancif ul, fantastic, and delu sional allegations.  Denton v.
Hernandez, 504 U.S. 25, 32-33 (1992) (citing Neitzke, 490 U.S. at 327-28).  Thus, the Court must
determine whether plaintiff’s claims are based on an indisputably meritless legal theory or clearly
baseless factual allegations.  Reeves v. Collins , 27 F.3d 174, 176 (5th Cir. 1994); Jackson v.
Vannoy, 49 F.3d 175, 176-77 (5th Cir. 1995); Moore v. Mabus, 976 F.2d 268, 269 (5th Cir. 1992).
III. Analysis
A. Improper Private Defendants
Sencial has named three privat e defendants, Reina, the victim and owner of a business
where the assault occurred, and two witnesses, Lopez and Cruz, who gave statements to police on
the day Sencial was arrested.  He claims that Re ina injured him by abuse of process, defamation,
libel, and slander for reporting false allegations to the police, and that Lopez and Cruz lied to police
to aid Reina’s efforts.
A plaintiff bringing a § 1983 complaint is require d to identify bot h a constitutional
violation and the responsible “state actor” or person acting under color of state law.  Flagg Bros.,
Inc. v. Brooks, 436 U.S. 149, 156 (1978).  Under the “color of law” requirement, a defendant in a
§ 1983 action must have committed th e complained-of acts in the course of their performance of
duties and must have misused their power possessed by virtue of state law and made possible only

4

because the alleged wrongdoer/defe ndant was clothed with the authority of state law.  United
States v. Classic , 313 U.S. 299, 325 (1941); Screws v. United States , 325 U.S. 91, 110 (1944).
However, “[p]rivate individuals generally are not considered to act under color of law, i.e., are not
considered state actors.”  Ballard v. Wall, 413 F.3d 510, 518 (5th Cir. 2005) (citations omitted).
Pretermitting Sencial’s failure to assert a constitutional violation against these persons, it
is clear from Sencial’s claims th at Reina, Lopez, and Cruz are not state actors as required for
liability under § 1983.  To the contrary, all three were private citizens who lodged a complaint or
provided statements to police about  Sencial’s criminal activity.  N one of them are alleged to be
clothed with or acting under the authority of state law.  Providing statements to the police, even if
false, is insufficien t to deem a private person  to be a state actor subject to § 1983 liability.  See
Hammond v. Wilson, No. 08-1063, 2009 WL 467648, at *2 (S.D. Ohio Fe b. 23, 2009) (a private
citizen who gave a statement to police during a criminal investigation was not subject to a § 1983
suit); Vasquez v. Rubalcava , No. 11-1968, 2012 WL 760860, at *5  (C.D. Cal. Mar. 7, 2012) (a
witness was not acting under color of state law when she reported what she believed was a crime,
and was therefore not subject to § 1983 liability); Goehring v. Wright, 858 F. Supp. 989, 998 (N.D.
Cal. Jul. 20, 1994) (private citizens who complained to local authorities about neighbor’s alleged
criminal activities, giving rise to neighbor’s arrest and prosecu tion, were not “state actors” for
purposes of § 1983); Marion v. Groh, 954 F. Supp. 39, 42-43 (D. Conn. Jan. 17, 1997) (even if the
witness’s statement to police wa s false, the witness did not act under color of law for § 1983
liability); Paulino v. Banguera , No. 10-4784, 2011 WL 1831759, at  *6 (D.N.J. May 12, 2011)
(plaintiff failed to state a § 1983 claim for which relief could be  granted against witnesses who
accused plaintiff of crimin al activity and gave allegedly untru e statements to police because the
witnesses were not state actors).

The Court recognizes that private individuals may be liable under § 1983 for engaging in
a conspiracy with state actors to violate a person’s constitutional rights.  Ballard, 413 F.3d at 518;
see Mills v. Criminal District Court # 3, 837 F.2d 677, 679 (5th Cir. 1988); see also Mowbray v.
Cameron Cnty., Tex., 274 F.3d 269, 278 (5th Cir. 2001) ( “[I]t is possible, in limited circumstances,
to allege a § 1983 conspiracy claim against a private actor . . .”)  However, Sencial does not allege
a conspiracy nor does he provide factual basis to support such claim between Reina, Lopez, and/or
Cruz and any state actor (police or otherwise) to create this rare state action status.  Mills, 837 F.2d
679-80; Marts v. Hines , 68 F.3d 134, 136 (5th Cir. 1995) (“‘mere conclusory allegations of
conspiracy cannot, absent refere nce to material fact s,’ state a substantia l claim of federal
conspiracy.”) (citing McAfee v. 5th Circuit Judges, 884 F.2d 221, 222 (5th Cir. 1989)).
For these reasons, Sencial’s § 1983 claims against Reina, Lopez, and Cruz should be
dismissed pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1915(e) and § 1915A as frivolous and for failure to state a claim
for which relief can be granted.  Even if Sencia l could formulate some factual support to suggest
that there was a conspiracy or joint action between these private persons and a state actor to assure
his arrest and indictment, his claims would be barred under the Heck doctrine discussed later in
this Report.  Nevertheless, because Sencial has no t alleged nor stated a conspiracy claim against
these or any defendants, the more prudent recommendation is dismissal.
B. Claims Against Officers Coll and Galvez
Sencial asserts claims of false arrest, ma licious prosecution, false testimony, and other
vague state law claims of defamation against O fficers Coll and Galvez, in their individual and
official capacities, arising from his arrest and the Officers’ testimony at probable cause and
suppression hearings.  Sencial essentially challenges the alleged false reports and false statements
by these Officers which he alleges unlawfully fabricated the probable cause needed to secure his
Case 2:24-cv-00874-SM-KWR     Document 8     Filed 06/21/24     Page 5 of 11
6

arrest and indictment.  Sencial’s claims are inextricably intertwi ned with his criminal pretrial
detention and are barred by the United States Supreme Court’s doctrine under Heck v. Humphrey,
512 U.S. 477 (1994).
In Heck, the Supreme Court held that a claim that, in effect, attacks the constitutionality of
a conviction or imprisonment is not cognizable under § 1983 and does not accrue until that
conviction or sentence has been “reversed on direct appeal, expunged by executive order, declared
invalid by a state tribunal authorized to make such determination, or called into question by a
federal court’s issuance of a writ of habeas corpus.”  Id. at 486-87.  The Heck doctrine applies
whether a plaintiff seeks mone tary or injunctive relief.  Wilkinson v. Dotson, 544 U.S. 74, 81-82
(2005) (under Heck, “a state prisoner’s section 1983 action is barred (absent prior invalidation) -
no matter the relief sought (damages or equitable relief), no matter the target of the prisoner’s suit
(state conduct leading to conviction or internal prison proceedings) - if success in that action would
necessarily demonstrate the invalidity of confinement or its duration”); Clarke v. Stalder, 154 F.3d
186, 189 (5th Cir. 1998) (citing Edwards v. Balisok, 520 U.S. 641 (1997)).
The Supreme Court applied a modified version of Heck to pretrial detainees in Wallace v.
Kato, 549 U.S. 384, 393-94 (2007).  In Wallace, the Court explained that, if a pretrial detainee
files civil rights claims related to  the validity of a pending crim inal proceeding, the best practice
is for the district court to stay the civil rights action until the pending criminal case is resolved to
determine the impact of the Heck limitations.  Id., 549 U.S. at 393-94; see Mackey v. Dickson, 47
F. 3d 744, 746 (5th Cir. 1995) (finding stay of pret rial § 1983 challenges to be the best practice).
The court in Wallace explained:
If a plaintiff files a false a rrest claim before he has b een convicted (or files any
other claim related to rulings that will li kely be made in a pending or anticipated
criminal trial), it is within the power of the district court, and in accord with
Case 2:24-cv-00874-SM-KWR     Document 8     Filed 06/21/24     Page 6 of 11
7

common practice, to stay the civil action until the criminal case or the likelihood of
a criminal case is ended. If the plaintiff is ultimately convicted, and if the stayed
civil suit would impugn that conviction, Heck will require dismissal; otherwise, the
civil action will proceed, absent some other bar to suit.
Wallace, 549 U.S. at 393-94 (citations omitted).
Sencial’s claims of false arrest and fabricated probable cause testimony and evidence
necessarily call into question the validity of the charges against him and his current incarceration.
See Goldstein v. City of Monroe, 621 F. App’x 274, 278 (5th Cir. 2015) (concluding that claim that
arrest warrant lacked probable cause is in esse nce a collateral attack on the conviction).  A
favorable finding on his claims wo uld necessarily call into questio n the legality of the ongoing
state criminal proceedings and his pretrial confinement.
Likewise, to the extent Sencial may intend to present some overarching claim that the
Officers acted in concert with others to violate his rights and cause his arrest and indictment, that
claim is also necessarily intertwined with his challenge to the constitutionality of his current
confinement.  Any such conspiracy claim asserted against the defendants would be similarly barred
by Heck.  Connors v. Graves, 538 F.3d 373, 378 (5th Cir. 2008).
The same is true to the extent Sencial sues the Officers in their of ficial capacities.  The
Supreme Court has made clear that “a suit against a public servant ‘in his official capacity’ imposes
liability on the entity that he represents . . .”  Brandon v. Holt, 469 U.S. 464, 471-72 (1985); Floyd
v. City of Kenner , 351 F. App’x 890, 893 n.2 (5th Cir. 2009) (“[O]fficial-capacity lawsuits are
typically an altern ative means of pleading an action agains t the governmental entity involved.”)
(quoting Baker v. Putnal, 75 F.3d 190, 195 (5th Cir. 1996)).  Thus, suit against these Kenner City
Police Officers is the same as suit against the City of Kenner.  See Brown v. Kenner Police Dep’t,
No. 17-3445, 2017 WL 6610467, at *4-5 (E.D. La. Dec. 27, 2017).  A plaintiff may bring a § 1983
Case 2:24-cv-00874-SM-KWR     Document 8     Filed 06/21/24     Page 7 of 11
8

claim against a municipality when  “official policy or governmental  custom is responsible for a
deprivation of rights protected by the Constitution.”  Bennet v. Slidell, 728 F.2d 762, 766 (5th Cir.
1984) (citing Monell v. Dept. of Soc. Servs. of City of New York, 436 U.S. 658, 690 (1987)).  This
type of Monell claim necessarily requires an underlying constitutional violation.  Because of this,
“where the underlying violation is barred by Heck, it cannot form the basis for a derivative Monell
claim.”  Williams v. Town of Delhi, No. 14-043, 2015 WL 868746, at *7 (W.D. La. Feb. 27, 2015);
DeLeon v. City of Corpus Christi, 488 F.3d 649 (5th Cir. 2007) (affirming judgment barring claims
against individual officer and municipality pursuant to Heck).
Thus, whether considered brought in an indivi dual or official capacity, Sencial’s claims
against Officers Coll and Galvez for false arrest, malicious prosecution, false testimony, all tied to
his arrest and indictment, fall under the Heck bar.  As a pretrial detainee, under Wallace, Sencial’s
case cannot proceed and will have to be stayed.  If he is or has since been convicted, he will have
to establish that a state or federal court has dete rmined the terms of his confinement to be invalid
or criticized in one of  the ways discussed in Heck for this Court to consider his § 1983 claims
against the Officers.  At this time, he cannot pursue his § 1983 cl aims against Officers Coll or
Galvez, and a stay of the § 1983 claims against Officers Coll and Galvez is recommended.
C. State Law Claims
Broadly construing his complaint, Sencial has asserted state law claims including abuse of
process, defamation, libel, slander, and false re porting against defendants Reina, false reporting
against Lopez and Cruz, and malicious prosecution and defamation by Officers Coll and Galvez.
Pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1367(a), in any civil action of which the district courts have original
jurisdiction, the district courts shall have supplemental jurisd iction over all other claims that are
so related to claims in action within such original jurisdiction that they form part of the same case
Case 2:24-cv-00874-SM-KWR     Document 8     Filed 06/21/24     Page 8 of 11
9

or controversy under Article III of the United States Constitution.  This jurisdiction is “a doctrine
of discretion,” and in so deciding, courts consider “judicial economy, convenience and fairness to
litigants,” as well as whether “the state issues substantially predominate.”  United Mine Workers
of America v. Gibbs , 383 U.S. 715, 726-27 (1966).  Ultimately , “[a] district court has ‘wide
discretion’ in deciding whether it should retain jurisdiction over st ate law claims once all federal
claims have been eliminated.”  Enochs v. Lampasas Cnty., 641 F.3d 155, 161 (5th Cir. 2011) (citing
Guzzino v. Felterman, 191 F.3d 588, 595 (5th Cir. 1999)).  Thus, the Fifth Circuit recognizes that
its “general rule is to dismiss state claims when  the federal claims to wh ich they are pendent are
dismissed.”  Parker & Parsley Petroleum Co. v. Dresser Indus., 972 F.2d 580, 585 (5th Cir. 1992)
(citing Wong v. Stripling, 881 F.2d 200, 204 (5th Cir. 1989)); see Carnegie-Mellon University v.
Cohill, 484 U.S. 343, 351 (1988) (when the federal claims are eliminated at an “early stage” of the
litigation the district court ha s “a powerful reason to choose not  to continue to exercise
jurisdiction”); Gibbs, 383 U.S. at 726 (“Certainly, if the federal claims are dismissed before trial,
even though not insubstantial in a jurisdictional sense, the state claims should be dismissed as
well.”).  The Supreme Court has for nearly half  a century cautioned federal courts to avoid
“[n]eedless decisions of state law[.]”  Gibbs, 383 U.S. at 726.
The Court has recommended that Sencial’s § 1983 claims against Reina, Lopez, and Cruz,
who are private persons not subject to suit under § 1983, be dismissed as frivolous and for failure
to state a claim for which relief can be granted.  Thus, there is no basis to maintain § 1983 claims
against them and nothing left to which the state law claims against them are pendent.  Considering
the statutory provisions of § 1367( c) and balancing the relevant factors of judicial economy,
convenience, fairness, and comity and considering the early stage of this proceeding, this court

should decline to exercise jurisdiction over any state law constitutional or tort claims against
defendants Reina, Lopez, and Cruz, and dismiss those claims without prejudice.
As for Sencial’s state law claims against Officers Coll and Galvez, the Court will, at least
for now, maintain jurisdiction over Sencial’s stayed § 1983 claims arising from his arrest and their
reports and testimony leading to his indictment, which are subject to the Heck doctrine.  Louisiana
courts also have followed the Heck doctrine to bar state law claims  if the claims challenge the
validity of the underlying criminal conviction.  See Anderson v. City of Minden, No. 07-0905, 2009
WL 1374122, at *5-6 (W.D. La. May 15, 2009) (citing Roberson v. Town of Pollock, 915 So. 2d
426, 433-34 (La. App. 3rd Cir. 11/9/05) (gathering Louisiana appellate decisions applying the Heck
doctrine to state law civil actions  and precluding such actions if it would invalidate a criminal
conviction)).  Thus, any state law claims against Officers Coll and Galvez challenging the validity
of Sencial’s arrest and detention may also be barred by Heck under state law.  For this reason, and
considering the balancing factors addressed above,  it would be prudent for the Court to simply
maintain jurisdiction over Sencial’s state law claims related to the on-going § 1983 claims asserted
against Officers Coll and Galvez.  Those claims also should be stayed along with the § 1983 claims
pending the Court’s determination on the ultimate applicability of Heck once the state criminal
charges against Sencial are resolved.
