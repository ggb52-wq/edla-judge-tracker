A Rule 12(b)(6) motion challenges the sufficiency of a plaintiff’s allegations.
A complaint, or any part of it, may be dismissed for failure to state a claim upon
which relief may be granted if plaintiff fails to set forth factual allegations that would

Cuvillier v. Taylor, 503 F.3d 397, 401 (5th Cir. 2007).
To survive, a complaint must contain sufficient facts, accepted as true, to state
a claim for relief that is plausible on its face.  Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 678
(2009).  “A claim has facial plausibility when the plaintiff pleads the factual content
that allows the court to draw the reasonable inference that the defendant is liable for
the misconduct alleged .”  Id.  Determining whether a complaint states a plausible
claim for relief  is a conte xt-specific task requir ing the application of  judicial
experience and common sense.  Id. at 679.
Although detailed factual allegations are not required, a complaint must offer
more than labels, legal conclusions, or formulaic recitations of the elements of a
cause of action.  Iqbal, 556 U.S. at 678.  “The complaint is construed in the light
most favorable to plaintiff, accepting as true all well-pleaded factual allegations and
drawing all reasonable inferences in plaintiff’s favor.”  Lovick v. Ritemoney Ltd., 378
F.3d 433, 437 (5th Cir. 2004) (citation omitted).  However, the court will not accept
“conclusory allegations, unwarranted factual inferences, or legal conclusions” as
true.  Ferrer v. Chevron Corp., 484 F.3d 776, 780 (5th Cir. 2007) (quoting Plotkin v.
IP Axess Inc. , 407 F.3d 690, 696 (5 th Cir. 2005)).   If factual allegations are
insufficient to raise a right to relief above the speculative level, the claim should be
dismissed.  Twombly, 550 U.S. at 555.

While a pro se litigant’s pleadings are held to “less stringent standards than

A. Motions to Dismiss (Sears Complaint) —- GM Financial, GCAR, Carly
Davis, Darren Guillot, and Christopher Wallace

Sears asserts three section 1983 claims, one section 1985 claim, and numerous
state law claims  against GM Financial, GCAR, Davis, Guillot, and Wallace .  She
adds a section 1983 claim for unlawful search and seizure against Wallace.
Section 1983 provides a remedy against “every person who , under color of”
state law, deprives another of any right secured by the Constitution and laws of the
United States.  42 U.S.C. § 1983; Monell v. Dep’t of Social Services, 436 U.S. 658
(1978).  The requirement that the alleged tortfeasor act “under the color of” state law
generally excludes private actors from the statute’s purview.  Cornish v. Correctional
Services Corp., 402 F.3d 545, 549 (5th Cir. 2005).  A private actor who is a “willful
participant in joint activity with the State or its agents” may be liable.  Carpenter v.
Webre, N. 17-cv-808, 2018 WL 1453201, at *23 (E.D. La. March 23, 2018) (citing
Cinel v. Connick , 15 F.3d 1338, 1343 (5 th Cir. 1994)).  In that circumstance , a

plaintiff must allege (1) “an agreement between the private and public defendants to
commit an illegal act” and (2) “an actual deprivation of constitutional rights”.  Cinel,
15 F.3d at 1343 (internal citations omitted).
A section 1985(3) claim requires a conspiracy between at least two people to
deprive another person of the equal protection of the law.  Hilliard v. Ferguson, 30
F.3d 649, 652–53 (5th Cir. 1994).  A section 1985(3) plaintiff must allege the intent
to discriminate based on race or other protected class.  Galloway v. State of La., 817
F.2d 1154, 1159 (5th Cir. 1987).
1. GM Financial
Sears alleges GM Financial authorized the repossession “despite notice that
the vehicle belonged to a deceased estate and succession proceedings were
pending.”29  GM Financial is a private actor, thus, only liable under section 1983 if
there is an agreement with a state actor to commit an illegal act.  See Cinel, 15 F.3d
at 1343.  Sears makes no allegation of an agreement between GM Financial and the
City of Slidell or its police officers reflecting anything related to the repossession,
much less an agreement reflecting the intent to violate Sears’ civil rights.  Sears ’
section 1983 claims against GM Financial fail.

1. Slidell
Sears asserts the City of Slidell is liable for maintaining “policies, customs,
practices, and ratification that caused the constitutional violations” when the vehicle

Municipalities are “persons” within the meaning of section 1983.  In Monell,
the Supreme Court held that a government entity is only liable under section 1983
when that entity causes the constitutional violation.  436 U.S. at 694.  To succeed, a
plaintiff must demonstrate (1) an official policy or custom that is facially
unconstitutional or adopted with deliberate indifference, (2) the policy was the
“moving force” behind the constitutional deprivation, and (3) a direct causal link
between the policy and the violation.  City of Canton, Ohio v. Harris, 489 U.S. 378,
389 (1989) (quoting Monell, 436 U.S. at 694).  “An official policy is evinced by a
widespread pattern or practice that is so common and well-settled as to constitute a
custom that fairly represents municipal policy or, occasionally, when an official with

A. Excessive Force and Unlawful Seizure
To prevail on an excessive force claim under section 1983, “a plaintiff must
show that he was seized and that he ‘suffered (1) an injury that (2) resulted directly
and only from the use of force that was excessive to the need and that (3) the force
used was objectively unreasonable.’”  Ballard v. Burton, 444 F.3d 391, 402 (5th Cir.
2006) (quoting Flores v. City of Palacios, 381 F.3d 391, 396 (5th Cir. 2004)).

According to Sears’ complaint, Wallace repossessed the vehicle.  When he
began, Sears refused to exit and allegedly sustained injuries.  She then called police

Wallace is a private actor.  Sears makes no allegation that her injuries resulted

B. Denial of Medical Care
“After the initial incidents of a seizure have concluded and an individual is
being detained by police officials but has yet to be booked, an arrestee ’s right to
medical attention, like that of a pretrial detainee, derives from the Fourteenth
Amendment.”  Nerren v. Livingston Police Dep’t, 86 F.3d 469, 473 (5th Cir.1996).
“The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment guarantees that a person
detained by the police is entitled to medical care.”  Carter v. Reach, 399 Fed. App’x.
941, 942 (5th Cir. 2010) (citation omitted).  Police officers violate that right if they
are deliberately indifferent to a serious illness or injury to a plaintiff.  Jacobs v. West
Feliciana Sheriff’ s Dept., 228 F.3d 388, 395 (5th Cir. 2000).  Deliberate indifference
requires a showing the defendant subjectively knew of a substantial and significant
Case 2:25-cv-02550-WJC-KWR     Document 41     Filed 10/15/26     Page 15 of 18

16

risk and effectively disregard ed that risk.  Id.  The plaintiff must also demonstrate
“substantial harm” resulted from that conduct.  Baldwin v. Dorsey, 964 F.3d 320,
326 (5th Cir. 2020).
“Deliberate indifference is an extremely high standard to meet and cannot be
inferred merely from a negligent or even a grossly negligent response to a substantial
risk of serious harm  … When multiple officials are named as defendants, each
officer’s knowledge and response is evaluated individually, to the extent possible. ”
Stapleton v. Lozano, 125 F.4th 743, 749 (5th Cir. 2025) (citations omitted).
Sears’ complaint is inadequate to determine if she was a “pretrial detainee” ,
or whether she was ever held in detention or placed under arrest.  See Bell v. Wolfish,
441 U.S. 520, 523 (1979) (defining pretrial detainees as “ persons who have been
charged with a crime but who have not yet been tried on the charge ”).  Due to the
vague complaint, the extent of Sears’ injuries, when and how they occurred, and
what exactly each individual Slidell officer did or did not do relating to her medical
needs cannot be determined.  Without sufficient factual allegations, Sears’ section
1983 claim for denial of medical care against the Slidell officers fails.
C. Failure to Intervene
An injury rising to the level of a constitutional violation  is a necessary
predicate to a section 1983 claim for failing to intervene.  See Espinal v. City of
Houston, 96 F.4th 741, 745 n.4 (5th Cir. 2024) (“[B]ystander liability would be

predicated on an underlying constitutional violation. Because we conclude [plaintiff]
has failed to allege any violation of his constitutional rights by anyone, his bystander
claims against [the police officers] necessarily fail.”) (citation omitted).  Sears fails
to plausibly allege a claim for  excessive force, unlawful seizure, or deliberate
indifference to medical care, thus her failure to intervene claim cannot proceed.
Sears has not adequately pled any violation of her constitutional rights.  Thus,
she fails to defeat the officers’ assertion of qualified immunity, and her section 1983
claims against them cannot proceed.
