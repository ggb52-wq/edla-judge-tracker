III. DISCUSSION
A. Sovereign Immunity
The United States and its agencies generally enjoy immunity from suit.
In re FEMA Trailer Formaldehyde Prods. Liab. Litig. , 668 F.3d 281, 287
(5th Cir. 2012) (noting that the Gove rnment’s consent to be sued “is a
prerequisite to federal jurisdiction”).  Absent a waiver of this immunity or

6

consent to be sued, any suit brought against the United States or any federal
agency must be dismissed for lack of subject matter jurisdiction.  Wagstaff
v. U.S. Dep’t of Educ., 509 F.3d 661, 664 (5th Cir. 2007).  The plaintiff bears
the burden of showing a waiver of sovereign immunity.  Freeman v. United
States, 556 F.3d 326, 334 (5th Cir. 2009).
The FTCA is a limited waiver of sovereign immunity and subjects the
United States to tort liability if a private person would be liable for the same
act under state law.  Life Partners Inc. v. United States , 650 F.3d 1026,
1030–31 (5th Cir. 2011).  The FTCA’s limited waiver of sovereign immunity
includes exceptions that, if applicable , bar suit against the government.
Truman v. United States, 26 F.3d 592, 594 (5th Cir. 1994).  These exceptions
are strictly construed in favor of the government.  Id.  There are two
exceptions to the FTCA’s limited waiver of sovereign immunity at issue here:
the discretionary function exception and the misrepresentation exception.
1. Discretionary Function Exception
The discretionary function exception provides that the government is
not liable for “any claim based upon an act or omission of an employee of the
Government, exercising due care, in the execution of a statute or
regulation . . . or based upon the exerci se or performance or the failure to
exercise or perform a discretionary functi on or duty on the part of a federal

a g e n c y . ”   2 8  U . S . C .  §  2 6 8 0 ( a ) .   T he exception covers only acts that are
discretionary in nature and involve an element of judgment or choice.
Berkovitz v. United States, 486 U.S. 531, 536 (1988).
The Fifth Circuit uses a two-part test to determine whether a
government agency’s actions fall within the discretionary function exception.
Campos v. United States, 888 F.3d 724, 731 (5th Ci r. 2018).  First, the
relevant agency’s conduct must be a matter of choice.  Id.  And second, the
choice or judgment “must be of the ki nd that the discretionary function
exception was designed to shield.”  Id. (quoting Tsolmon v. United States ,
841 F.3d 378, 382 (5th Cir. 2016)).  As the party asserting jurisdiction, the
plaintiff bears the burden of establishing that the test is not satisfied.  Id.
As to the first prong, a plaintiff must identify a federal “statute,
regulation, or policy” that specifically prescribes a course of action for the
agency to follow to establish an absence of discretion.  Freeman, 556 F.3d
at 337.  If the agency is not bound to act “in a particular manner” by such a
statute, regulation, or policy, then the conduct is discretionary.  Id.

2. Misrepresentation Exception
The misrepresentation exception to  the FTCA’s limited waiver of
sovereign immunity bars plaintiff’s failure to warn claims against the
government.  The FTCA’s waiver of sovereign immunity does not apply to
any “claim arising out of . . . misrepresentation.”  28 U.S.C. § 2680(h).
In its earlier order, the Court fo und that the misrepresentation
exception barred plaintiff’s failure to warn claims. 38  Plaintiff concedes that
her amended complaint does not affect this analysis and only points out that
her other claims are not barred by the misrepresentation exception.39  As the
Court held before, plaintiff’s failure to warn claim falls squarely within the
misrepresentation exception.40  Thus, the Court again dismisses the failure
to warn claim for lack of subject matter jurisdiction.
