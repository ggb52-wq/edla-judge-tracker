LEGAL STANDARD
Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(5) provides for dismissal of an
action for “insufficient service of process.” 1 A Rule 12(b)(5) challenge contests
the manner in which process was served. 2 “[O]nce the validity of service of
process has been contested, the plaintiff bears the burden of establishing its
validity.”3 That said, “if the plaintiff meets the burden of showing proper
service,” such as a signed return of service,  “the burden shifts to the defendant
to present strong and convincing proof of insufficient service.”4 In ruling on a
Rule 12(b)(5) motion, district courts enjoy broad discretion.5

Pursuant to the Louisiana Long Arm Statute,  actual delivery is a
requirement for commercial courier service. In addition, a “commercial courier”
is defined by the statute as an entity “having as its primary purpose the
delivery of letters and parcels of any type, and which . . . [a]cquires a signed
receipt from the addressee, or the addressee’s agent, of the letter or parcel upon
completion of delivery.”10 Accordingly, courts have held that a signature is “a
requirement for commercial courier service under the long-arm statute. ”11
Plaintiff does not have a signature from Defendant proving delivery and has
not presented any other evidence that service was actually delivered to
Defendant. Accordingly, Plaintiff has failed to carry its burden to show proper
service of process. “Personal jurisdiction over a nonresident obtained by use of

Because Plaintiff’s attempts at service were insufficient, its claim
against Defendant is subject to dismissal.  Rule 4(m) of the Federal Rules
provides as follows:13
If a defendant is not served within 90 days after the
complaint is filed, the court . . . must dismiss the
action without prejudice against that defendant or
order that service be made within a specified time. But
if the plaintiff shows good cause for the failure, the
court must extend the time for service for an

“To establish good cause, a litigant must demonstrate at least as much as
would be required to show excusable neglect, as to which simple inadvertence
or mistake of counsel or ignorance of the rules usually does not suffice.”15 Even
in the absence of good cause, however, the Court has discretion to extend the
time for service.16
Plaintiff has not set forth any basis for this Court to find good cause for
its failure to properly serve Defendant. That said,  where, as here, “there is a
‘reasonable prospect that plaintiff ultimately will be able to serve defendant
