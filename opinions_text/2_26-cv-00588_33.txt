II. THE MOTION TO COMPEL
In this motion, Defendants argue that Plaintiff should supplement its initial disclosures to
provide a computation of damages and produce all documents supporting its computation  as
required by Rule 26(a)(1)(A)(iii) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure.  ECF No. 31, at 1.  They
object to Plaintiff repeating vague damages allegations from the complaint in place of a
computation of damages.  ECF No. 31- 1, at 1.  Defendants explain that Plaintiff’s “claims could
be worth four figures or seven, and Defendants would be none the wiser.”  Id.  at 3.  Defendants
contend that they are prejudiced by Plaintiff’s insufficient disclosures because they cannot target
discovery, pursue prompt third-party discovery, or make reasoned decisions on expert discovery.
Id. at 4–5.
Plaintiff argues that this motion should be denied as moot because it has supplemented its
initial disclosures and produced the supporting documents.  ECF No. 32, at 1.  Plaintiff notes that
it promised its supplement by the end of the week but, Defendants filed their motion to compel
that Friday morning, while Plaintiff, as promised, supplemented the disclosure by the end of that
day.  Id.  While some categories of damages are listed as “not yet quantified/expert opinion
needed,” Plaintiff contends that it has fulfilled its Rule 26 obligations by disclosing what is
“reasonably available.”  Id. at 2–3.  It explains that its complicated damages calculations must be
computed by experts.  Id. at 4.  As one example, it explains that  “economic loss resulting from
interruption of a producing gas well requires more than multiplying a period of downtime by a
historic daily-production figure.”  Id.

III. APPLICABLE LAW AND ANALYSIS
Damages discovery begins with initial disclosures, which may be supplemented as
necessary.1  Rule 37(a)(3)(A) and (B) authorize a party to move for an order compelling disclosure
when the responding party fails to provide full and complete disclosures.
Rule 26(a)(1)(A)(iii) mandates the disclosure of
a computation of each category of damages claimed by the disclosing party—who
must also make available for inspection and copying as under Rule 34 the
documents or other evidentiary material, unless privileged or protected from
disclosure, on which each computation is based, including materials bearing on the
nature and extent of injuries suffered[.]
The purpose of this provision, which requires a party to disclose damages information without
awaiting a discovery request, is to “accelerate the exchange of basic information about the case
and to eliminate the paper work involved in requesting such infor mation.”2 “A party claiming
damages has the obligation, when it makes its initial disclosures, to disclose to the other parties
the best information then available to it concerning that claim, however limited and potentially
changing it may be.”3
Rule 26(a)(1)(A)(iii) “does not elaborate on the level of specificity required in the initial
damages disclosure,” 4 but “[a] party must make its initial disclosures based on the information
then reasonably available to it.”5  “‘Computation’ at a minimum requires a disclosure of a specific
dollar figure. Simply reciting a dollar figure, however, is not enough.” 6  Likewise, identifying

Parties are then required to “supplement or correct [their] disclosure or response . . . in a
timely manner if the party learns that in some material respect the disclosure or response is
incomplete or incorrect, and if the additional or corrective informa tion has not otherwise been
made known to the other parties during the discovery process or in writing.” 8  Even when a party
will rely on an expert to calculate damages, it cannot refuse to disclose its computation of damages
before expert disclosures are due.9
Plaintiff supplemented its initial disclosures on the same day that Defendant filed this
motion.  Plaintiff’s supplement, however, still does not comply with its obligations under Rule 26.
At a minimum, a computation of damages requires “a disclosure of a specific dollar figure.”10
While Plaintiff identified approximate values for three categories of damages, at least six
categories remain completely unquantified.  Plaintiff must supplement its initial disclosures with
currently estimated specific dollar amounts based on currently available information for its
categories of damages.  Of course, Plaintiff can later supplement its initial disclosures as its
investigation and expert analysis continues.
