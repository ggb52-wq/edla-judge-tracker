considers “all of the evidence in the record but refrain[s] from making credibility
determinations or weighing the evidence.”58 All reasonable inferences are drawn in

III. Analysis
A. The claims against defendants Bullen, Johnson and Erwin, sued
in their individual capacities.
 Individual defendants Bullen, Johnson, and Erwin assert they are protected
from suit by the doctrine of qualified immunity  as public officials .71 Government
officials performing discretionary functions generally are shielded from civil liability
for damages so long as their conduct did not violate clearly established statutory or
constitutional rights of which a reasonable person would have known.72 The qualified
immunity analysis thus has two components: “(1) whether a plaintiff alleges or shows
the violation of a federal constitutional or statutory right; and (2) whether the right
in question was clearly established at the time of the alleged violat ion.”73 Once a
defendant raises qualified immunity as  a defense, “the plaintiff bears a heightened
burden to negate the defense.”74
 When demonstrating that a right is “clearly established,” the plaintiff must
show that the defendant officials had “fair warning” under past precedent.75 In doing
so, “she must point to ‘authority at a sufficiently high level of specificity to put a
reasonable official on notice that his conduct is definitely unlawful.”76 Here, Plaintiff

3. Count V: Denial of adequate medical care in violation of
the Fourteenth Amendment
 Plaintiff alleges that Johnson and Erwin were aware of Thomas’s serious
physical distress through their interactions with him and, as evidenced by  their
response and/or nonresponse, intended for  harm to occur to him  in violation of his
Fourteenth Amendment right to due process .86 The Fourteenth Amendment
guarantees pretrial detainees the “right not to have their serious medical needs met
with deliberate indifference on the part of the confining officials.” 87 Thus, a  state
official violates a pretrial detainee’s due process right to adequate medical care if the
official “acts with deliberate indifference to a substantial risk of serious medical harm
and resulting injuries.”88 A serious medical need is “one for which treatment has been
recommended or for which the need is so apparent that even laymen would recognize
that care is required.”89
 A d eliberate-indifference claim requires that the officer  (1) was objectively
aware of facts from which the officer could infer that a substantial risk of harm exists,
and (2) had actual, subjective knowledge of the risk of harm.90 “Mere negligence or a
failure to act reasonably is not enough.”91 The plaintiff “must show that the officials
