III. LAW AND ANALYSIS
A. Legal Standard
A scheduling order “may be modified only for good cause and with the judge’s consent.”49
The Amended Scheduling Order likewise permits deadlines to be extended only “upon timely
motion filed in compliance with Local Rules and upon a showing of good cause.” 50 The “good
cause standard requires the party seeking relief to show that the deadlines cannot reasonably be
met despite the diligence of the party needing the extension.”51 The Court considers four factors:
“(1) the explanation of the failure to timely comply with the scheduling order; (2) the importance
of the modification; (3) potential prejudice in allowing the modification; and (4) the availability of
a continuance to cure such prejudice.”52 The same standard governs a request to file a dispositive
motion after the deadline has passed.53 Resetting trial and pretrial dates does not revive deadlines

B. Analysis
The Court will analyze the competing interests at stake. A scheduling order is not a mere
formality. Parties organize litigation around its deadlines, and its value depends on the certainty
that those deadlines will be enforced, and enforced even-handedly. District courts therefore have
“broad discretion to preserve the integrity and purpose of the pretrial order”55 and “must have the
power to control their dockets by holding litigants to a schedule.” 56 At the same time, summary
judgment is “not . . . a disfavored procedural shortcut, but rather . . . an integral part of the Federal
Rules as a whole, which are designed ‘to secure the just, speedy and inexpensive determination of
every action,’”57 and Rule 16 itself contemplates that the Court may act before trial to “formulat[e]
and simplify[] the issues” and “eliminat[e] frivolous claims or defenses.” 58 The good- cause
standard reconciles these interests. A dispositive -motion deadline may yield in a rare case: for
example, where the movant obtains information it could not have obtained before the deadline
despite its diligence; where the motion presents a discrete legal question that can be decided
without disrupting the trial date or prejudicing the opposing party; or where a party that timely
sought summary judgment asks the Court to address a limited matter closely related to its timely

1. The Explanation for the Delay
This factor does not turn on whether J.H.’s  deposition produced testimony the School
Board considers helpful, but on whether the School Board could not reasonably have met the
deadline despite its diligence. The School Board contends that it could not know whether to seek
summary judgment until it deposed J.H. and reviewed his testimony. J.H. is a central figure in this
case, and his account of the events of May 13, 2021 may bear on some of the issues at trial. But
the School Board’s explanation does not account for its decision not to move for summary
judgment on any claim before the deadline.
First, a party moving for summary judgment on a claim on which the nonmovant will bear
the burden of proof at trial need not produce evidence negating that claim; it may satisfy its initial
burden by pointing out that the record lacks sufficient evidence of an essential element.60 The
School Board therefore did not need J.H.’s  testimony to test whether Plaintiffs could prove their
claims against it. Lopinto moved for summary judgment in August 2023 without J.H.’s deposition
and obtained dismissal of all but two of Plaintiffs’ claims against him.61
Second, many of Plaintiffs’ claims against the School Board turn on evidence that does not
depend on J.H.’s recollection. Plaintiffs allege, for example, that the School Board knew of J.H.’s
disabilities and that school administrators chose to call 911 rather than use de-escalation

2. The Importance of the Relief Sought
The School Board’s motion is clearly an important one. All of Plaintiffs’ claims against
the School Board remain pending, and if the School Board is correct that J.H.’s testimony reveals
that Plaintiffs cannot prove those claims, resolving them before trial would spare the parties and
the Court the burden of trying Plaintiffs’ case against the School Board. The Court takes that
interest very seriously, but the importance of a dispositive motion does not, by itself, establish
good cause to file it out of time; otherwise, every untimely dispositive motion would satisfy this
factor. As the Fifth Circuit has explained, the importance of the relief sought “cannot singularly
override the enforcement of local rules and scheduling orders.”86 This factor weighs in favor of
the School Board, but it does not outweigh the other factors.

3. Prejudice to Plaintiffs
Allowing the School Board’s motion would prejudice Plaintiffs in two respects. First, in
June 2025, over the strong opposition of both the School Board and Lopinto, the Court denied
Plaintiffs’ request to reopen discovery, a request limited to obtaining more complete responses to
written discovery already served and obtaining expert opinions. Plaintiffs’ request rested
principally on a change in Plaintiffs’ counsel and new counsel’s desire to do what prior counsel
had not done. The Court rejected Plaintiffs’ request, explaining that “[i]n every case, a new set of
lawyers can come in and find things they might have done differently, but to reopen discovery in
this case would unfairly prejudice the defendants.”87 The Court found that Plaintiffs’ newly
enrolled counsel would have sufficient time “to prepare for trial based on the current record.” 88
The School Board urged that result, arguing that Plaintiffs’ new counsel sought to redo the work
of prior counsel and that the case should proceed to trial without further delay.89 The School
Board’s request rests on a similar wish to do now what it chose not to do before the deadline.
Permitting the School Board, which never sought summary judgment, to belatedly test every claim
against it based in part on testimony obtained after that ruling, while Plaintiffs remain bound by
the lapsed discovery deadline, would not treat the parties even-handedly. J.H.’s  deposition
occurred only because Plaintiffs, who initially sought to preclude J.H. from testifying, later advised
that they would call him at trial. The Court allowed the deposition so that Defendants’ trial
preparation would not be prejudiced if Plaintiffs were to call J.H., not to give the School Board a
complete do-over at summary judgment, particularly after the Court, at Defendants’ urging, denied
Plaintiffs’ request to reopen discovery.

5. Balancing the Factors
Considering the factors together, the Court finds that the School Board has not shown good
cause to modify the Amended Scheduling Order. This is not the rare case in which an expired
dispositive-motion deadline should yield. The School Board’s motion does not rest on information
it could not have obtained before the deadline, does not present a discrete legal question, and does
not seek to complete or supplement a timely motion. Instead, a party that never sought summary
judgment asks, a month before trial, to test for the first time every claim against it, based largely
on a deposition the Court allowed solely for trial preparation. 99 The explanation factor weighs
heavily against modification, and the importance of the relief does not overcome the prejudice to
Plaintiffs or the cost of a continuance.

School Board from presenting that testimony at trial or using it on cross-examination. If the School

as a matter of law once Plaintiffs have been “fully heard” at trial, on a complete record.101
Enforcing the dispositive-motion deadline is the course the Court finds most consistent with both
