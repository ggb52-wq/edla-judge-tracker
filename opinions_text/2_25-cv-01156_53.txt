I.  Background
A) Factual Background
This case arises from a lane change motor vehicle accident that occurred on September 12,
2024, on US Highway 90 at the Tchoupitoulas Street exit in New Orleans, Louisiana. Plaintiff
Victoria Hamilton (“Hamilton”) was driving a 1998 Toyota Camry in the right lane of Highway
90B. At the same time, Defendant commercial driver, Landon Ronnie Foster (“Foster”), was
operating a commercial truck/big rig for his employer, MasTec North America, Inc. (“MasTec”),
in the middle lane of Highway 90B.

Plaintiff alleges negligence against both Foster and MasTec. First, Hamilton alleges that

II. Standard of Review
A) Scope of Discovery
Rule 26 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedur e provides that unless otherwise limited by
the court, “[p]arties may obtain discovery regarding any nonprivileged matter that is relevant to
any party’s claim or defense and proportional to the needs of the case,” considering factors such
as the parties’ access to the information and the importance of the discovery in resolving the issues.
FED. R. CIV. P. 26(b)(1).
However, the court must limit discovery if it determines that the discovery is duplicative .
FED. R. CIV. P. 26(b)(2)(C). Also, it may be limited if the discovery can be obtained from some
other source that is more convenient, or if the proposed discovery is outside the scope of Rule
26(b)(1). Id.
B) Privileged Information
Rule 26 protects against the discovery of privileged information. See FED. R. CIV. P.
26(b)(1). A party who invokes a privilege in response to a discovery request has the burden of
proving the privilege’s applicability. Meador v. Starr Indem. & Liab. Co., No. 19-2378, 2020 WL
8256370, at *3 (E.D. La. Sept. 1, 2020) (citing Hodges, Grant & Kaufmann v. United States, 768
F.2d 719, 721 (5th Cir. 1985)).

i) Work-Product Doctrine
The work-product doctrine provides that “a party may not discover documents and tangible
things that are prepared in anticipation of litigation or for trial by or for another party or its
representative (including the other party’s attorney, consultant . . . or agent).” FED. R. CIV. P.
26(b)(3)(A). For documents or things to be considered work product, litigation is not required to
be imminent “as long as the primary motivating purpose behind the creation of the document was
to aid in possible future litigation.” U.S. v. Davies, 636 F.2d 1028, 1040 (5th Cir. 1981), cert.
denied, 454 U.S. 862 (1981).
The factors considered to determine the primary purpose for creating a document are “the
retention of counsel and his involvement in the generation of the document .” Canter v. Cleco
Corp., No. 08-0343, 2009 WL 902435, at *3 (E.D. La. Mar. 30, 2009) (internal citations omitted).
Also, the court should consider “whether it was a routine practice to prepare that type of document
or whether the document was instead prepared in response to a particular circumstance.” Id.
Materials prepared in the ordinary course of business are not considered to be prepared in
anticipation of litigation. Therefore, such materials are excluded from the work-product doctrine.
Meador, 2020 WL 8256370, at *4 (citing United States v. El Paso Co., 682 F.2d 530, 542 (5th Cir.
1982); additional citation omitted).
The involvement of an attorney is not dispositive , but it “ is a highly relevant factor . . .
making materials more likely to have been prepared in anticipation of litigation.” Fieldwood
Energy, L.L.C. v. Diamond Servs. Corp., No. 14-650, 2015 WL 1415501, at *2 (E.D. La. Mar. 27,
2015) (quoting Wikel v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc., 197 F.R.D. 493, 495 (N.D. Okla. 2000)).

ii) Attorney-Client Privilege
In a federal civil action invoking diversity jurisdiction, state law—in this case Louisiana—
provides the applicable law of attorney- client privilege. See  FED. R. EVID. 501; LA. CODE EVID.
ANN. art. 506.
Under Louisiana law, the party asserting a claim of attorney-client privilege must establish:
(1) the holder of the privilege is or sought to become a client; (2) the communication was made to
an attorney or his subordinate in a professional capacity; (3) the communication was made outside
the presence of strangers; (4) the communication was made to obtain a legal opinion or services;
and (5) the privilege has not been waived. Meador, 2020 WL 8256370, at *3 (citing Cacamo v.
Liberty Mut. Fire Ins. Co., 798 So.2d 1210, 1216 (La. App. 4th Cir. 2001)).

III. Analysis
Plaintiff seeks to compel the Defendants to produce MasTec’s incident report and Foster’s
termination records on the grounds that they are discoverable because they contain relevant and
nonprivileged information. Defendants object to Plaintiff’s motion, arguing that the incident report
is protected by the work- product doctrine and the attorney -client privilege, and that Foster’s
termination records are irrelevant because the reason for his termination is unrelated to the subject
matter of this suit, and that Plaintiff has no need for the termination records because Defendants
have provided the reason for Foster’s termination via Foster’s deposition testimony.
A) MasTec’s Incident Report
i) Work-Product Doctrine

Plaintiff contends that MasTec’s incident report is relevant and therefore discoverable
because MasTec’s incident report likely contains evidence of the cause of the accident, which
Plaintiff’s contention bears directly on the question of Defendants’ negligence.
Defendants object by asserting that the incident report is protected by the work product
doctrine. Defendants conceded at oral argument that incident reports are routine but contend that
this incident report was only created after the claim was classified as a “CAT” matter, when
litigation was considered probable and corporate counsel was engaged to direct the review.
In response, Plaintiff argues that the incident report is not work product because it is created
in the ordinary course of MasTec’s business, as evidenced by its training, policy, and procedure
materials. Plaintiff points to a training video, which MasTec produced in response to discovery
requests, which instructs MasTec employees to investigate all incidents, including “near misses.”
The training video further provides that documentation and investigations are “completed for
reporting purposes” and are “used to notify management of the facts and circumstances
surrounding the incident.”
The Court has reviewed the MasTec incident report in camera. First, the instructions listed
on the “Motor Vehicle Incident Report” form provide that the report is to be completed by the
employee, who is then required to report the incident to their supervisor “as soon as possible, but
at least before the end of the employee’s scheduled work day or shift.” Then, the supervisor must
“notify ESIS and Safety Department.” The incident report documenting this accident does not
identify the employee that completed the form. Thus, nowhere does the form indicate that the
report is created at the instruction of counsel or by or for counsel.
Relevant here, the Court previously held in Canter v. Cleco Corp., No. 08-0343, 2009 WL
902435, at *2, 3 (E.D. La. Mar. 30, 2009), that a party’s bare assertion that an incident report was
Case 2:25-cv-01156-JTM-KWR     Document 53     Filed 10/07/26     Page 8 of 13
9

created “under orders of the legal department” was insufficient, without more, to prove that the
incident report was created in anticipation of litigation. There, an individual alleged negligence
against a city and an electrical corporation because he was electrocuted by an energized wire. Id.
at *1. The individual requested the electrical corporation’s incident report, but the corporation
refused to produce the report by asserting that it was privileged. Id.
However, the corporation was unable to produce any direct evidence that the report was
privileged, and one of the employees who completed an incident report testified that it “was
standard operating procedure (SOP) to get out a report after he responds to a call regarding an
outage or a wire down.” Id. at *2, 3. Another employee testified that he did the report “to his
supervisor”—not legal counsel. Id. The court reasoned that because it did not appear that either of
the reporting employees “were aware that their handwritten reports were prepared at the request
of [the corporation’s] legal department,” there was no evidence that the report was created by or
for legal counsel, let alone with the primary motivating purpose of aiding in future litigation. Id.
at *3, 4. The incident report was therefore discoverable. Id. at *4.
Defendants offer only a bare assertion that the incident report was created once “corporate
counsel was engaged to direct the review [of the accident] for the purpose of providing legal advice
and preparing for the anticipated claim.” R. Doc. 49 at 10. Like those in Canter, the Defendants
offer no evidence of counsel’s involvement in the creation of the incident report. Defendants
provide no record of communication in which legal counsel directed anyone at MasTec to create
the incident report. Further, Defendants do not even provide the identity of the legal counsel who
allegedly gave the instruction.
Further, the incident form’s instructions provide that all incidents must be reported, not just
those which may justify anticipating litigation. The categories of information sought by the report

10

form are general, including location of the accident, contact information, general facts about the
accident itself, and company vehicle details. Most general details about the incident are
documented by checking boxes on the form. Defendants do not produce any evidence that the
designation of this accident on the report form as “Extreme/CAT Claim” caused it to change its
conduct from the procedure followed for reporting all motor vehicle incidents. Further,
Defendants’ routine self-reporting of the severity of the incident and whether it was preventable
are not protected by the work-product doctrine when there is no direct evidence that distinguishes
the “CAT” claim from all else.
This conclusion is further substantiated by MasTec’s training video, which provides that
documentation of incidents is completed for “reporting purposes.” This supports Plaintiff’s
argument that the incident report was created in the ordinary course of business, not in anticipation
of litigation.
Finally, the incident report form contains a flag which reads “Confidential Report of
Incident Privileged, Confidential and/or Proprietary (Attorney -Client Communication and
Attorney Work-Product).” This flag is not evidence of counsel’s involvement in the  creation of
the report and therefore does not confer privileged status.
Here, Defendants offer no direct evidence to justify their assertion of protection under the
work-product doctrine. This, along with Plaintiff’s offer that the training video is direct evidence
that MasTec produces incident reports in the ordinary course of its business, which is supported
by the incident report reviewed in camera by the Court, Defendants have not satisfied their burden
of proof to be entitled to protection under the work- product doctrine. Defendants therefore must
produce the incident report.

ii) Attorney-Client Privilege
Both the Plaintiff and the Defendants primarily give their attention to arguing the issue of
whether the work-product doctrine applies to MasTec’s incident report. In contrast, the parties do
not argue  at length as to whether the MasTec incident report is entitled to protection by the
attorney-client privilege.
Defendants offer no direct evidence that the incident report itself is a confidential
communication made for the purpose of obtaining legal services. The incident report is not directed
to counsel, does not contain legal counsel’s name, and Defendants do not even offer an email
communication in which the incident report was transmitted to counsel. Further, it appears that the
MasTec incident report was prepared for purposes other than for obtaining legal advice, such as to
inform management of incidents and to ensure proper safety protocols.
And as previously mentioned, the statement at the bottom of the incident report designating
privilege does not serve as direct evidence that the incident repot is protected by the attorney-client
privilege.
Thus, Defendants have failed to satisfy their burden of proof to establish entitlement to the
attorney-client privilege. Defendants must produce the incident report to Plaintiff.

to Foster’s termination. Defendants argue that Foster’s termination records are irrelevant and
should not be produced because discovery would be duplicative and because Plaintiff has been
provided with the reason for Foster’s termination via Foster’s deposition testimony.

After conducting an in camera review, the Court finds that the termination records contain
no new information. Plaintiff has been provided with the reason for Foster’s termination via
Foster’s deposition testimony, and the termination records confirm that reason. Any order for
Defendants to produce the termination records would therefore be duplicative and unnecessary.
Defendants are not required to produce Foster’s termination records to Plaintiff.
C) Motion to Compel & Reasonable Expenses
Plaintiff seeks reasonable expenses, including attorneys’ fees, associated with bringing the
instant motion. Defendants object on the grounds that granting fees would be too punitive.
Rule 37 provides that “a party may move for an order compelling disclosure or discovery.”
FED. R. CIV. P. 37(a)(1). If the motion is granted in part and denied in part, the court “may, after
giving reasonable opportunity to be heard, apportion the reasonable expenses for the motion.” FED.
R. CIV. P. 37(a)(5)(C).
In exercising its sound discretion under Rule 37(a)(5)(C), the Court finds that each party
should bear their own costs for bringing and defending the instant motion. The motion and
opposition were submitted with good cause and the matter resulted in a split decision. Therefore,
Plaintiff’s request for reasonable expenses and attorneys’ fees is denied.
