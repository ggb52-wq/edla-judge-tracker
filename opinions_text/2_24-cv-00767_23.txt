II. Standard of Review
Under Rule 12(b)(6), the Cour t may dismiss a complaint if it lacks jurisdiction over the
subject matter or for failur e to state a claim upon which any relief may be granted. See Fed. R.
Civ. P. 12(b)(1), (6). The same standard is applied for a motion to dismiss brought under either
Rule 12(b)(1) for lack of jurisdiction or under Ru le 12(b)(6) for failure to state a claim for which
relief can be granted. Benton v. United States, 960 F.2d 19, 21 (5th Cir. 1992).
“To survive a motion to dismiss, a complain t must contain sufficient factual matter,
accepted as true, to ‘state a claim to relief that is plausible on its face.’” Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S.
662, 678 (2009) (quoting Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly, 550 U.S. 544, 570 (2007)). The Court must
accept all well-pleaded facts as tr ue, viewing the complaint in th e light most favorable to the
plaintiff. In re Great Lakes Dredge & Dock Co., 624 F.3d 201, 210 (5th Cir. 2010); Guidry v. Am.
Pub. Life Ins. Co., 512 F.3d 177, 180 (5th Cir. 2007).
The Supreme Court, however, has declared that “[t]hreadbare recitals of the elements of a
cause of action, supported by mere conclusory statements, do not suffice.” Iqbal, 556 U.S. at 678
(internal citation omitted). Moreover, “[f]actual allegations must be enough to raise a right to relief
above the speculative level,” and “[t]he plaintiff must plead enough facts to state a claim to relief
that is plausible on its face.” Guidry v. Am. Pub. Life Ins. Co. , 512 F.3d 177, 180 (5th Cir. 2007)
(quotation marks omitted). The United States Supreme Court has explained:
A claim has facial plausibility when the plaintiff pleads factual content that allows
the court to draw the reasonable inference that the defendant is liable for the
misconduct alleged. The plausibility standard is not akin to a “probability
requirement,” but it asks for more than a sheer possibility that a defendant has acted
unlawfully. Where a complaint pleads fact s that are merely consistent with a
defendant's liability, it stops short of the line between possibility and plausibility of
entitlement to relief.
Case 2:24-cv-00767-GGG-KWR     Document 23     Filed 12/04/24     Page 6 of 35
7

Iqbal, 556 U.S. at 678 (citations and quotation marks omitted).
In determining whether a compla int states a claim that is pl ausible on its face, the Court
“draw[s] on its judicial experience and common sense.” Iqbal, 556 U.S. at 679. Thus, as mentioned
above, to avoid dismissal, “a complaint must contain sufficient factual matter, accepted as true, to
‘state a claim to relief that is plausible on its face.”’ Iqbal, 556 U.S. at 678 (quoting Twombly, 550
U.S. at 570). In order for a claim to be plausible at the pleading stage, the complaint need not strike
the reviewing court as probably meritorious, but it must raise “more than a sheer possibility” that
the defendant has violated the law as alleged. See id.
The complaint “must provide the defendant with fair notice of what the plaintiff's claim is
and the grounds upon which it rests.” Dura Pharms., Inc. v. Broudo, 544 U.S. 336, 346, 125 S.Ct.
1627, 161 L.Ed.2d 577 (2005) (quotations omitted). “[P]laintiffs must allege facts that support the
elements of the cause of action in order to make out a valid claim.” Webb v. Morella, 522 F. App'x
238, 241 (5th Cir. 2013) (quoting City of Clinton, Ark. v. Pilgrim's Pride Corp ., 632 F.3d 148,
152–53 (5th Cir. 2010) (quotation marks omitted)).  “[C]onclusory allegations or legal conclusions
masquerading as factual conclusions will not  suffice to prevent a motion to dismiss.” Webb, 522
F. App'x at 241 (quoting Fernandez-Montes v. Allied Pilots Ass'n , 987 F.2d 278, 284 (5th Cir.
1993) (quotation marks omi tted)). “Dismissal is a ppropriate when the complaint ‘on its face
show[s] a bar to relief.’ ” Cutrer v. McMillan , 308 F. App'x 819, 820 (5th  Cir. 2009) (quoting
Clark v. Amoco Prod. Co., 794 F.2d 967, 970 (5th Cir. 1986) (alteration in original)).

amendments of pleadings prior to trial. Under Ru le 15(a), if a party seeks to amend its pleading

its pleading only with the oppos ing party’s written consent or the court’s leave.” FED. R. CIV. P.

pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6), meaning that the plai ntiff has failed to state a claim upon which relief

12(b)(6), a pleading must contain “enough facts to state a claim to relief that is plausible on its
face.” Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly, 550 U.S. 544, 547 (2007). Under this jurisprudence, a claim
is facially plausible “when the plaintiff pleads f actual content that allows the court to draw the

a. Vicarious Liability
The City Defendants allege that Overton’s cl aims of vicarious liability are improper and
should be dismissed. R. Doc. 14-1 at 5. The Ci ty Defendants contend that under § 1983, local
governments cannot be held vicariously liable for their employee’s actions because they are only
responsible for “their own illegal acts.” Id. (citations omitted).

Overton does not specifically address this i ssue but generally alle ges that the proposed
Amended Complaint remedies the deficiencies cited in the Motion to Dismiss. R. Doc. 18-1 at 4.
In the proposed Amended Complaint, Overton alleges that the City of New Orleans is
individually and solidarily liable for the negligence and intentional torts of Director Williams and
Duplessis under the doctrine of respondeat superior.  R. Doc. 18-2 at ¶135.  Regarding Duplessis,
Overton contends that his actions in this matter were conducted with in the scope of his
employment with the City of New Orleans, and that the City of New Orleans is therefore
vicariously liable for J.L.’s injuries resulting from Duplessis’s conduct.  Id. at ¶132.  Regarding
Director Williams, Overton alleges she was also  acting within the course and scope of her
employment with the City of New Orleans, a nd that both Director Wi lliams and Duplessis
negligently and recklessly, directly, and proximately caused physical and emotional injury to J.L.
by their acts and omissions.  Id. at ¶¶133-134.
The City Defendants are correct that the doctrine of respondeat superior, which imposes
liability on an employer for their employee’s conduct if they were acting in the course and scope
of their employment, does not apply to § 1983 claims.  Connick v. Thompso n, 131 S.Ct. 1350,
1359, 179 L.Ed.2d 417 (2011); James v. Harris Cnty ., 577 F.3d 612, 617 (5th Cir. 2009).
Therefore, the City cannot be held liable for § 1983 violations pursuant to respondeat superior
simply because Duplessis and Director Williams were in their employ or under their supervision.
See Connick, 131 S.Ct. at 1359 (holding that municipali ties are not vicariously liability under §
1983 for their employees’ actions). See also James, 577 F.3d at 617 (“A municipality is not liable
under § 1983 on the theory of respondeat superior”).
However, Overton’s proposed Amended Comp laint seeks to hold the City Defendants
vicariously liable for the alleged negligence and intentional torts of its employees, which are
distinct from the § 1983 claims against them. See R. Doc. 18-2 at ¶135.  In Barrios-Barrios v.
Case 2:24-cv-00767-GGG-KWR     Document 23     Filed 12/04/24     Page 10 of 35

Clipps, the plaintiffs raised both § 1983 claims and claims of fa lse arrest, home invasion, sexual
battery, and malfeasance in office under Louisiana state law against the City of New Orleans,
among other defendants, after a po lice officer invaded their home under the pretense of a police
drug investigation and subsequently sexually molested and threatened them. 825 F.Supp.2d 730,
733. (E.D. La. Oct. 20, 2011) (Wilkinson, M.J.). The Court found that although the doctrine of
respondeat superior clearly did not apply to the plaintiffs’ § 1983 claims against the City of New
Orleans, it did apply to the plaintiff’s state law claims. 825 F.Supp.2d at 741-43. In Barrios-
Barrios, the plaintiffs sought to hold the City of New Orleans vicariously liable for the intentional
torts of the police officer, on the basis that they  were committed in the course and scope of his
employment with the City.  Id.
In this case, Overton alleges that the City of New Orleans is vica riously liable for the
conduct of Director Williams and Duplessis.  R. Doc. 18-2 at ¶¶131-135.  Overton further clarifies
that this liability is for: (1) the intentional torts committed by Duplessis; and (2) the negligence of
both Duplessis and Director Williams.  Id.
i. Vicarious Liability for the Intentional Torts of Duplessis
Regarding intentional torts, Overton alleges th at Duplessis’ beating of J.L. constitutes an
assault and battery under Louisiana state law, since it resulte d in both offensive, unjustified
physical contact without J.L.’s consent as well as reasonable appreh ension of the same.  R. Doc.
18-2 at ¶¶ 125-130.
Louisiana law defines battery as “harmful or offensive contact with a person, resulting from
an act intended to cause the plai ntiff to suffer such a contact.” Terrell v. Pichon, 413 F.Supp.3d
515, 522 (E.D. La. Sept. 11, 2019) (Vitter, W.) (citations omitted). By contrast, assault is defined
as “the imminent threat of a battery.” Terrell, 413 F.Supp.3d at 522 (citations omitted). Under this
Case 2:24-cv-00767-GGG-KWR     Document 23     Filed 12/04/24     Page 11 of 35
12

standard, it appears that Overton has met the plead ing requirements for he r claim of assault and
battery against Duplessis.
Regarding the City Defendants’ vicarious liab ility for this alleged assault and battery,
Louisiana law provides that “an employer is liable for the tortious acts of its employees ‘in the
exercise of the functions in which they are employed.’” Barrios-Barrios, 825 F.Supp.2d at 741
(quoting La. C.C. art. 2320). The Louisiana Supreme Court has outlined four factors for evaluating
a claim of vicarious liab ility under the doctrine of respondeat superior: (1) whether the tortious
act was primarily employment related; (2) whether the tortious act was reasonably incidental to
the performance of the employee’s duties; (3) whether the act occurr ed on the employer’s
premises; and (4) whether it occu rred during the hours of employment. Id. (citing LeBrance v.
Lewis, 292 So.2d 216, 218 (La. 1974)).
In this case, Overton alleges that Duplessis was in uniform, on the clock, and acting in his
capacity as Juvenile Detention Counselor III at JJIC at the time of the incident in controversy.  R.
Doc. 18-2 at ¶132.  Overton furthe r alleges that Duplessis’ actions, namely his violence toward
J.L., were closely connected in time, place, and causation to his employment.  Id.  Under the
standard above, it appears that Overton has therefore met the pleading requirements for a claim of
vicarious liability against the City Defendants for Duplessis’ alleged assault and battery of J.L.
Therefore, Overton’s request to amend should be granted to the extent she seeks to raise a claim
of vicarious liability against the City Defendants for Duplessis’ alleged assault and battery of J.L.,
and the City Defendants’ request to dismiss should be denied as to this claim.

A municipality or other local government may be liable under § 1983 “if the governmental
body itself subjects a person to a depr ivation of rights or causes a pe rson to be subjected to such
deprivation. Under § 1983, local governments are responsible only for their own illegal acts. They
are not vicariously liable under § 19 83 for their employees' actions.” Connick, 131 S.Ct. at 1359
(quotations and citations omitte d). Additionally, an official-cap acity suit against the Director
Williams, in her official capacity as Director of the Juvenile Justice Center, “is, in all respects other
than name, to be treated as a suit against the enti ty. It is not a suit agains t the official personally,
for the real party in interest is the entity.” Goodman v. Harris Cnty. , 571 F.3d 388, 394–95 (5th
Cir. 2009) (quotations  omitted) (citing Bd. of Cnty. Comm'rs v. Brown , 520 U.S. 397, 403, 117
S.Ct. 1382, 137 L.Ed.2d 626 (1997); Kentucky v. Graham, 473 U.S. 159, 166, 105 S.Ct. 3099, 87
L.Ed.2d 114 (1985); Monell, 436 U.S. at 690 n. 55, 98 S.Ct. 2018 (1978)).

To state a claim against the City Defendants under § 1983, Overton must plead that action
pursuant to an official municipal policy caused J.L.’s injury.  Connick, 131 S.Ct. at 1359.   “Official
municipal policy includes the decisions of a government's lawmakers, the acts of its policymaking
officials, and practices so persistent and widespread as to practically have the force of law. These
are action[s] for which the municipality is actually responsible.”  Id. (quotations and citations
omitted).  “The existence of a policy can be shown through evidence of an actual policy, regulation,
or decision that is officially adopted and promulgated by lawmakers or others with policymaking
authority.” Valle v. City of Houston , 613 F.3d 536, 542 (5th Cir. 2010), cert. denied, 131 S.Ct.
2094, 179 L.Ed.2d 890 (2011) (citation omitted).
In this case, Overton alleges that the City Defendants violated § 1983 through their de facto
policies of failing to train JJIC employees or implement the established JJIC policies. R. Doc. 18-
2 at ¶67-113.  “In limited circumstances, a local government's decision not to train certain
employees about their legal duty to  avoid violating citizens' rights may rise to the level of an
official government policy” for purposes of § 1983. Connick, 131 S.Ct. at 1359. However,
“[a] municipality's culpability for a deprivation of rights is at its most tenuous where
a claim turns on a failure to train. See Oklahoma City v. Tuttle , 471 U.S. 808, 822–823,
105 S.Ct. 2427, 85 L.Ed.2d 791 (1985) (plurality opinion) (“[A] ‘policy’ of ‘inadequate
training’ ” is “far more nebulous, and a good deal further removed from the constitutional
violation, than was the policy in Monell”). To satisfy the statute, a municipality's failure to
train its employees in a relevant respect must amount to deliberate indifference to the rights
of persons with whom the [untrained employees] come into contact. Only then can such a
shortcoming be properly thought of  as a city policy or custom  that is actionable under §
1983.”

Id. at 1359–60 (additional quotations and citations omitted).

Thus, a supervisor or municipali ty may “be liable for failure to supervise or train if: ‘(1)
the supervisor either failed to supervise or train the subordinate official; (2) a cau sal link exists
between the failure to train or supervise and the violation of the plaintiff's rights; and (3) the failure
to train or supervise amounts to deliberate in difference.’ ” Porter, 659 F.3d at 446 (quoting

Goodman, 571 F.3d at 395). However, “[w]here a plaintiff fails to establish deliberate indifference,
the court need not address the other two prongs of supervisor liability.” Goodman, 571 F.3d at 395

Monell without an underlying c onstitutional violation.  See Albert v. City of Petal , 819 F. App'x

The Fifth Circuit has held that “[p]roof of more than a single instance of the lack of training
or supervision causing a violation of constitutional rights is normally required before such lack of
training or supervision constitutes deliberate indifference.”  Livezey v. City of Malakoff , 657 F.

a single incident.  Pena, 879 F.3d at 623-24.  Federal jurisprudence is clear that the single-incident

consequence of a failure to train would result in the specific injury suffered.”  Hutcheson v. Dallas
County, Texas, 994 F.3d 477, 482-83 (5th Cir. 2021) (quoting Valle, 613 F.3d at 549).  “An injury
is ‘highly predictable’ where the municipality ‘fail[s] to train its employees concerning a clear
constitutional duty implicated in recurrent situations that a particular employee is certain to face.’
” Hankins v. Wheeler, No. 21-1129, 2022 WL 2208848, at *7 (E.D. La. Jun. 21, 2022) (Fallon, E.)
(quoting Hutcheson v. Dallas Cty., Tex., 994 F.3d 477, 482-83 (5th Cir. 2021)). Additionally, the
single-incident exception “is generally reserved for those cases in which the government actor was
provided no training whatsoever.” Pena, 879 F.3d at 623-24.

Fifth Circuit jurisprudence shows that claims un der RA and ADA are analyzed in tandem, since

that he has a qualifying disability; (2) that he is being denied the benefits of services, programs, or
activities for which the public entity is responsible , or is otherwise discriminated against by the
public entity; and (3) that such discrimination is by reason of his disability.” See Hale v. King, 642
F.3d 492, 499 (5th Cir. 2011). To establish that the plaintiff has a qualifying disability, the plaintiff
must demonstrate “(A) a physical or mental impairment that substa ntively limits one or more of

the major life activities of such individual; (B) a record of such an impairment; or (C) being
regarded as having such an impairment.” Hale, 642 F.3d at 499-500.

medical care to a detainee are not cognizable under the ADA or RA, since the ADA does not set a
standard of care for medical treatment. Wagner, No. 23-2886 at *16. Therefore, the failure to
provide detainees with compet ent medical care doe s not support a claim under the ADA or RA
unless the there is some “indication that [the plaintiff] was treated differently because of his
disability.” Id. Citing Nottingham, 499 F.App’x at 377.
In Nottingham v. Richardson, the Court found that although the plaintiff’s alleged flu and
thrush diagnosis did not qualify as disabilities, his alleged limited ability to walk may qualify as a
disability.  499 Fed. Appx. at 377.  However, the Court instructed that ‘[t]he ADA is not violated
by ‘a prison’s simply failing to attend to the medical needs of its disabled prisoners.’”  Nottingham,
499 Fed. Appx. at 377 (citations omitted).  In Nottingham, the plaintiff alleges that he was left on
the floor of a transit van and not given the opportunity to use the restroom, which violated his right
to safe transit as a disabled person during his incarceration.  Id.  The Court found that there was
no evidence that the allegedly im proper action of leaving him on the floor had any connection to
his alleged disability, nor that there was any indication that he had been treated differently due to
his disability.  Id.  Therefore, the Court found that he ha d not established a claim under the ADA
and affirmed the dismissal of this claim.  Id.
In Wagner v. Harris County , twenty-seven pretrial deta inees filed suit under § 1983, the
ADA, and RA, alleging that defendant Harris County had intentionally discriminated against them
“because of their mental and physical disabiliti es by failing to provide medications timely, by
failing to modify medical care in accordance wi th their physical condition, by failing to modify
observation and monitoring requirements, by faili ng to remove disabled  individuals from the

29

general population, and by failing to use proper techniques in handling detainees with disabilities.”
No. 23-2886 at *1. Notably, two of these plaintiffs  were diagnosed with bipolar and two were
diagnosed with schizophrenia. Id. at *1-5. The Court found that the plaintiffs’ ADA and RA claims
had to be dismissed, since they hinged upon the ja il’s failure to provide them adequate medical
care. Id. at *16. The Court clarified that although the plaintiffs’ claims “suggest the Jail acted with
alarming disregard for the detainee s’ wellbeing, they [did] not create an inference of intentional
discrimination against detainees w ith disabilities. Likewise, ther e[ was] no indication that the
detainees were denied accommodations that caused disabled detainees to have ‘an unequal ability
to use and enjoy the facility compared to individuals who do not have a disability.’” Id. at *17
(quoting Providence Behavioral Health v. Grant Rd. Pub. Utility Dist., 902 F.3d 448, 459 (5th Cir.
2018)).
However, in Hinojosa v. Livingston , the Court held that the “f ailure to make reasonable
accommodations to the needs of a disabled prisoner may have the effect of discriminating against
that prisoner because the lack of an accommodation may cause the disabled prisoner to suffer more
pain and punishment than non-disabl ed persons.” 994 F.Supp.2d at 843. In Hinojosa, the Court
found that the plaintiff had alleged sufficien t facts to show how the decedent had been
discriminated against. Id. Therein, the plaintiff alleged that the defendant had known of the risks
and dangers associated with hypertension, diabet es, schizophrenia, and depression, and their
associated prescribed medications, which make individuals more vulnerable to extreme heat
conditions. Id. at 842-843. The plaintiff alleged that despite this knowledge of the decedent’s
conditions and associated medications, the defe ndant failed to make reasonable accommodations
for his heat-sensitive disability, “resulting in  the decedent suffering more pain and punishment
than non-disabled prisoners – namely, his death.” Id.

Additionally, the Court in Hinojosa held that “[b]ecause Pl aintiff has alleged that
Defendant had knowledge of the decedent’s disability and the needs that his disability created but
took no action, it was not necessary that the decedent request any accommodation, and his failure
to do so is not fatal to Plai ntiff’s claims.” 994 F.Supp.2d at 844. The Court also held that the
plaintiff had alleged at least some facts suffici ent to identify what accommodations should have
been provided to the decedent and what facilities, programs, or services should have been modified
by the Defendant. Id. For example, the plaintiff in Hinojosa alleged that the defendant did not have
policies contemplating special housing for prisoner s with heat-sen sitive disabilities and did not
employ twenty-four (24) hour medical staff at the prison. Id.
In Adams v. Louisiana Department of Corrections , the Court held that the plaintiff had
stated a claim against the defendant, the Louisian a Department of Public Safety and Corrections,
for violations of the ADA and RA where the plain tiff alleged that the defendant had failed “to
make reasonable accommodations for the plaintiff’s mental disabilities which include a traumatic
brain injury and severe mental illness. ” No. 22-20, 2023 WL 2442386, at *13 (M.D. La. Feb. 6,
2023) (Bourgeois, M.J.). In Adams, the plaintiff alleged that he had been punished for behavior
that was a product of his mental disabilities, and that  he had been placed  in administrative
segregation where he did not rece ive sufficient mental health services. Additionally, the plaintiff
alleged that he and other inmates with mental illnesses had been routinely subject to administrative
segregation, extended lockdown, and solitary confinement. Id. at *13. In particular the Court held
that “it is the failure to treat him differently, in a way which accommodates his mental illness, that
is discriminatory. The plaintiff is alleging that a failure to treat him differently from other inmates
by not considering his mental illn ess prior to or after placing h im in segregation violates the
ADA/RA.” Id. at *14. Therefore, the Court denied the defendant’s request to dismiss the plaintiff’s
ADA and RA claim against them. Id.

D. Overton’s Claim of Direct Negligence against the City Defendants
Finally, the City Defendants seek dismissal of Overton’s state law direct negligence claim
against them. R. Doc. 14 at 1. The City Defendants  allege that Overton has failed to plead facts
showing any specific standard to which the City Defendants failed to conform, nor any factual
allegations supporting a causal link between their conduct and J.L.’s injuries. R. Doc. 14-1 at 14.
Therefore, the City Defendants allege that Overton failed to meet the pleading requirements for a
claim of direct negligence. Id.
Although Overton does not raise any specific argument in opposition on this issue, Overton
generally alleges that the proposed Amended Comp laint addresses all deficiencies alleged by the
City Defendants, including the deficiencies of the direct negligence claim.  See R. Doc. 18-1 at 4.
Upon review, the text of Overton’s direct ne gligence claim is almo st identical in the
Original Complaint and proposed Amended Complaint.  See R. Doc. 1 at 9-10. See also R. Doc.
18-2 at 29.  In fact, the only difference between the two is that the proposed Amended Complaint
removes one of the twelve originally raised grounds for direct negligence: “failing to put in place
procedures and methods to help it s employees, like Duplessis, mana ge the stress that the City of
New Orleans asked Duple ssis to undertake[.]” R. Doc. 1 at 10.  Inst ead, the proposed Amended

Complaint alleges that the City is responsible for its own neg ligence and the negligence of its

Under Louisiana law, a plaintiff must plead five elements to state a claim of direct
negligence: (1) duty; (2) breach of duty; (3) ca use-in-fact; (4) scope of liability or scope of

As described above, Overton has raised a substantial amount of proposed factual

claim for negligent training and supervision.
However, Overton also claims that the C ity Defendants “negligently hir[ed] employees
with proclivities towards violence” and “retain[ed ] Duplessis after he had shown a lack of self-

further seeks to allege that the City Defendants “fail[ed] to timely  render medical aid or call for
medical assistance[.]”  Id.
Unlike Overton’s claims of negligent trai ning and supervision, the proposed Amended
Complaint does not contain any proposed factua l allegations regarding negligent hiring and
retention of Duplessis or failure to timely render medical aid to J.L.  In fact, the only allegation
