B. Conflicts Between State Law and the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure
“A federal court entertaining state law claims cannot apply a state law or rule
if (1) the state law or rule ‘ directly collides’ with a Federal Rule of Civil Procedure
and (2)  the Federal Rule ‘ represents a valid exercise of Congress ’s rulemaking
authority.’” Passmore v. Baylor Health Care Sys. , 823 F.3d 292, 296 (5th Cir. 2016)
(alterations omitted) (quoting Burlington N. R.R. Co. v. Woods,  480 U.S. 1, 4– 5
(1987)); see also Berk v. Choy, 607 U.S. 187, 192 (2026).
“A state law directly collides with a Federal Rule if it provides a different
answer to the question in dispute.” Passmore, 823 F.3d at 296. This occurs when the
state law “imposes additional procedural requirements not found in the [F]ederal
[R]ules” or “ each specifies requirements for a case to proceed at the same stage of
litigation.” Klocke v. Watson, 936 F.3d 240, 245 (5th Cir. 2019).
A Federal Rule of Civil Procedure is a valid exercise of Congress’s rulemaking
authority if it “really regulates procedure,” or, in other words, “[i]f it governs only the
manner and the means by which the litigants’ rights are enforced.” Berk, 607 U.S. at
198 (internal quotation marks omitted) (quoting Sibbach v. Wilson & Co., 312 U.S. 1,
14 (1941); then Shady Grove Orthopedic Assocs., P.A. v. Allstate Ins. Co. , 559 U.S.
393, 407 (2010) (plurality opinion )). So far, t he Supreme Court has  “rejected every
statutory challenge to a Federal Rule that has come before”  it. Id. at 199 (quoting
Shady Grove, 559 U.S. at 407 (plurality opinion)).

III. LEGAL ANALYSIS
The stay provision of § 1892.2 does not apply to proceedings in federal court
because it directly collides with at least two valid Federal Rules of Civil Procedure:
Federal Rule of Civil Procedure  81(c)(2) (“Rule 8 1(c)(2)”), which determines  when
defendants must respond to a removed action , and Federal Rule of Civil Procedure
16(b) (“Rule 16(b) ”), which requires courts to issue a scheduling order .23 These
collisions occur because §  1892.2 and Rules 81(c)(2) and 16(b) provide different
answers to two questions in dispute: (1) when Garrison must answer or present other
defenses or objections to the Ziifles’ complaint; and (2) when the Court must issue a
scheduling order limit ing the time to join other parties, amend the pleadings,
complete discovery, and file motions.
Because this action was removed from state court, the first question in dispute
is answered by Rule 81(c)(2), which provides that:
A defendant who did not answer before removal must answer or present
other defenses or objections under these rules within the longest of these
periods:
(A) 21 days after receiving—through service or otherwise—a copy
of the initial pleading stating the claim for relief;
(B) 21 days after being served with the summons for an initial
pleading on file at the time of service; or

 The second question in dispute is governed by Rule 16(b) , which requires the

§ 1892.2 directly collides with Rule 16(b).

Both parties frame the question s at issue  too narrowly. It is true that the
Federal Rules of Civil Procedure do not expressly forbid the stay required by § 1892.2.
“But the test of whether a conflict between the Federal Rules and a state statute
exists is . . . whether the Federal Rules in question are sufficiently broad to control
the issue before the court.” Klocke, 936 F.3d at 247 (internal quotation marks omitted)
(quoting Carbone v. Cable News Network, Inc., 910 F.3d 1345, 1355 (11th Cir. 2018)).
Here, the scope of Rules 81(c)(2) and 16(b) are clearly broad enough to preclude the
stay required by §  1892.2 because a stay would prevent the Court and the parties
from complying with those Federal Rules of Civ il Procedure . “[E]ach specifies
requirements for a case to proceed at the same stage of litigation”  and these
requirements are inconsistent with each other.  Id. at 245. Section 1892.2 requires a
60-day cure period to elapse before the defendant is required to answer  or a
scheduling order must be issued . Rules 81(c)(2) and 16(b)  do not. Section 1892.2 is
therefore displaced by the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure.
The only remaining question is whether Rules 81(c)(2) and 16(b)  are “valid
exercise[s] of Congress’s rulemaking authority.” Id. at 247–48 (quoting All Plaintiffs
v. All Defendants, 645 F.3d 329, 333 (5th Cir. 2011)).  They undoubtedly are. Rules
81(c)(2) and 16(b) govern the timeline by which litigation must proceed. They regulate
only “the judicial process for enforcing rights and duties recognized by substantive

remedies, or the rules of decision.’” Berk, 607 U.S. at 199 (2026) (quoting Shady Grove,

In support of a stay, Garrison points to Baber v. Edman,28 a case in which the
Fifth Circuit ordered a district court to enter a similar sixty -day stay required by
state law. 719 F.2d 122, 123 (5th Cir. 1983) . A Texas statute required a medical
malpractice plaintiff  to provide defendants with a  written notice of the plaintiff’s
intent to sue  at least sixty days before filing a lawsuit . Id. at 122 n.1. Just before
Baber, the Texas Supreme Court had held that, if a medical malpractice plaintiff
failed to provide notice before suing, the case should be stayed for sixty days.  Id. at
123. The Fifth Circuit stated that “[t]he notice statute is so intertwined with Texas’s
substantive policy on medical malpractice actions that, to give that policy full effect,
federal courts sitting in diversity must enforce its requirements. ” Id. In light of the
Texas Supreme Court’s decision, t he Fifth Circuit remanded the case with
instructions to stay the proceedings for sixty days. Id.
However, Baber does not control this case. It concerned a different state law,
and its reasoning is inconsistent with the Supreme Court’s intervening decisions in
Shady Grove and Berk, which the Fifth Circuit recognize as governing conflicts
