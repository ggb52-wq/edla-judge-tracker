II. LEGAL STANDARD
A. Rule 12(b)(6) Motion to Dismiss for Failure to State a Claim
Under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6), a defendant can seek dismissal
of a complaint, or any part of it, for failure to state a claim upon which relief may be
granted. F ED. R. CIV. P. 12(b)(6). To survive a Rule 12( b)(6) motion to dismiss, “a
complaint must contain sufficient factual ma tter, accepted as true, to ‘state a claim
to relief that is plausible on its face.’” Ashcroft v. Iqbal , 556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009)
(quoting Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly , 550 U.S. 544, 570 (2007)). “A claim has facial
plausibility when the plaintiff  pleads factual content that allows the court to draw
the reasonable inference that the defendant is liable for the misconduct alleged.” Id.
(citing Twombly, 550 U.S. at 556).

A motion under Rule 12(b)(6) tests the sufficiency of the pleadings, not the
merits of the case. George v. SI Group, Inc. , 36 F.4th 611, 619 (5th Cir. 2022).
Therefore, the Court accepts “all well-pleaded facts as true, viewing them in the light
most favorable to the plaintiff.” In re Katrina Canal Breaches Litig. , 495 F.3d 191,
205 (5th Cir. 2007) (quoting Martin K. Eby Constr. Co. v. Dallas Area Rapid Transit,
369 F.3d 464, 467 (5th Cir. 2004)). Still, th is principle has limits. “[T]he factual
allegations must ‘raise a right to relief above the speculative level.’” Carlucci v.
Chapa, 884 F.3d 534, 538 (5th Cir. 2018) (quoting Twombly, 550 U.S. at 555).
“[C]onclusory allegations, unwarranted fact ual inferences, or legal conclusions are
not accepted as true.” Hodge v. Engleman, 90 F.4th 840, 843 (5th Cir. 2024) (quoting
Allen v. Hays, 65 F.4th 736, 743 (5th Cir. 2023)).
B. The Court May Not Consider Material Beyond the Pleading
In resolving a Rule 12(b)(6) motion, the Court “must limit itself to the contents
of the pleadings, including attachments thereto.” Collins v. Morgan Stanley Dean
Witter, 224 F.3d 496, 498 (5th Cir. 2000). The Court may rely only on material beyond
the pleadings, without converting the motion into one for summary judgment,25 under
limited exceptions. George, 36 F.4th at 619. Under the doctrine of incorporation by
reference, the Court may consider a document attached to the Rule 12(b)(6) motion if
it is (1) referred to in the complaint an d (2) central to the plaintiff’s claim. F ED. R.

The Court now turns to the scope of materials that may be considered in
resolving Defendant’s motion. In its motion to dismiss, FEMA attaches a December

This letter is neither referenced in the complaint nor central to White’s claim. To the

III. ANALYSIS
FEMA’s renewed motion to dismiss argues that the complaint fails to state a
claim because the SFIP’s appraisal clause applies only to disputes over the dollar
amount of a covered loss, not to disputes over the scope of the loss. White responds
that the “scope” language in her complaint describes only a quantum dispute over the
cost, quantity, and method of repairing pr operty that FEMA concedes was damaged
by the flood, and that such a dispute is appraisable. Because the allegations are taken
as true and viewed in the light most fa vorable to White, the complaint plausibly
describes a dispute over the amount of loss rather than a dispute over coverage, and
the Court finds FEMA’s arguments unpersuasive.

White’s policy contains an appraisal clause, which provides that “if the insured
and FEMA fail to agree on the actual cash value or replacement cost of the damaged
property to settle upon the amount of loss, then either party may demand an
Appraisal of the loss.”28 The terms of a SFIP policy are dictated by FEMA, and those
terms can be found in the Code of Federal Regulations at 44 C.F.R. Pt. 61, App. A(1).
Sharpe v. American Nat’l Prop. & Cas. Co., No. 13-161, 2014 WL 2520210, at *1 (E.D.
La. June 4, 2014).
A dispute over the cost of doing repair work, as opposed to a dispute over
coverage, is a dispute over the “amount of loss” that triggers the appraisal provision
under the SFIP. Dwyer v. Fid. Nat’l Prop. & Cas. Ins. Co., 565 F.3d 284, 286 (5th Cir.
2009) (explaining that, to the extent an insured challenges only the adjuster’s
estimated repair costs, not what needs to be repaired, the dispute falls within the
SFIP’s appraisal clause). By contrast, a dispute over what items were actually
damaged, or whether those items were in fact damaged by flood waters, falls outside
the scope of the appraisal clause. De La Cruz v. Bankers Ins. Co. , 237 F. Supp. 2d
1370, 1373–74 (S.D. Fla. 2002). De La Cruz  divided appraisal disputes into three
categories: (1) disputes over the actual ca sh value of property the parties agree was
damaged, (2) disputes over how agreed-upon damage should be repaired or replaced,
and (3) disputes over what property was damaged or the manner in which it was
damaged. Id. at 1375. The first two categories concern the amount of loss and are

FEMA contends that the appraisal clau se is inapplicable because the parties
here dispute the “scope” of damages ( i.e., what items were damaged by the flood),
rather than the “amount” of the loss. FEMA points to the complaint’s own language
alleging “a clear disagreement regarding the scope of damage and costs” and that the
parties “disagree as to the scope and uni t cost of the property damage loss.” 29
Defendant relies principally on De La Cruz, which held that the appraisal clause of
the SFIP is unavailable to resolve disputes over what items were damaged by flooding
(a “scope” or “coverage” dispute), as distinguished from disputes over the value of
concededly damaged items (an “amount of loss” dispute amenable to appraisal). 237
F. Supp. 2d at 1374–75.

The Court’s task at this stage is circumscribed. On a Rule 12(b)(6) motion, the
Court accepts all well-pleaded facts as true  and draws all reasonable inferences in
the plaintiff’s favor. See Iqbal, 556 U.S. at 679. The Court does not resolve factual
disputes and is generally limited to the “four corners” of the complaint. Id. at 678–69;
Wolcott v. Sebelius , 635 F.3d 757, 763 (5th Cir. 2011) (citing Dorsey v. Portfolio
Equities, Inc. , 540 F.3d 333, 338 (5th Cir. 2008) (“Generally, a court ruling on a
12(b)(6) motion may rely on the complaint , its proper attach ments, ‘documents
incorporated into the complaint by reference, and matters of which a court may take
judicial notice.’”).
Applying that standard and considerin g the facts set forth in the complaint,
White states a plausible claim for enforcement  of the SFIP’s appraisal clause. First,
White plausibly alleges that FEMA accepted coverage and causation. The complaint
alleges—and FEMA does not dispute—that FEMA accepted White’s claim and paid
her more than $150,000 under both Covera ge A and Coverage B. White further
alleges that her dispute with FEMA conc erns the cost and method of repairing
concededly flood-damaged proper ty, the same type of dispute Dwyer held to be
appraisable. 565 F.3d at 286–87. Under the framework in De La Cruz , which both
parties invoke, the allegations concern a di spute in the second category, which is

appraisable. At the 12(b)(6) stage, wher e all reasonable inferences are drawn in
White’s favor, the complaint plausibly alleges a dispute over the “amount of loss” that
triggers the appraisal clause.
Second, the complaint’s use of the word “scope” does not defeat the plausibility
of White’s claim. The complaint alleges di sagreements over both “scope of damage
and costs” and “scope and unit cost.”32 Read in context and in the light most favorable
to White, the word “scope” as used in the complaint can be read to refer to the extent
and quantity of repair, not to whether particular items of damage are covered under
the SFIP. Whether the parties’ disagreement constitutes a non-appraisable “scope”
dispute or an appraisable “amount of loss” dispute is a factual question that cannot
be resolved on the pleadings. Accordingly,  Defendant’s motion to dismiss for failure
to state a claim is denied.
