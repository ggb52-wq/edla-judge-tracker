ORDER & REASONS
Before the Court is a Motion to Review Magistrate Judge’s Decision filed by Plaintiff Richard
Blackman (“Blackman”). R. Doc. 66. American Honda Finance Corporation (“AHFC”) and Honda Lease
Trust (“HLT”) (collectively, “Honda Defendants”) oppose the Motion. R. Doc. 70. Blackman and Honda
Defendants filed supplemental memoran da. R. Docs. 75, 76.  After considering the record, briefing, and
applicable law, the Court rules as follows:
I. BACKGROUND
Blackman filed this action asserting state law claims for fraud, absolute  and relative nullity of
contract, unfair trade practices, intentional/negligent infliction of emotional distress, and a violation of the
Fair Credit Reporting Act. R. Doc. 1-2 at  4–9. Blackman represents that, on July 18, 2024, he signed an
automobile lease with Defendant Mamco Harvey, LLC (“Mamco”) as a co-lessor with his daughter. Id. at
2. Blackman claims that, after he left the dealership thinking the deal was complete, an agent of Mamco
forged his signature on a different lease than the one he originally signed, which now lists him as the primary
lessee, and  on an  application  for  automobile insurance.  Id.  at  2. Blackman  alleges  that  Mamco  later
transferred the forged lease to its affiliates, Honda Defendants. Id. Blackman avers that Honda Defendants
failed to reasonably investigate the alleged fraud despite receiving notice from Blackman that his signature
was forged. Id. at 3, R. Doc. 66 at 3.  Honda Defendants instead relied on the forged documents to force
Blackman to make payments under the terms of the lease, and furnished information that he defaulted on
payments under the disputed lease to Equifax, TransUnion, and Experian, thereby harming Blackman’s

III. LEGAL STANDARD
A magistrate judge may issue orders regarding non -dispositive pretrial matters. Fed. R. Civ. P.
72(a). Under Rule 72(a), a magistrate judge’s ruling on a non-dispositive matter is subject to reversal only
where the decision is “clearly erroneous or contrary to law.” A ruling is “clearly erroneous” only when “the
reviewing court on the entire evidence is left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been
committed.” Anderson v. Bessemer City, N.C. , 470 U.S. 564, 565 (1985) . As such, the party seeking
reversal “bears a heavy burden.” Seacor Marine, L.L.C. v. Cummins Mid -S., Inc., No. 09-383, 2010 WL
2292152, at *2 (E.D. La. June 3, 2010).
IV. DISCUSSION
 Subpoenas issued for discovery purposes are subject to the discovery limitations detailed in Fed.
R. Civ. P. 26(b). Rule 26(b)(1) permits discovery “ regarding any nonprivileged matter that is relevant to
any party's claim or defense and proportional to the needs of the case . . . .” Relevant information, however,
may not be discoverable where “the discovery sought is unreasonably cumulative or duplicative .” Fed. R.

Civ. P. 26(b)(2)(C).
 Rule 26(c) allows the court, “for good cause, [to] issue an order” restricting discovery “to protect
a party or person from annoyance, embarrassment, oppression, or undue burden or expense.” In determining
whether to grant a protective order, a court is tasked with balancing “the hardship to the person from whom
discovery is sought against the information’s probative value to the requesting party. ” Cazorla v. Koch
Foods of Miss., L.L.C ., 838 F.3d 540, 555 (5th Cir. 2016) . The party seeking a protective order has the
burden “to show the necessity of its issuance, which contemplates a particular and specific demonstration
of fact as distinguished from stereotyped and conclusory statements.” In re Terra Int'l, Inc., 134 F.3d 302,
306 (5th Cir. 1998).
 The magistrate judge determined that the Master Service Agreements were not discoverable under
Rule 26. Reviewing the record, briefing, and applicable law, the Court is not left with a firm and definite
conviction that the magistrate judge erred.
 First, Honda Defendants, as the movants , presented evidence indicating that the Master Service
Agreements constituted confidential business information. Patterson’s affidavit explained that the software
systems provided by Dealertrack and FIS were specifically designed for Honda Defendants to serve their
specific needs, and the Master Service Agreements contained confidential information discussing these
“customized configurations.” R. Doc. 52 -1 at 2-3. Patterson continued that disclosure of the Agreements
would “risk significant and irreparable damage to [Honda Defendants].” Id. at 3.
 Second, although Honda Defendants’ ability to access the system data is relevant to Blackman’s
reasonable investigation claim, Honda Defendants had already stated repeatedly in discovery responses and
in briefing that they do not have the contractual right to access such data on demand. R. Docs. 45 at 5, 57
at 6 , 33-6 at 3 –4, 8, 33 -1 at 5, 33-7 at 9 –10. The Master Service Agreement provisions sought were
essentially duplicative of information already obtained.
 Finally, FIS’s statement that the subpoenaed information could be obtained from Honda Defendants
did not specify whether it was referring to the raw data, Master Service Agreements , or other materials

requested in the subpoena. R. Doc. 51-6 at 2. It was not a clear statement that Honda Defendants had access
to the raw or native data. The magistrate judge therefore did not clearly err in refusing “to endorse a fishing
expedition to probe the veracity at AHFC’ s [representations]” on the basis of this vague, inconclusive
statement.
The record presented to the magistrate  judge indicated that the probative value of the Master
Service Agreements was negligible because Blackman had already learned through discovery that Honda
Defendants did not have access to the raw data. The risk of harm, on the other hand, was genuine given
Honda Defendants’ confidentiality concerns. In balancing these interests, the magistrate judge elected not
to implement a middle ground solution, such as an in-camera review, and instead to prevent the disclosure
of the Master Service Agreements in any form. Given the balance of interest s clearly weighed in favor of
protecting Honda Defendants’ confidentiality concerns, the Court cannot conclude that the magistrate judge
clearly erred in her decision.
V. CONCLUSION
Considering the foregoing;
IT IS ORDERED that Plaintiff’s Motion for Review of Magistrate Judge’s Decision, R. Doc.
66, is hereby DENIED.
