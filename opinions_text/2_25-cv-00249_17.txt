II.  LEGAL STANDARD
A. Rule 12(b)(6) Motion to Dismiss for Failure to State a Claim
Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 8(a)(2) requires “a short and plain statement
of the claim showing that the pleader is entitled to relief.” F ED . R.  CIV . P.  8(a)(2).
A complaint that does not meet Rule 8(a)(2)’s pleading standard should be dismissed
for failing to state a claim upon which relief can be granted. FED . R.  CIV . P.  12(b)(6).
“[T]he pleading standard . . . does not require ‘detailed factual allegations,’ but it
demands more than an unadorned, the -defendant-unlawfully-harmed-me
accusation.” Ashcroft v. Iqbal , 556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009) (quoting Bell Atl. Corp. v.
Twombly, 550 U.S. 544, 555 (2007)). “A pleading that offers ‘labels and conclusions’
or ‘a formulaic recitation of the elements of a cause of action will not do.’” Id. (quoting
Twombly, 550 U.S. at 555). “Nor does a complaint suffice if it tenders ‘naked
assertion[s]’ devoid of ‘further factual enhancement.’” Id. (quoting Twombly, 550 U.S.
at 557).
To survive a motion to dismiss, “a complaint must contain sufficient factual
matter, accepted as true, to ‘state a claim to relief that is plausible on its face.’” I d.
(quoting Twombly, 550 U.S. at 570). “A claim has facial plausibility when the plaintiff
pleads factual content that allows the court to draw the reasonable inference that the
defendant is liable for the misconduct alleged.” Id. (citing Twombly, 550 U.S. at 556).

A motion under Rule 12(b)(6) “ tests the sufficiency of the pleadings, not the
merits of the case. ” George v. SI Grp. , Inc., 36 F.4th 611, 619 (5th Cir. 2022).  The
Court’s task is “to determine whether the plaintiff has stated a legally cognizable
claim that is plausible, not to evaluate the plaintiff's likelihood of success.” George v.
Hobby Lobby Stores, Inc., 769 F. Supp. 3d 537, 544 (E.D. La. 2025) (quoting Body by
Cook, Inc. v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins., 869 F.3d 381, 385 (5th Cir. 2017)). Therefore,
the Court accepts “ all well- pleaded facts as true, viewing them in the light most
favorable to the plaintiff.” In re Katrina Canal Breaches Litig., 495 F.3d 191, 205 (5th
Cir. 2007) (quoting Martin K. Eby Constr. Co. v. Dall. Area Rapid Transit, 369 F.3d
464, 467 (5th Cir. 2004)). “ [C]onclusory allegations, unwarranted factual inferences,
or legal conclusions are not accepted as true.” Hodge v. Engleman, 90 F.4th 840, 843
(5th Cir. 2024) (quoting Allen v. Hays, 65 F.4th 736, 743 (5th Cir. 2023)).
B. The Court May Not Consider Material Beyond the Pleading

Before turning to any count, the Court addresses the scope of the materials it
may consider in resolving this motion. In resolving a Rule 12(b)(6) motion, the Court
“must limit itself to the contents of the pleadings, including attachments thereto.”
Collins v. Morgan Stanley Dean Witter, 224 F.3d 496, 498 (5th Cir. 2000). The Court
may only rely on material beyond the pleading s without converting the motion into
one for summary judgment under limited exceptions. George, 36 F.4th at 619. Under
the doctrine of incorporation by reference, the Court may consider a document
attached to a  Rule 12(b)(6) motion if it is (1) referred to in the complaint ; and (2)
central to the plaintiff’s claim. FED. R. CIV. P. 10(c); Collins, 224 F.3d at 498–99. Going

A. Failure to State a Claim under Title II of the Americans with
Disabilities Act

The School Board first  moves to dismiss A.F.’s claim of disability
discrimination under the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA). A.F. presents
several theories of liability under the ADA, including failure to accommodate,
retaliation, conscious discrimination, and intentional discrimination. 43 The School
Board moves to dismiss A.F.’s  ADA claims on two grounds. First, the School Board
asserts that it never identified A.F. as a student with a disability. 44 Second, the
amended complaint fails to allege sufficient facts indicating the School Board’s
actions amounted to intentional discrimination .45 Neither of these arguments is
persuasive.
Title II of the ADA provides that individuals with disabilities shall not “ be
excluded from participation in or be denied the benefits of the services, programs, or

In its motion to dismiss, the School Board claims it never “identified,

arising from the impairment. Windham v. Harris Cnty., 875 F.3d 229, 236 (5th Cir.
2017).

student with a disability, but whether it recognized her as a student with a limitation.
A.F. alleges it did. A.F.’s mother alerted the School Board to A.F.’s diagnoses as early

C. Failure to State a Claim for Negligence
The School Board next moves to dismiss A.F.’s claim of negligence because the
amended complaint fails to allege facts sufficient to support a breach of duty. 55 The
Court does not agree. A.F.’s  allegations that the School Board violated its statutory
duties under the ADA and LHRA present  a plausible breach of duty to support her
negligence claim.
Louisiana courts determine liability for negligence by applying a duty/risk

analysis. Pellerin v. Foster Farms, L.L.C., 354 So. 3d 790, 795 (La. Ct. App. 2023). To
prevail, a plaintiff must prove:  (1) the defendant had a duty to conform his conduct
to a specific standard; (2) the defendant’s conduct failed to conform to the appropriate
standard; (3) the defendant ’s substandard conduct was a cause -in-fact of the
plaintiff’s injuries; (4) the defendant’s substandard conduct was a legal cause of the
plaintiff’s injuries; and, (5) proof of actual damages. Farrell v. Circle K Stores, Inc. ,
359 So. 3d 467, 473 (La. 2023).
Louisiana law does not recognize the doctrine of negligence per se. Pellerin, 354
So. 3d at 795–97. However, “statutory duties” like those set out in the ADA and LHRA
can “establish the duty of care in a negligence analysis.” Lane v. Baywood Hotels, Inc.,
No. 25-cv-103, 2025 WL 1026452, at *4 (E.D. La. Apr. 7, 2025); see Merrell v. 1st Lake
Props., Inc. , 717 F. Supp. 3d 512, 517 (E.D. La. 2024) (“The Fifth Circuit has
recognized that Louisiana law may look to federal statutory and regulatory schemes
in delineating the scope of a duty . . . .”).
A.F. has alleged that the School Board had a duty to comply with the ADA and
LHRA and failed to do so. For purposes of the instant motion, this statutory violation
is sufficient to allege breach of duty under a duty/risk analysis. A.F. also alleges that
this failure caused her to sustain injuries, including emotional distress and
hospitalization due to severe suicidal thoughts and self -harm. Accepting these facts
as true and viewing them in the light most favorable to the Plaintiff, A.F. has
sufficiently alleged that the School Board violated the ADA and LHRA and thereby
negligently breached its statutorily imposed duty to her.

D. Failure to State a Claim for Intentional Infliction of Emotional
Distress

Finally, the School Board moves to dismiss A.F.’s claim of intentional infliction
of emotional distress (IIED) because (1) the pleadings fail to link the conduct of the
School Board to A.F.’s emotional distress; and (2) the alleged conduct does not rise to
the requisite level of “extreme and outrageous.” 56 But considering the Plaintiff’s age
and susceptibility to emotional distress, the Court is persuaded that A.F.’s allegations
of repeated conduct by the School Board presents at least a plausible claim of IIED.
To state a claim for IIED under Louisiana law, a plaintiff must allege that: (1)
the conduct “was extreme and outrageous”; (2) the conduct caused “severe” emotional

56 Id. at 9.

distress; and (3) “the defendant desired to inflict severe emotional distress or knew
that severe emotional distress would be certain or substantially certain to result from
his conduct.” White v. Monsanto Co., 585 So. 2d 1205, 1209 (La. 1991).
The Louisiana Supreme Court recognizes  that “outrageous  conduct is a
nebulous concept, as it does not refer to any specific type of  conduct.” Bustamento v.
Tucker, 607 So.  2d 532, 538, n.6 (La. 1992). As a guiding principle, e xtreme and
outrageous conduct is that which exceeds “all possible  bounds of decency” and is
“regarded as atrocious and utterly intolerable in a civilized community.” White , 585
So. 2d at 1209. Conduct can become extreme and outrageous when  it amounts to a
pattern of  behavior or  an abuse of  a position of power.  Id. at 1209–
10 (citing Restatement (Second) of Torts § 46).  “Even relatively ‘mild’ harassment
may become tortious if continued over a substantial time period.” Bustamento , 607
So. 2d at 538.
Louisiana courts also apply “the vulnerable victim factor” to the IIED analysis
and consider the “defendant’s knowledge that plaintiff is particularly susceptible to
emotional distress.” Sullivan v. Malta Park, 156 So. 3d 751, 759 (La. Ct. App. 2014)
(quoting White, 585 So.  2d at 1210).  Therefore, sick people, children, and pregnant
women have recovered for conduct that “would not otherwise have been sufficient to
constitute [IIED].” Id. at 760 (quoting Dan B. Dobbs, Robert E. Keeton & David G.
Owen, Prosser and Keeton on the Law of Torts § 12 (5th ed. 1984)).
