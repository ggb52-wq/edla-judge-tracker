To survive a Rule 12(b)(6) motion to dismiss, a plaintiff must plead
enough facts “to state a claim for relief that is plausible on its face.”6 A claim is
“plausible on its face ” when the pleaded facts allow the court to “ draw the
reasonable inference that the defendant is liable for the misconduct alleged.”7
A court must accept the complaint’s factual allegations as true and must “draw
all reasonable inferences in the plaintiff’s favor.”8 The court need not, however,
accept as true legal conclusions couched as factual allegations. 9 To be legally
sufficient, a complaint must establish more than a “sheer possibility” that the
plaintiff’s claims are true.10 If it is apparent from the face of the complaint that
an insurmountable bar to relief exists and the plaintiff is not entitled to relief,
the court must dismiss the claim. 11 The court’s review is limited to the
complaint and any documents attached to the motion to dismiss that are
central to the claim and referenced by the complaint.12

Under Louisiana’s rules for statutory interpretation, “[w] hen a law is
clear and unambiguous and its application does not lead to absurd
consequences, the law shall be applied as written and no further interpretation
may be made in search of the intent of the legislature.”33 The Court finds that,
as a predicate to valid lapse, § 1911 unambiguously requires notice of pending
lapse be given to the insured and a third person before an insurer can validly
lapse a policy for nonpayment of premiums , unless the insured has
affirmatively waived their right to designat e a third party in writing . The
regulation prescribes a mandatory two-part notice process that an insurer
“shall … comply with” in order to prevent “unintentional lapse ” of long-term
care insurance policies  for nonpayment of premiums .34 Unless waived  in
writing, § 1911.A.1.a gives insureds “the right to designate at least one person
… to receive the notice of termination [for nonpayment of premium], in
addition to the insured.” Further, § 1911.A.1.d states that “[n]o individual long-
term care policy … shall lapse or be terminated f or nonpayment of premium
unless the insurer … has given notice to the insured and  to those persons
designated pursuant to § 1911.A.1.a.” The phrase “unless the insurer … has
given notice to the insured and those persons designated pursuant to §
1911.A.1.a” establishes that a policy cannot lapse under § 1911.A.1.d where
the insurer fail ed to comply with § 1911.A.1.a.  Interpreting the designation
requirement permissively would not only go against the plain words of the
regulation, but it would render both § 1911.A.1.a and § 1911.A.1.d ineffective.
