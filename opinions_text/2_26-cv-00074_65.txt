ORDER AND REASONS
 Plaintiff in this action is a judgment creditor alleging that the judgment debtor along with
its principals and affiliated business entities engaged in a scheme to hide and transfer assets to
avoid paying the judgment. Before the Court is plaintiff’s Motion to Compel Discovery Responses
(Rec. Doc. 54). The Court held oral argument on September 16, 2026. A number of the issues have
been mooted. As to the remaining issues, as discussed in more detail below, the Court finds that
both transactions with the judgment debtor and transactions between the non -debtor defendants
are relevant and proportional with t he needs of the case. Defendants must supplement their
discovery responses accordingly. However, as the plaintiff conceded at oral argument, transactions
with third parties are not relevant, at least at this stage. The Motion to Compel (Rec. Doc. 54) is
GRANTED in part and DENIED in part as follows.

Law and Analysis
1. Scope of Discovery
The Federal Rules of Civil Procedure provide that “parties may obtain discovery regarding
any nonprivileged matter that is relevant to any party's claim or defense and proportional to the
needs of the case.” Fed. R. Civ. Proc. 26(b)(1). “Information within this scope of discovery need
not be admissible in evidence to be discoverable.” Id.
“[I]information is relevant if it ‘bears on, or . . . reasonably could lead to other matters that
could bear on, any issue related to the claim or defense of any party.’” Leonard v. Martin, 38 F.4th
481, 489 (5th Cir. 2022)  (quoting Coughlin v. Lee, 946 F.2d 1152, 1159 (5th Cir. 1991) ). “The
threshold for relevance at the discovery stage is lower than the threshold for relevance of
Case 2:26-cv-00074-ASJ-JVM     Document 65     Filed 10/01/26     Page 5 of 13
6

admissibility of evidence at the trial stage.” Tate v. DG Louisiana LLC, 653 F. Supp. 3d 316, 319–
20 (E.D. La. 2023).
Relevant information falls within the scope of discovery only if it is proportional to the
needs of the case. Rule 26  requires consideration of the following factors in assessing
proportionality: “the importance of the issues at stake in the action, the amount in controversy, the
parties’ relative access to relevant information, the parties’ resources, the importance of the
discovery in resolving the issues, and whether the burden or expense of the proposed discovery
outweighs its likely benefit.” Fed. R. Civ. Proc. 26(b)(1).
2. Revocatory Actions

This lawsuit includes a revocatory action against Carl Causey, Toni Causey, and C4,
seeking to undo transfers of funds by Tri-C to each of them after Direct Steel’s rights against Tri-
C arose. “An obligee has a right to annul an act of the obligor, or the result of a failure to act of the
obligor, made or effected after the right of the obligee arose, that causes or increases the obligor's
insolvency.” La. Civ. Code Ann. 2036 . To establish a revocatory claim, Direct Steel must show
each “offending transaction: (1) was made or effected after the right of the obligee arose and (2)
caused or increased the obligor's insolvency.” Traina v. Whitney Nat. Bank, 109 F.3d 244, 246 (5th
Cir. 1997). No fraud is required.
3. Alter Ego and Single Business Enterprise Claims
This lawsuit also includes claims that each Mr. and Ms. Causey are alter egos of Tri-C and
that each C4 and TMC are a single business enterprise with Tri-C. “[T]he legal fiction of a distinct
corporate entity may be disregarded when a corporation is so organized and controlled as to make
it merely an instrumentality or adjunct of another corporation. ” Green v. Champion Ins. Co., 577
So. 2d 249, 257 (La. Ct. App.), writ denied, 580 So. 2d 668 (La. 1991). Thus, Louisiana courts use
Case 2:26-cv-00074-ASJ-JVM     Document 65     Filed 10/01/26     Page 6 of 13
7

the “single business enterprise” theory to extend liability to a group of separate corporations. Id.
Courts use the following, non-exclusive, list of factors to find that a group of entities constitute a
“single business enterprise:”
1. corporations with identity or substantial identity of ownership, that is, ownership
of sufficient stock to give actual working control;
2. common directors or officers;
3. unified administrative control of corporations whose business functions are
similar or supplementary;
4. directors and officers of one corporation act independently in the interest of that
corporation;
5. corporation financing another corporation;
6. inadequate capitalization (“thin incorporation”);
7. corporation causing the incorporation of another affiliated corporation;
8. corporation paying the salaries and other expenses or losses of another
corporation;
9. receiving no business other than that given to it by its affiliated corporations;
10. corporation using the property of another corporation as its own;
11. noncompliance with corporate formalities;
12. common employees;
13. services rendered by the employees of one corporation on behalf of another
corporation;
14. common offices;
15. centralized accounting;
16. undocumented transfers of funds between corporations;
17. unclear allocation of profits and losses between corporations; and
18. excessive fragmentation of a single enterprise into separate corporations.

Id. at 257-58.

 While the “single business enterprise” theory applies to corporate affiliates, the alter -ego
theory is used to impose liability on an owner or shareholder for the debts of the company. See In
re Royal Alice Props., LLC, 619 B.R. 839, 861 (Bankr. E.D. La. 2020); Riggins v. Dixie Shoring
Co., 590 So. 2d 1164, 1168 (La. 1991). Courts consider the following non-exclusive list of factors
when determining whether to apply the alter ego doctrine: “ 1) commingling of corporate and
shareholder funds; 2) failure to follow statutory formalities for incorporating and transacting
corporate affairs; 3) undercapitalization; 4) failure to provide separate bank accounts and
Case 2:26-cv-00074-ASJ-JVM     Document 65     Filed 10/01/26     Page 7 of 13
8

bookkeeping records; and 5) failure to hold regular shareholder and director meetings. ” Riggins,
590 So. 2d at 1168. Courts are “reluctant to hold a shareholder, officer, or director of a corporation
personally liable for corporate obligations, in the absence of fraud, malfeasance, or criminal
wrongdoing.” Id.  “[T]he totality of the circumstances is determinative.” Id.  at 1169.

each of the non -debtor defendants must be revoked. The transactions and relationships between
the non-debtor defendants are not directly relevant.

Nonetheless, the Court finds information about these transactions and relationships could
reasonably lead to information that is relevant to the issues in this case. Direct Steel alleges that
Toni Causey and Carl Causey own each  Tri-C, C4, and TMC. They allege and have evidence

As to the burden of production, as noted above, defendants have not shown that production
of the requested information would be overly burdensome. The Court finds the requested
production is proportionate with the needs of the case. In allowing broader discovery, the Court is
also influenced by the District Court’s decision to deny C4 and TMC’s motion to dismiss the
single-business-enterprise claims for failure to state a claim. Furthermore, this is not a case where
plaintiff has alleged a contract or tort claim against one defendant  and then further alleged alter
ego and single business enterprise as to its owners and affiliates. In such a case,  it might serve
judicial economy to delay  discovery into alter ego and single business enterprise  pending a
judgment against the lead defendant. In contrast, here, Direct Steel has already obtained a judgment
against Tri-C and is in the enforcement phase.
