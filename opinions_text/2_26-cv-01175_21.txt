ORDER & REASONS
 Before the Court is Defendant’s Motion to Dismiss Pursuant to Federal Rule of
Civil Procedure 12(b)(6) (Rec. Doc. 10)  filed by Defendants Delaware North
Companies, Inc., Areas USA, LLC, and Areas MSY (collectively “Defendants”).
Plaintiff Jarlyn Johnson opposes the motion. (Rec. Doc. 12). Defendants have filed
their reply. (Rec. Doc. 13). After considering the motion, the memorandum, the
record, and the applicable law, the Court finds that the motion should be GRANTED.
FACTS AND PROCEDURAL HISTORY
 The following factual allegations derive  from Plaintiff Johnson’s pro se
Complaint. This employment discrimination dispute arises from Johnson ’s
termination with Defendant Delaware North Companies where she worked as a
multi-unit manager  at the Louis Armstrong New Orleans International Airport
(“MSY”).
On June 13, 2025, Delaware North terminated Johnson’s employment after a
female internal candidate complained that Johnson did not select the internal
candidate for a promotion . Johnson provided a written explanation of her hiring
decision to a Delaware North Human Resources (HR) coordinator. Citing the internal

LEGAL STANDARD
To survive a Rule 12(b)(6) motion to dismiss, the plaintiff must plead sufficient
facts to “‘state a claim to relief that is plausible on its face.’” Ashcroft v. Iqbal , 556
U.S. 662, 678 (2009) (quoting Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly , 550 U.S. 544, 570 (2007)).

A claim is facially plausible when the plaintiff pleads facts that allow the court to
“draw the reasonable inference that the defendant is liable for the misconduct
alleged.” Id. The factual allegations in the Complaint “must be enough to raise a right
to relief above the speculative level.”  Twombly, 550 U.S. at 555. “[D]etailed factual
allegations” are not required, but the pleading must present “more than an
unadorned, the-defendant-unlawfully-harmed-me accusation.” Iqbal, 556 U.S. at 678.
The court must accept all well -pleaded facts as true and must draw all reasonable
inferences in favor of the plaintiff. Lormand v. U.S. Unwired, Inc., 565 F.3d 228, 232
(5th Cir. 2009).  However, “‘conclusory allegations or legal conclusions masquerading
as factual conclusions will not suffice to prevent a motion to dismiss.’” Beavers v.
Metro. Life Ins. Co., 566 F.3d 436, 439 (5th Cir. 2009) (citation omitted).
When considering a Rule 12(b)(6) motion to dismiss, a court must restrict its
analysis “to the contents of the pleadings, including attachments thereto.” Collins v.
Morgan Stanley Dean Witter, 224 F.3d 496, 498 (5th Cir. 2000) (citing Fed. R. Civ. P.
12(b)(6)). Typically, if the movant presents “matters outside the pleadings” and the
court chooses not to exclude them, the court must treat the motion as one for
summary judgment under Rule 56. F ed. R. Civ. P. 12(b)(6).  If a document is only
referenced rather than attached to the plaintiff's complaint, then the court may not
incorporate the referenced document into the complaint. See Scanlan v. Tex. A & M
Univ., 343 F.3d 533, 536-37 (5th Cir. 2003).

DISCUSSION
 Construing Johnson’s sparse Complaint liberally,  Andrade v. Gonzalez , 459
F.3d 538, 543 (5th Cir. 2006), this Court will address Johnson’s claims of (1) Title VII
sex discrimination, (2) Title VII retaliation1, (3) sex discrimination under LEDL, and
(4) retaliation under LEDL.
A. Title VII Sex Discrimination
To prevail under a Title VII sex/gender discrimination claim, a plaintiff must
establish that she is (1) a member of a protected group, (2) was qualified for the
position at issue, (3) suffered an adverse employment action, and (4) was treated less
favorably than other similarly situated employees outside the protected group. Haire
v. Bd. of Supervisors of La. State. Univ. Agric. and Mech. Coll., 719 F.3d 356, 363 (5th
Cir. 2013). Title VII provides that a sex discrimination claim is established when the
plaintiff shows sex was a motivating factor with regards to “fail[ing] or refus[ing] to
hire or to discharge any individual,” or with respect to the plaintiff’s “compensation,
terms, conditions, or privileges of employment.” 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-2(a)(1), (m).
In her Opposition to Defendants’ Motion to Dismiss, Johnson alleges that
Defendants engaged in sex discrimination when they terminated  her due to a “ sex-
related dispute arising from her managerial hiring decision between two internal
candidates.” (Rec. Doc. 12, at 4). Defendants argue Johnson has not plausibly pled

B. Title VII Retaliation
A plaintiff establishes prima facie retaliation by showing that: (1) the plaintiff
engaged in a protected activity under Title VII; (2) the plaintiff endured an adverse
employment action; and (3) a causal connection existed between the protected activity
and the adverse employment action. Ackel v. Nat’l Commc’ns, Inc., 339 F.3d 376, 386
(5th Cir. 2003).
Johnson asserts that her termination was retaliatory because it was tied to a
protected activity: the “sex-related dispute” arising out of the complaint made by the
female candidate. (Rec. Doc. 12, at 4).  She also claims Areas HR’s refusal to rehire
was directly connected to her “prior protected activity” and her termination. (Rec.
Doc. 1, at 9). Overall, she  contends that the close temporal proximity between the
female candidate’s “sex-related” complaint, her termination, and Defendants’ refusal
to rehire her support a plausible inference of retaliation  under Title VII. (Rec. Doc.
12, at 4-5).
Defendants argue Johnson’s retaliation claim fails  because she did not plead
engaging in any protected activity within the meaning of Title VII. (Rec. Doc. 10-1, at
9-10); (Rec. Doc. 13, at 2 -3). Defendants argue Johnson neither plausibly alleges
partaking in a protected activity nor Defendants’ awareness of her protected activity.
(Rec. Doc. 13, at 3).

This Court finds that Johnson has not sufficiently pled that she engaged in a
Title VII  protected activity before her termination.  Title VII protected activities
include an employee’s opposition to unlawful employment practices or participation
in a workplace investigation. 42 U.S.C. § 2000e -3(a); Green v. Admin. of the Tulane
Educ. Fund, 284 F.3d 642, 657 (5th Cir. 2002). Notably, the United States Court of
Appeals for the Fifth Circuit previously established that an employee’s participation
in an internal employer investigation unaffiliated  with a formal EEOC proceeding
does not qualify as a protected activity under Title VII’s anti -retaliation provision.
E.E.O.C. v. Rite Way Serv., Inc., 819 F.3d 235, 239 n.2 (5th Cir. 2016).
Based on the sparse  facts alleged in her Complaint, Johnson’s  written
explanation of her hiring decision and the female candidate’s “sex-related” complaint
do not qualify as a Title VII protected activit ies. Johnson alleges the written
explanation of her hiring decision discussed only her unit’s operational needs and the
gender-neutral hiring procedures she followed—not any opposition to unlawful
employment practices .2 Johnson never alleges  the female candidate’s complaint
pertain to unlawful employment practices directed against Johnson herself .
Moreover, Johnson’s Complaint does not mention that she participated in an EEOC-
related investigation  or expressed  concerns to her employer about any unlawful

Effectively, because Johnson has not plausibly pled sufficient facts that she
exercised her rights in a Title VII protected activity, her Title VII retaliatory refusal
to rehire theory also fails.  Although a refusal to hire or rehire may constitute a
materially adverse action in an appropriate case, a plaintiff must still establish a
prima facie case of retaliation by pleading facts showing that the adverse action was
causally connected to protecte d activity. See Harmon v. Collier , 158 F.4th 595, 617
(5th Cir. 2025) (holding that a refusal to rehire retaliation claim requires
demonstrating the defendant’s retaliatory animus was the but-for causation of the
failure to rehire). “[A]n employer cannot be guilty of retaliation for an employee’s
opposition to discrimination unless he is aware of that opposition.” Corley v. Jackson
Police Dept. , 639 F.2d 1296, 1300 (5th Cir.  1981); accord Casarez v. Burlington
N./Santa Fe Co., 193 F.3d 334, 339 (5th Cir. 1999) (concluding no Title VII retaliation
claim where a terminated employee failed to show his former employer ’s knowledge
of his EEOC charge filed before he applied for another position).
Here, Johnson’s Complaint does not plead sufficient facts to state a plausible
claim of Title VII retaliation under a  refusal to rehire  theory. Johnson does not
provide dates or details as to how and when her general manager “attempted to
rehire.” (Rec. Doc. 1, at 9). Nor does Johnson state she had filed an EEOC charge
against Defendants before they decided not to rehire her . Rather, Johnson only
alleges having filed a single EEOC charge approximately six months after her June

2025 termination date, and that “HR blocked [her] return” after Areas HR consulted
with Delaware North HR. (Rec. Doc. 12, at 5).
Further, w hile Johnson provides sufficient facts leading to a reasonable
inference that Defendants were aware of her termination, this awareness does not
translate to Defendants’ awareness of her protected activity. Like in Casarez v.
Burlington N./Santa Fe Co. , where the terminated employee failed to demonstrate
his employer’s knowledge of his EEOC charge filed before he had applied for a job ,
Johnson also does not allege that Delaware North HR or Areas HR were aware of any
EEOC charge filed before their consideration to rehire her , that she opposed
Defendants’ unlawful employment practices, or that she exercised in any other
protected conduct. 193 F.3d at 339. In fact, Johnson only offers that  “[d]uring the
EEOC interview, the investigator focused on the conduct of corporate management
and the HR coordinator involved in both the termination and the refusal to rehire .”
Id. Because t he EEOC interview took place after Defendants refused to rehire
Johnson, this Court finds these facts insufficient to support a reasonable inference
that the refusal to rehire was “ directly connected to [Johnson]’s prior protected
activity and the discriminatory termination.” Id.
Therefore, this Court finds that Johnson has not pled sufficient facts of her
engagement in a Title VII protected activity before her termination and refusal to
rehire.

C. LEDL Claims of Sex Discrimination and Retaliation
The Louisiana Employment Discrimination Law  (LEDL) prohibits an
employer from discriminating against an individual based on race, color, religion, sex,
age, or national origin. La. Rev. Stat. Ann . § 23:332(A)(1). Because the scope of the
LEDL is the same as Title VII, claims under the LEDL are analyzed under the Title
VII framework and jurisprudential precedent. See DeCorte v. Jordan, 497 F.3d 433,
437 (5th Cir. 2007) (“Claims of racial discrimination in employment, pursuant to . . .
the [LEDL],  are governed by the same analysis as that employed for such claims
under Title VII. ”); Alderman v. Great Atlantic & Pacific Tea Co., Inc.,  332 F. Supp.
2d 932, 936 (E.D.  La. 2004) (noting Louisiana courts consistently look to federal
employment discrimination jurisprudence when interpreting LEDL claims).
Because Johnson fails to allege facts sufficient to support her Title VII sex
discrimination and retaliation claims, her parallel sex discrimination and retaliation
claims under LEDL necessarily fail to state a claim upon which relief can be granted.
As such, this Court finds all of Johnson’s LEDL claims were not plausibly pled.
