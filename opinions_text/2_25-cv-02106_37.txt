ORDER AND REASONS
 Before the Court is a Motion to Dismiss filed by Defendant Wexford Health Sources, Inc.
(“Wexford”). R. Doc. 31. Although the Court granted pro se Plaintiff Laura Amanda Collins’s
motion for an extension of time to file a response to Wexford’s m otion, R. Docs. 32, 33, Collins
missed the extended deadline and now moves the Court for  another extension. R. Doc. 34.
Additionally, Collins requests leave to file an amended complaint, R. Doc. 36, the appointment of

II. DI
SCUSSION
In its motion to dismiss, Wexford argues first that Collins’ claims against it should be
dismissed for failure to serve it with the complaint within 90 days of filing as required by Federal
Rule of Civil Procedure 4(m). R. Doc. 31. Given that Collins is a pro se, incarcerated plaintiff, she
is reliant on the United States Marshals Service to effectuate timely service. The Marshals were
directed by the Court to effect service upon Wexford and summons was issued on January 22,
2026. As of the date of this order, no return of service upon Wexford has been filed into the record,
and Wexford asserts it was never served. However, that failure cannot be attributed to Collins, and
dismissal of Collins’s claims on this basis would not be in the interest of justice. That being the
case, the Court will exercise its discretion under Rule 4(m) to extend the time within which service
must be effected upon Wexford and will renew its order that the United States Marshal effect such
service.

Secondly, Wexford argues that Collins fails to state a viable claim against it because
Colllins merely states that she was “denied medical[,]” without providing any factual allegations
or even attributing that denial of medical care to Wexford specifically. R. Doc. 7 at 4; see also R.
Doc. 31-1 at 4 –6. Wexford is correct. However, Collins seeks leave to amend her complaint. R.
Doc. 36. It is axiomatic that “[i]n the absence of any apparent or declared reason —such as undue
delay, bad faith or dilatory motive . . ., repeated failure to cure deficiencies by amendments
previously allowed, undue prejudice to the opposing party . . ., futility of amendment, etc.—the
leave sought should . . . be ‘freely given.’”  Foman v. Davis , 371 U.S. 178, 182 (1962) ( quoting
Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a)(2)). Due to the “liberal pleading presumption” underlying Rule 15, the Rule
“evinces a bias in favor of granting leave to amend,” such that “the term ‘discretion’ in this context
‘may be misleading[.]’” Mayeaux v. Louisiana Health Serv. & Indem. Co., 376 F.3d 420, 425 (5th
Cir. 2004) (quoting Stripling v. Jordan Prod. Co., LLC, 234 F.3d 863, 872 (5th Cir. 2000)). While
courts may deny leave to amend if amendment would be futile, see Stripling, 234 F.3d at 872–73,
the Court cannot hold on the basis of the record before it that permitting Collins to  amend her
complaint to plead more specific and individualized allegations against Wexford and, if desired,
the other named defendants, would be futile. Accordingly, the Court will deny Wexford’s motion
to dismiss and grant Collins leave to amend her complaint. In order to facilitate Collins to amend
her complaint, the Court will also grant Collins’ request for copies of the documents she submitted
to the Court as attachments to her complaint.
 As to Collins’s motion for the appointment of counsel, R. Doc. 35, a civil rights plaintiff
has no constitutional or statutory right to appointed counsel. Branch v. Cole , 686 F.2d 264, 266
(5th Cir. 1981). A court may, however, appoint counsel in exceptional circumstances. Id.  In
deciding whether exceptional circumstances exist, courts within the Fifth Circuit apply the four

factors identified in Ulmer v. Chancellor: (1) the type and complexity of the case; (2) the plaintiff's
ability to present his case adequately; (3) his ability to investigate the facts; and (4) whether the
case will likely involve conflicting testimony that requires skilled presentation of evidence and
cross-examination. 691 F.2d 209, 213 (5th Cir. 1982).  Courts may also consider whether
appointing counsel would promote the efficient and equitable resolution of the case. Jackson v.
Dallas Police Dep’t, 811 F.2d 260, 262 (5th Cir. 1986). Because every pro se litigant would benefit
from counsel, the plaintiff bears the burden of showing that her circumstances differ from those of
the typical pro se litigant. James v. City of Deer Park, No. 4:18- CV-646, 2018 WL 11353746, at
*2 (S.D. Tex. June 7, 2018).
Applying the Ulmer  factors here, the Court concludes that this case does not present
exceptional circumstances. Collins’s claims involve issues that courts routinely address in prisoner
civil rights litigation and do not present unusual legal or factual complexity. See Lazard v. Gusman,
No. 14-2860, 2015 WL 5882984, at *2 (E.D. La. Oct. 8, 2015). Plaintiff has also demonstrated
that she can articulate her claims and file appropriate pleadings, and nothing in the record suggests
that she cannot investigate the relevant facts. Although the Court recognizes the challenges Collins
faces as an incarcerated pro se litigant, those challenges do not materially differ from those that
other pro se  litigants routinely encounter. Collins therefore has not shown the exceptional
circumstances necessary to justify appointing counsel.
III. CONCLUSION
